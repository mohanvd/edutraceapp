# Cell Membrane – Structure, Transport & Diabetes

<Callout type="info" emoji="🌊">
Imagine standing at the edge of the ocean. The salty water is teeming with bacteria, toxins, and dissolved minerals – yet a jellyfish drifts through it all, its delicate cells perfectly intact. How? Every single one of its cells is wrapped in an **ultrathin, intelligent barrier** – the **plasma membrane** – that decides, molecule by molecule, what gets in and what stays out. This lesson is about that barrier: how it's built, how it moves materials, and what happens when it fails – as in **diabetes**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧩">
**Essential Question:** How does structure determine function?

**Big Idea:** Cells are the basic unit of structure and function in organisms. The cell membrane's unique molecular architecture enables it to control the flow of materials, maintain homeostasis, and communicate with the environment. Diseases like diabetes have a cellular basis rooted in membrane transport.
</Callout>

---

## Why the Cell Membrane Matters

All cells – prokaryotic and eukaryotic – must interact with their environment while maintaining a stable internal state called **homeostasis** (الاتزان الداخلي). The cell membrane is the gatekeeper that makes this possible.

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Barrier Function (وظيفة الحاجز)">
Separates the cell's internal environment (**intracellular fluid, ICF**) from the external environment (**extracellular fluid, ECF**). Without this barrier, the cell's carefully balanced chemistry would dissolve into chaos.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Selective Permeability (النفاذية الاختيارية)">
The membrane is **semi-permeable** – it does NOT let everything through. It selects which molecules enter and exit based on size, charge, and polarity. This is the membrane's superpower.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Communication (التواصل)">
Receives chemical signals (hormones, neurotransmitters) from the environment and relays them inside the cell, triggering responses.
</Step>
<Step title="4. Waste Removal (التخلص من الفضلات)">
Allows the cell to excrete metabolic waste products to prevent toxic build-up.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Structure of the Plasma Membrane

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/Cell_membrane_detailed_diagram_en.svg/600px-Cell_membrane_detailed_diagram_en.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of the cell membrane showing the phospholipid bilayer, integral and peripheral proteins, cholesterol, and the glycocalyx" caption="The Plasma Membrane consists of a phospholipid bilayer embedded with proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates." width="500px" />

The plasma membrane is made of **four main components**: phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and glycolipids/glycoproteins.

### The Phospholipid Bilayer – The Foundation

<Definition term="Phospholipid Bilayer (طبقة الفوسفوليبيد الثنائية)">
Two layers of **phospholipid molecules** arranged tail-to-tail, forming the basic structural framework of every cell membrane. "Bi" means two → bilayer = two layers. This is the scaffold upon which everything else sits.
</Definition>

Each phospholipid molecule has two distinct parts:

<Compare>
<Item label="Hydrophilic Head (الرأس المحب للماء)">
- Contains a **phosphate group**
- **Polar** – attracted to water
- Faces **outward** toward the watery environment (ICF on one side, ECF on the other)
- "Hydro" = water, "philic" = loving
</Item>
<Item label="Hydrophobic Tails (الذيول الكارهة للماء)">
- Made of **two fatty acid chains**
- **Nonpolar** – repelled by water
- Face **inward**, away from water, toward each other
- "Hydro" = water, "phobic" = fearing
- Create a **hydrophobic core** that blocks water-soluble molecules
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🥪">
**Sandwich analogy**: Think of the bilayer as a sandwich. The bread slices = hydrophilic heads (facing the watery world). The filling = hydrophobic tails (hidden inside, away from water). This arrangement is **spontaneous** – phospholipids naturally organise this way because of the laws of chemistry.
</Callout>

### Cholesterol – The Fluidity Buffer

<Definition term="Cholesterol (الكوليسترول)">
A **sterol lipid** molecule that inserts itself between phospholipid tails in the membrane of **animal cells** (eukaryotic). Acts as a **fluidity buffer** – stabilising membrane consistency across different temperatures.
</Definition>

<Tabs items={["🥶 In Cold Temperatures", "🔥 In Hot Temperatures"]}>
<Tab label="🥶 In Cold Temperatures">

When it's cold, phospholipid tails pack tightly together and the membrane risks **freezing** (becoming too rigid).

Cholesterol **prevents freezing** by wedging between the tails and **disrupting their tight packing** → keeps the membrane fluid even in the cold.

**Effect:** ↑ Fluidity (prevents solidification)

</Tab>
<Tab label="🔥 In Hot Temperatures">

When it's hot, phospholipids move apart too much and the membrane risks becoming **too fluid** (like melting butter).

Cholesterol **restricts movement** by holding neighbouring phospholipids in place → prevents the membrane from becoming too loose.

**Effect:** ↓ Fluidity (prevents excessive movement)

</Tab>
</Tabs>

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**Exam favourite**: "What is the role of cholesterol in the membrane?" → It acts as a **fluidity buffer** that **stabilises** the membrane. At low temperatures it increases fluidity (prevents freezing); at high temperatures it decreases fluidity (prevents melting). It keeps the membrane in its optimal "Goldilocks zone."
</Callout>

### Membrane Proteins – The Workers

Proteins embedded in or attached to the membrane perform most of the membrane's active functions. They come in two main categories:

<Compare>
<Item label="Integral Proteins (البروتينات المدمجة)">
- **Embedded** within the phospholipid bilayer
- Have **hydrophobic regions** that anchor them inside the membrane's hydrophobic core
- Some span only **one side**; others go **all the way through** (transmembrane proteins)
- **Amphipathic** – have both hydrophobic (inside membrane) and hydrophilic (exposed) regions
- Cannot be removed without disrupting the membrane
</Item>
<Item label="Peripheral Proteins (البروتينات الطرفية)">
- **Not embedded** in the bilayer
- Sit on the **surface** of the membrane (inner or outer side)
- Attached by weak bonds to integral proteins or phospholipid heads
- Can be **easily removed** without disrupting membrane structure
- Often involved in signalling and cytoskeletal attachment
</Item>
</Compare>

<Definition term="Transmembrane Protein (بروتين عابر للغشاء)">
A type of integral protein that spans the **entire** phospholipid bilayer, exposed on **both** the intracellular and extracellular sides. Many function as **channels**, **carriers**, or **receptors**. They typically cross the membrane as an **alpha-helix** structure.
</Definition>

### Five Functions of Membrane Proteins

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Transport (النقل)">
**Channel proteins** and **carrier proteins** move molecules across the membrane that cannot pass through the hydrophobic core on their own. This is the most important function for this LO.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Signal Transduction (نقل الإشارات)">
**Receptor proteins** (often peripheral) bind to signalling molecules like hormones and neurotransmitters, triggering a response inside the cell.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Enzymatic Activity (النشاط الإنزيمي)">
Some membrane proteins function as **enzymes**, catalysing chemical reactions at the membrane surface.
</Step>
<Step title="4. Intercellular Joining (الربط بين الخلايا)">
Proteins form junctions between adjacent cells – **tight junctions**, **desmosomes**, and **gap junctions** – connecting cells into tissues.
</Step>
<Step title="5. Cell-to-Cell Recognition (التعرف بين الخلايا)">
**Glycoproteins** on the cell surface act as identity tags, allowing the immune system to distinguish "self" from "foreign."
</Step>
</Steps>

---

### The Glycocalyx – The Cell's ID Card

<Definition term="Glycocalyx (الغلاف السكري)">
A fuzzy, sugar-rich coat on the **outer surface** of the cell membrane, made of **glycoproteins** (protein + carbohydrate) and **glycolipids** (lipid + carbohydrate). Functions as the cell's identity badge and protective layer.
</Definition>

| Function | How It Works |
| --- | --- |
| **Cell recognition** | Acts as an "ID tag" – the immune system uses it to distinguish **self** cells from **foreign** invaders |
| **Blood type determination** | The A, B, AB, and O blood types are determined by the specific carbohydrates on RBC glycoproteins |
| **Protection against dehydration** | The sugar coat traps water molecules, reducing water loss from the cell surface |
| **Immune defence** | Provides **antigenic identity** – helps immune cells recognise "this is mine ✅" vs "this is foreign ❌" |
| **Cell adhesion** | Helps cells stick to each other and to the extracellular matrix |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Virus entry trick**: Some viruses (like HIV) exploit the glycocalyx by **mimicking** or **binding to** specific glycoproteins on the cell surface. They essentially use the cell's own "ID system" as a door handle to break in. This is why the glycocalyx is medically significant.
</Callout>

## The Fluid Mosaic Model

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/10/Fluid_Mosaic.svg" alt="Diagram illustrating the fluid mosaic model with proteins floating in the lipid bilayer" caption="The Fluid Mosaic Model showing how proteins and lipids can move laterally within the membrane." width="450px" />

<Definition term="Fluid Mosaic Model (نموذج الفسيفساء المائع)">
The accepted scientific model describing membrane structure, proposed by **Singer and Nicolson (1972)**. "**Fluid**" because lipids and proteins can move laterally within the membrane (like boats on a lake). "**Mosaic**" because the membrane is a patchwork of different molecules – phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates – creating a pattern like a mosaic artwork.
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🎨">
The **fluid mosaic model** was confirmed by experiments that fused mouse and human cells. Within one hour, the membrane proteins from both species had completely **intermixed** – proving the membrane is fluid, not rigid. If it were solid, the proteins would stay in place. If it were gas, they would mix instantly.
</Callout>

### Movement Within the Membrane

Phospholipids are not stationary – they move constantly:

<Compare>
<Item label="Lateral Diffusion (الانتشار الجانبي)">
- Movement **side to side** within the **same layer**
- Happens **~10⁷ times per second** (10 million times!)
- Extremely common and fast
- No enzymes needed
- Like shuffling sideways in a crowded row of seats
</Item>
<Item label="Transverse Diffusion / Flip-Flop (الانتشار العرضي)">
- Movement from **one layer to the other** (outer ↔ inner)
- Happens only **once per month** – extremely rare
- Requires **enzyme assistance**:
  - **Flippase**: moves lipids outer → inner
  - **Floppase**: moves lipids inner → outer
- Rare because the hydrophilic head must pass through the hydrophobic core
</Item>
</Compare>

### Factors Affecting Membrane Fluidity

Three factors determine how fluid (flexible) or rigid (stiff) the membrane is:

<Tabs items={["🌡️ Temperature", "🧈 Cholesterol", "⛓️ Fatty Acid Type"]}>
<Tab label="🌡️ Temperature">

### Temperature

| Condition | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **High temperature** | Phospholipids move apart → **↑ fluidity** (more movement, less viscosity) |
| **Low temperature** | Phospholipids pack tightly → **↓ fluidity** (less movement, more viscosity) |

Think of it like butter: warm butter is soft and spreadable (high fluidity); cold butter is hard and rigid (low fluidity).

</Tab>
<Tab label="🧈 Cholesterol">

### Cholesterol (The Buffer)

| Condition | Cholesterol's Action | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High temperature** | Restricts phospholipid movement | **↓ fluidity** (stabilises) |
| **Low temperature** | Prevents tight packing | **↑ fluidity** (prevents freezing) |

Cholesterol is the **thermostat** of the membrane – always working to keep fluidity in an optimal range.

</Tab>
<Tab label="⛓️ Fatty Acid Type">

### Saturated vs Unsaturated Fatty Acids

| Type | Structure | Packing | Effect on Fluidity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Saturated** | Straight tails, no double bonds | Pack **tightly** together | **↓ fluidity** (more rigid, more viscous) |
| **Unsaturated** | Kinked tails (double bonds create a "bend") | **Cannot** pack tightly | **↑ fluidity** (more flexible, less viscous) |

**Winter wheat** survives extreme cold by **increasing the percentage of unsaturated phospholipids** in its membranes – the kinks prevent freezing.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**"Like dissolves like"**: The hydrophobic interior of the membrane allows **small, nonpolar, lipid-soluble** molecules (O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones) to pass through freely. **Large, polar, or charged** molecules (glucose, ions, water in bulk) **cannot** cross without protein assistance.
</Callout>

## Transport Across the Membrane

The membrane's selective permeability means different molecules cross in different ways. Transport is classified into two main categories:

| Feature | Passive Transport | Active Transport |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Energy (ATP)** | ❌ Not required | ✅ Required |
| **Direction** | **Down** the concentration gradient (high → low) | **Against** the concentration gradient (low → high) |
| **Analogy** | Rolling a ball downhill – gravity does the work | Pushing a ball uphill – you must supply energy |
| **Types** | Simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis | Primary active, secondary active, bulk transport |

---

### Passive Transport – No Energy Needed

<Definition term="Passive Transport (النقل السلبي)">
Movement of molecules across the membrane **without** energy (ATP). Molecules move **down** their concentration gradient – from an area of **high** concentration to an area of **low** concentration. This is spontaneous, like water flowing downhill.
</Definition>

<Tabs items={["1️⃣ Simple Diffusion", "2️⃣ Facilitated Diffusion", "3️⃣ Osmosis"]}>
<Tab label="1️⃣ Simple Diffusion">

### Simple Diffusion (الانتشار البسيط)

The simplest form of transport – molecules pass **directly through** the phospholipid bilayer without any protein help.

**What can cross?**
- **Lipid-soluble (nonpolar)** molecules: O₂, CO₂, fatty acids, glycerol, steroid hormones
- **Very small polar** molecules: water (in tiny amounts)

**Key characteristics:**
- No carrier or channel protein needed
- No energy (ATP) needed
- Moves **high → low** concentration
- No structural specificity – no special shape required
- No saturation point – rate increases linearly with concentration
- **Less sensitive** to temperature changes

<Callout type="info" emoji="🫧">
**Daily life example**: When you spray perfume in one corner of a room, you can smell it across the room within minutes. The perfume molecules diffuse from high concentration (where you sprayed) to low concentration (the rest of the room) – this is simple diffusion through air!
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="2️⃣ Facilitated Diffusion">

### Facilitated Diffusion (الانتشار الميسر)

For molecules that are **too large**, **too polar**, or **charged** (ions) to pass through the hydrophobic bilayer alone. They need **protein assistance** but still move down the gradient (no ATP).

**Two types of transport proteins:**

**Channel Proteins (بروتينات القنوات):**
- Form a **water-filled pore** through the membrane
- Allow **small water-soluble molecules** and **ions** to pass through
- Three subtypes:
  - **Leak channels**: always open (e.g., K⁺ leak channels)
  - **Ligand-gated channels**: open when a specific molecule binds (e.g., neurotransmitter receptors)
  - **Voltage-gated channels**: open in response to changes in electrical charge (e.g., in nerve cells)

**Carrier Proteins (بروتينات الحامل):**
- Bind a **specific** molecule → change shape → release it on the other side
- Show **structural specificity** – each carrier fits only certain molecules
- Slower than channels (shape change takes time)
- Can become **saturated** – there's a maximum transport rate
- Example: glucose transporters

**Key characteristics:**
- Requires protein help (channel or carrier)
- Still **no ATP** needed – still passive
- Moves **high → low** concentration
- Has **specificity** – particular proteins for particular molecules
- **More sensitive** to temperature (proteins can denature)
- Carrier proteins show **saturation** (maximum rate when all proteins are occupied)

**Examples:** Glucose, amino acids, ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻), water (via aquaporins)

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Aquaporins** (أكوابورين) are **channel proteins** specifically for water. Even though water is small enough to pass slowly through the bilayer by simple diffusion, aquaporins allow **rapid, bulk water movement**. Aquaporin-mediated water transport is classified as **facilitated diffusion** (passive, protein-assisted, down gradient).
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="3️⃣ Osmosis">

### Osmosis (التناضح / الأسموزية)

<Definition term="Osmosis (التناضح)">
The movement of **water molecules** from a region of **high water concentration** (low solute) to a region of **low water concentration** (high solute) across a **semi-permeable membrane**. It is essentially the diffusion of water.
</Definition>

Osmosis is driven by differences in **tonicity** – the relative solute concentration on either side of the membrane:

| Solution Type | Solute Concentration | Water Movement | Effect on Animal Cell | Effect on Plant Cell |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hypertonic** (محلول مفرط التوتر) | Higher solute **outside** the cell | Water moves **OUT** of the cell | Cell **shrinks** (crenation) | Cytoplasm pulls away from wall (**plasmolysis**) |
| **Hypotonic** (محلول منخفض التوتر) | Lower solute **outside** the cell | Water moves **INTO** the cell | Cell **swells** and may **burst** (lysis/cytolysis) | Cell becomes **turgid** (firm) – wall prevents bursting |
| **Isotonic** (محلول متساوي التوتر) | Equal solute on both sides | **No net** water movement | Cell stays **normal** | Cell stays **flaccid** (limp) |

<Callout type="info" emoji="🥒">
**Kitchen experiment**: When you put cucumber slices in salt water (hypertonic), they become limp and shrivelled – water left the cells via osmosis. Put them in fresh water (hypotonic), and they swell back up as water rushes in. You've just witnessed osmosis!
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Turgor pressure** is what keeps plants upright and rigid. Plant cells in hypotonic solutions (normal condition in soil) absorb water → the central vacuole swells → pushes the cytoplasm against the **cell wall** → creates turgor pressure. This is why plants wilt when they lose water – without turgor pressure, they collapse. Animal cells **lack a cell wall**, so they have no protection against swelling and can **burst** (lyse).
</Callout>

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Active Transport – Energy Required

<Definition term="Active Transport (النقل النشط)">
Movement of molecules across the membrane **against** their concentration gradient – from **low** concentration to **high** concentration. Requires **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) as an energy source. Like pushing a boulder uphill.
</Definition>

**How ATP provides energy:**
ATP has **3 phosphate groups**. When one phosphate is removed (hydrolysis), energy is released. The ATP becomes **ADP** (adenosine **di**phosphate). This released energy powers the carrier protein to change shape and pump molecules against the gradient.

<Tabs items={["⚡ Primary Active Transport", "🔄 Secondary Active Transport"]}>
<Tab label="⚡ Primary Active Transport">

### Primary Active Transport

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a5/Scheme_sodium-potassium_pump-en.svg" alt="Diagram showing the 6 steps of the Sodium-Potassium pump" caption="The Sodium-Potassium (Na⁺/K⁺) pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in against their concentration gradients." width="500px" />

Uses ATP **directly** – the carrier protein itself is an ATPase (an enzyme that breaks down ATP).

**The Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na⁺/K⁺ Pump) – The Star Example:**

This is the most important example of primary active transport in your body:

<Steps>
<Step title="Step 1: Bind Na⁺">
3 sodium ions (Na⁺) from **inside** the cell bind to the pump protein.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 2: ATP → ADP + P">
ATP donates a phosphate group to the pump → releases energy → pump **changes shape**.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 3: Release Na⁺ outside">
The shape change opens the pump to the **outside** → 3 Na⁺ are released into the ECF.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 4: Bind K⁺">
2 potassium ions (K⁺) from **outside** bind to the pump.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 5: Phosphate released">
The phosphate group detaches → pump returns to **original shape**.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 6: Release K⁺ inside">
2 K⁺ are released **inside** the cell. Cycle repeats.
</Step>
</Steps>

**Result:** For every cycle → **3 Na⁺ pumped OUT**, **2 K⁺ pumped IN**.

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**Memory trick – "بُرص" (BORS)**: **بُ**رَّة (outside) = **ص**وديوم (sodium). Sodium is more concentrated **outside** the cell (بره). Potassium is more concentrated **inside** (جوه). The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains this imbalance, which is critical for nerve impulses and muscle contraction.
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="🔄 Secondary Active Transport">

### Secondary Active Transport (Co-transport)

Does **NOT** use ATP directly. Instead, it **harnesses the energy** stored in an ion gradient that was created by **primary** active transport.

**How it works:**
1. Primary active transport (e.g., proton pump) uses ATP to pump H⁺ ions **out** of the cell
2. This creates a **concentration gradient** of H⁺ (more outside, less inside)
3. H⁺ ions naturally want to flow **back in** (down their gradient)
4. A **co-transporter protein** lets H⁺ back in, but **only if it brings another molecule** (e.g., glucose) with it
5. The glucose gets a "free ride" into the cell, powered by the H⁺ gradient

**Two types of co-transport:**

| Type | Direction | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Symport** (نقل متوازي) | Same direction → → | Both molecules move in the **same** direction (e.g., H⁺ and glucose both enter the cell) |
| **Antiport** (نقل متعاكس) | Opposite directions → ← | Molecules move in **opposite** directions (e.g., Na⁺ enters while Ca²⁺ exits) |

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Bulk Transport – Moving Large Cargo

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Endocytosis_types.svg/600px-Endocytosis_types.svg.png" alt="Diagram showing phagocytosis, pinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis" caption="The three main types of Endocytosis: Phagocytosis (solid particles), Pinocytosis (fluids), and Receptor-mediated." width="500px" />

<Definition term="Bulk Transport (النقل بالجملة)">
Transport of **very large molecules** or particles that are too big for any channel or carrier protein. Requires **energy (ATP)** and involves the membrane itself wrapping around the cargo. Classified as a form of **active transport**.
</Definition>

<Tabs items={["📥 Endocytosis", "📤 Exocytosis"]}>
<Tab label="📥 Endocytosis">

### Endocytosis (الإدخال الخلوي) – "Endo = Into"

The cell **takes in** large materials by wrapping the membrane around them to form a **vesicle** that pinches off inside the cell.

| Type | What It Takes In | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phagocytosis** (البلعمة) – "cell eating" | Large **solid** particles (bacteria, food, debris) | Pseudopods (extensions of the membrane) surround and engulf the particle → forms a **food vacuole** |
| **Pinocytosis** (الشرب الخلوي) – "cell drinking" | **Fluids** and dissolved substances | Membrane folds inward forming small vesicles containing extracellular fluid |
| **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** | **Specific** molecules that bind to receptors | Target molecule (ligand) binds to specific receptor → coated pit forms → **coated vesicle** pinches off |

**Example:** Macrophages (immune cells) use **phagocytosis** to engulf and destroy bacteria – connecting back to the immune system concepts from LO 2.

</Tab>
<Tab label="📤 Exocytosis">

### Exocytosis (الإخراج الخلوي) – "Exo = Out of"

The cell **pushes out** large materials by fusing a vesicle with the plasma membrane, releasing its contents outside.

**Used for:**
- Removing **waste** products
- **Secreting** hormones, neurotransmitters, enzymes
- Plant cells send out **carbohydrates** (cellulose) to build the cell wall

**Example:** Neurons release neurotransmitters via exocytosis at the synapse – this is how nerve signals are transmitted from one cell to the next.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Master Summary Table – All Transport Types

| Type | Energy? | Direction | Protein Needed? | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple Diffusion** | ❌ No | High → Low | ❌ No | O₂, CO₂, fatty acids, steroid hormones |
| **Facilitated Diffusion** | ❌ No | High → Low | ✅ Yes (channel or carrier) | Glucose, ions, water (aquaporins) |
| **Osmosis** | ❌ No | High water → Low water | ✅ Yes (aquaporins) or through bilayer | Water |
| **Primary Active Transport** | ✅ Yes (ATP directly) | Low → High | ✅ Yes (pump/carrier) | Na⁺/K⁺ pump, proton pump |
| **Secondary Active Transport** | ✅ Yes (indirectly from gradient) | Low → High | ✅ Yes (co-transporter) | Glucose-H⁺ symport |
| **Endocytosis** | ✅ Yes | Into cell | Membrane wraps cargo | Phagocytosis, pinocytosis |
| **Exocytosis** | ✅ Yes | Out of cell | Vesicle fuses with membrane | Neurotransmitter release, waste removal |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📊">
**Factors that increase passive transport rate:**
1. **↑ Concentration gradient** – steeper gradient = faster transport
2. **↑ Temperature** – molecules move faster
3. **↑ Surface area** – more membrane area = more room for molecules to cross
4. **↑ Membrane permeability** – lipid-soluble molecules cross faster
5. **↓ Membrane thickness** – thinner membrane = shorter distance to cross
6. **↓ Molecular size** – smaller molecules cross faster
</Callout>

## Diabetes Mellitus – The Case Study

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/01/Main_symptoms_of_diabetes.svg" alt="Diagram showing the main symptoms of diabetes" caption="Main symptoms of diabetes. Notice how many, like extreme thirst and frequent urination, are caused by osmosis due to high blood glucose." width="420px" />

<Callout type="info" emoji="🩺">
Diabetes perfectly illustrates how a failure in **membrane transport** mechanisms can cause a systemic disease. Everything you've learned about glucose transport, carrier proteins, and concentration gradients comes together here.
</Callout>

<Definition term="Diabetes Mellitus (داء السكري)">
A chronic disease characterised by **elevated blood glucose levels** (hyperglycaemia). Caused by problems with **insulin** – the hormone that enables glucose to enter cells via membrane transport proteins. Without functioning insulin signalling, glucose accumulates in the blood while cells starve for energy.
</Definition>

### The Insulin-Glucose Connection

In a healthy body:
1. You eat food → carbohydrates are broken down into **glucose**
2. Glucose enters the bloodstream → blood glucose rises
3. The **pancreas** detects high blood glucose
4. **Beta cells** (β-cells) in the pancreas secrete **insulin**
5. Insulin travels through the blood to body cells
6. Insulin binds to **receptors** on cell membranes → signals **glucose transporters (GLUT)** to move to the cell surface
7. Glucose enters the cell via **facilitated diffusion** through GLUT proteins
8. Blood glucose returns to normal

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**The membrane connection**: Insulin works by activating **membrane transport proteins** (GLUT transporters). Without insulin, these transporters stay inactive inside the cell and glucose **cannot cross the membrane** – even though glucose is abundant in the blood. This is diabetes at the cellular level.
</Callout>

### The Three Types of Diabetes

<Tabs items={["🔵 Type 1", "🟠 Type 2", "🤰 Gestational"]}>
<Tab label="🔵 Type 1">

### Type 1 Diabetes (النوع الأول)

- **Cause:** An **autoimmune disorder** – the immune system mistakenly attacks and **destroys the beta cells** in the pancreas
- **Result:** The pancreas produces **little or no insulin**
- **Onset:** Usually diagnosed in **children and young adults**
- **Treatment:** Daily **insulin injections** (the body cannot make its own)
- **Percentage:** ~5-10% of all diabetes cases

<Callout type="info" emoji="💉">
Type 1 diabetics are **insulin-dependent** for life. Without external insulin, glucose stays trapped in the blood, cells cannot access energy, and the body begins breaking down **fats** as an alternative fuel source – producing toxic acidic by-products called **ketones** that can lower blood pH dangerously (diabetic ketoacidosis).
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="🟠 Type 2">

### Type 2 Diabetes (النوع الثاني)

- **Cause:** Cells become **resistant to insulin** – they stop responding properly to insulin's signal. The pancreas may also produce **insufficient insulin** over time
- **Result:** Glucose cannot enter cells efficiently despite insulin being present
- **Onset:** Most common in **middle-aged and older adults**, but increasingly seen in younger people
- **Treatment:** Lifestyle changes (diet, exercise), oral medications (e.g., **Metformin**), sometimes insulin
- **Percentage:** ~90-95% of all diabetes cases
- **Strong link to:** Obesity, physical inactivity, genetics

</Tab>
<Tab label="🤰 Gestational">

### Gestational Diabetes (سكري الحمل)

- **Cause:** During pregnancy, the **placenta produces hormones** that make cells **more resistant to insulin**
- **Result:** Blood glucose rises during pregnancy
- **Onset:** Develops during **pregnancy** (usually 2nd or 3rd trimester)
- **Outcome:** Usually resolves **after birth**, but increases the mother's risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later in life

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Symptoms of Diabetes

<Steps>
<Step title="Extreme thirst (العطش الشديد)">
High blood glucose pulls water out of tissues via osmosis → dehydration → intense thirst.
</Step>
<Step title="Frequent urination (كثرة التبول)">
Kidneys cannot reabsorb all the excess glucose → glucose spills into urine → pulls water with it (osmotic diuresis) → increased urine volume.
</Step>
<Step title="Unexplained weight loss">
Cells cannot access glucose for energy → body breaks down **fats and muscle** as alternative fuel → weight loss despite eating. More common in Type 1.
</Step>
<Step title="Extreme hunger (الجوع الشديد)">
Cells are starving for glucose even though blood glucose is high → hunger signals increase.
</Step>
<Step title="Fatigue & blurry vision">
Cells lack energy (no glucose access) → tiredness. High blood sugar affects the lens of the eye → blurry vision.
</Step>
<Step title="Slow wound healing">
High glucose damages blood vessels and impairs immune function → wounds heal slowly.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🔬">
**Connecting to membrane transport:** Notice how many symptoms are explained by **osmosis**. High glucose in blood pulls water out of tissues (thirst). High glucose in urine pulls water into the kidneys (frequent urination). Diabetes is a disease of **disrupted membrane transport** at every level.
</Callout>

### Prevention & Treatment

| Strategy | Details |
| --- | --- |
| **Healthy diet** | Choose foods lower in fat/calories, higher in fibre. Focus on fruits, vegetables, whole grains |
| **Physical activity** | ≥150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic exercise. Exercise makes cells **more sensitive to insulin** |
| **Weight management** | Losing just **7%** of body weight significantly reduces Type 2 risk |
| **Blood sugar monitoring** | Regular testing to keep glucose within target range. **A1C test** measures average glucose over 2-3 months |
| **Insulin therapy** | Essential for Type 1; sometimes needed for Type 2. Delivered via injection, pen, or insulin pump |
| **Oral medications** | **Metformin** (first-line for Type 2) – reduces glucose production by the liver and increases insulin sensitivity |
| **Pancreas transplant** | Option for severe Type 1 cases – eliminates need for insulin injections but carries surgical risks |

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms – LO 3">
<Term word="Plasma Membrane (غشاء البلازما)">The selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer surrounding all cells. Controls what enters and exits. Made of phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and glycolipids/glycoproteins.</Term>
<Term word="Phospholipid Bilayer (طبقة الفوسفوليبيد الثنائية)">Two layers of phospholipids forming the membrane framework. Hydrophilic heads face outward; hydrophobic tails face inward.</Term>
<Term word="Hydrophilic (محب للماء)">Water-loving. The phosphate heads of phospholipids are hydrophilic and face the aqueous environment.</Term>
<Term word="Hydrophobic (كاره للماء)">Water-fearing. The fatty acid tails of phospholipids are hydrophobic and face inward, away from water.</Term>
<Term word="Cholesterol (الكوليسترول)">A sterol lipid in animal cell membranes. Acts as a fluidity buffer: increases fluidity at low temps, decreases fluidity at high temps.</Term>
<Term word="Integral Protein (بروتين مدمج)">A protein embedded within the phospholipid bilayer. Has hydrophobic regions anchoring it in the membrane. Includes transmembrane proteins.</Term>
<Term word="Peripheral Protein (بروتين طرفي)">A protein attached to the membrane surface (not embedded). Easily removed without disrupting bilayer structure.</Term>
<Term word="Transmembrane Protein (بروتين عابر للغشاء)">An integral protein spanning the entire bilayer, exposed on both sides. Often functions as a channel, carrier, or receptor.</Term>
<Term word="Glycocalyx (الغلاف السكري)">Sugar-rich coat on the outer cell surface made of glycoproteins and glycolipids. Functions in cell recognition, immune identity, and protection.</Term>
<Term word="Glycoprotein (بروتين سكري)">A protein with carbohydrate chains attached. Found on the membrane surface for cell recognition and signalling.</Term>
<Term word="Glycolipid (ليبيد سكري)">A lipid with carbohydrate chains attached. Part of the glycocalyx; aids in cell recognition.</Term>
<Term word="Fluid Mosaic Model (نموذج الفسيفساء المائع)">The accepted model of membrane structure: a fluid phospholipid bilayer with a mosaic of embedded proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates.</Term>
<Term word="Lateral Diffusion (الانتشار الجانبي)">Side-to-side movement of phospholipids within the same membrane layer. Occurs ~10⁷ times/second.</Term>
<Term word="Transverse Diffusion / Flip-Flop">Movement of a phospholipid from one layer to the other. Very rare (~once/month). Requires flippase or floppase enzymes.</Term>
<Term word="Selective Permeability (النفاذية الاختيارية)">The membrane's ability to allow some substances to pass while blocking others, based on size, polarity, and charge.</Term>
<Term word="Simple Diffusion (الانتشار البسيط)">Passive movement of small nonpolar/lipid-soluble molecules directly through the bilayer. No protein needed.</Term>
<Term word="Facilitated Diffusion (الانتشار الميسر)">Passive movement of large, polar, or charged molecules through channel or carrier proteins. No ATP needed.</Term>
<Term word="Channel Protein (بروتين قنوي)">A transmembrane protein forming a water-filled pore for ions and small polar molecules. Types: leak, ligand-gated, voltage-gated.</Term>
<Term word="Carrier Protein (بروتين حامل)">A transmembrane protein that binds specific molecules and changes shape to move them across. Shows specificity and saturation.</Term>
<Term word="Aquaporin (أكوابورين)">A channel protein specifically for rapid water transport across the membrane. Classified under facilitated diffusion.</Term>
<Term word="Osmosis (التناضح)">Diffusion of water from high water concentration (low solute) to low water concentration (high solute) across a semi-permeable membrane.</Term>
<Term word="Hypertonic (مفرط التوتر)">A solution with higher solute concentration than the cell. Water moves out → cell shrinks (crenation in animal cells, plasmolysis in plant cells).</Term>
<Term word="Hypotonic (منخفض التوتر)">A solution with lower solute concentration than the cell. Water moves in → cell swells. Animal cells may lyse; plant cells become turgid.</Term>
<Term word="Isotonic (متساوي التوتر)">A solution with equal solute concentration as the cell. No net water movement. Cell maintains normal shape.</Term>
<Term word="Turgor Pressure (ضغط الامتلاء)">The pressure exerted by water inside the central vacuole against the cell wall in plant cells. Keeps plants rigid and upright.</Term>
<Term word="Active Transport (النقل النشط)">Movement of molecules against their concentration gradient (low → high). Requires ATP energy. Uses carrier proteins.</Term>
<Term word="Na⁺/K⁺ Pump (مضخة الصوديوم-البوتاسيوم)">Primary active transport pump: moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP consumed. Maintains electrochemical gradient.</Term>
<Term word="Symport (نقل متوازي)">Co-transport where two molecules move in the same direction across the membrane.</Term>
<Term word="Antiport (نقل متعاكس)">Co-transport where two molecules move in opposite directions across the membrane.</Term>
<Term word="Endocytosis (الإدخال الخلوي)">Bulk transport bringing large materials INTO the cell by wrapping the membrane around them. Types: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated.</Term>
<Term word="Exocytosis (الإخراج الخلوي)">Bulk transport pushing large materials OUT of the cell by fusing vesicles with the plasma membrane.</Term>
<Term word="Phagocytosis (البلعمة)">Endocytosis of large solid particles (cell eating). Used by macrophages to engulf bacteria.</Term>
<Term word="Pinocytosis (الشرب الخلوي)">Endocytosis of fluids and dissolved substances (cell drinking).</Term>
<Term word="Diabetes Mellitus (داء السكري)">Chronic disease of elevated blood glucose due to problems with insulin production or insulin resistance.</Term>
<Term word="Insulin (الأنسولين)">Hormone produced by beta cells of the pancreas. Enables glucose to enter cells by activating GLUT transporters on the membrane.</Term>
<Term word="Concentration Gradient (مدروج التركيز)">The difference in concentration of a substance between two areas. Molecules naturally move down their gradient (high → low).</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Membrane structure**: Phospholipid bilayer (hydrophilic heads out, hydrophobic tails in) + proteins (integral and peripheral) + cholesterol (fluidity buffer) + glycocalyx (cell recognition).</Point>
<Point>**Fluid Mosaic Model**: The membrane is fluid (lipids and proteins move laterally) and mosaic (a patchwork of different molecules).</Point>
<Point>**Fluidity factors**: ↑Temperature → ↑fluidity. Cholesterol buffers fluidity. Unsaturated fatty acids (kinked tails) → ↑fluidity. Saturated (straight tails) → ↓fluidity.</Point>
<Point>**Lateral diffusion** = side-to-side movement (~10⁷/sec, very common). **Flip-flop** = layer-to-layer movement (once/month, needs flippase/floppase).</Point>
<Point>**"Like dissolves like"**: Small, nonpolar, lipid-soluble molecules pass freely through the hydrophobic core. Large, polar, or charged molecules need protein help.</Point>
<Point>**Passive transport** = no ATP, moves DOWN gradient (high → low). Includes simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and osmosis.</Point>
<Point>**Active transport** = requires ATP, moves AGAINST gradient (low → high). Na⁺/K⁺ pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP.</Point>
<Point>**Osmosis**: Water moves from low solute to high solute. Hypertonic → cell shrinks. Hypotonic → cell swells (turgid in plants, lysis in animals). Isotonic → no net change.</Point>
<Point>**Bulk transport** (endocytosis/exocytosis): For very large molecules. Membrane wraps around cargo. Requires ATP. Phagocytosis = solids; pinocytosis = fluids.</Point>
<Point>**Diabetes** = failure of glucose membrane transport. Type 1: immune system destroys beta cells → no insulin. Type 2: cells resist insulin → glucose can't enter. Both result in high blood glucose.</Point>
<Point>**Glycocalyx** determines blood type (A, B, AB, O), enables immune recognition (self vs foreign), and prevents dehydration.</Point>
<Point>**Integral proteins** are amphipathic (both hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions). **Peripheral proteins** sit on the surface and are not embedded.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
