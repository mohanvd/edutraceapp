# Cell Structure & Function – Comparing Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

<Callout type="info" emoji="🔬">
This is the **heart** of Grade 10 Biology – the **Cytology** topic. LO 1 gave you the roadmap; now you're zooming in to study every room in the building. You'll compare plant, animal, protist, and bacterial cells at the organelle level, understand why structure determines function, and finish with a real-world case study on **Tuberculosis (TB)** – the disease that brings cell biology to life.
</Callout>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧩">
**Essential Question:** How does structure influence function?

**Big Idea:** Cells are the basic unit of structure and function in organisms. Every organelle has a specific structure engineered for a specific job – change the structure and you change (or destroy) the function.
</Callout>

## Two Domains of Cellular Life

Every living cell on Earth falls into one of two categories: **prokaryotic** or **eukaryotic**. The single most important distinction in all of cell biology.

<Definition term="Prokaryotic Cell (خلية بدائية النواة)">
A cell that **lacks** a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. The DNA floats freely in the cytoplasm in a region called the **nucleoid**. From Greek: ***pro*** = before + ***karyon*** = kernel/nucleus. Examples: bacteria and archaea.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Eukaryotic Cell (خلية حقيقية النواة)">
A cell that **has** a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc.). From Greek: ***eu*** = true + ***karyon*** = kernel/nucleus. Examples: animals, plants, fungi, and protists.
</Definition>

<Compare>
<Item label="Prokaryotic Cells">
- **No** membrane-bound nucleus – DNA in nucleoid region
- **No** membrane-bound organelles – no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi
- Generally **smaller** (1–10 μm)
- **Simpler** internal structure
- Always **unicellular**
- Examples: bacteria, archaea
- Have **ribosomes** (smaller, 70S)
- May have **cell wall** (peptidoglycan in bacteria)
</Item>
<Item label="Eukaryotic Cells">
- **Has** membrane-bound nucleus with nuclear envelope
- **Has** membrane-bound organelles – mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, etc.
- Generally **larger** (10–100 μm)
- **Complex** internal structure with compartmentalisation
- Can be unicellular OR multicellular
- Examples: plants, animals, fungi, protists
- Have **ribosomes** (larger, 80S)
- Cell wall present in plants (cellulose), fungi (chitin) – **absent** in animal cells
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Common exam trap**: Not all unicellular organisms are prokaryotic! Amoeba, yeast, and many protists are **unicellular eukaryotes** – they HAVE a membrane-bound nucleus. Unicellular ≠ prokaryotic. And all prokaryotes are unicellular, but not all unicellular organisms are prokaryotes.
</Callout>

## The Four Things ALL Cells Share

Despite the enormous differences between a bacterium and a human muscle cell, **every living cell** on Earth shares these four components:

<Steps>
<Step title="1. DNA (الحمض النووي)">
The genetic material – the instruction manual for everything the cell does. In prokaryotes, DNA is circular and floats in the cytoplasm. In eukaryotes, DNA is linear and wrapped inside the nucleus.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Cytoplasm (السيتوبلازم)">
The gel-like fluid filling the interior of the cell. All chemical reactions of life occur here. Contains water, salts, enzymes, and (in eukaryotes) organelles.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Plasma Membrane (غشاء البلازما)">
The selectively permeable barrier surrounding every cell. Controls what enters and exits. Made of a **phospholipid bilayer** with embedded proteins.
</Step>
<Step title="4. Ribosomes (الريبوسومات)">
The protein-making machines. Found in ALL cells – prokaryotic and eukaryotic. Prokaryotes have smaller 70S ribosomes; eukaryotes have larger 80S ribosomes.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**Exam favourite**: "What do ALL cells have in common?" → DNA, cytoplasm, plasma membrane, and ribosomes. These four are **universal** – remove any one and the cell cannot survive.
</Callout>

## Cell Theory – A Quick Reminder

You covered this in LO 1, but it's the foundation of everything here:

1. **The cell is the fundamental unit of structure and function** in all living organisms
2. **All organisms are made of one or more cells**
3. **All cells arise from pre-existing cells** through cell division

<Timeline>
<TimelineItem date="1665" title="Robert Hooke">
Observed dead cork cells under a microscope and coined the term **"cell"**.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1670s" title="Antonie van Leeuwenhoek">
First to observe **living cells** – bacteria, protists, and sperm cells.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1838" title="Matthias Schleiden">
Concluded all **plants** are made of cells.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1839" title="Theodor Schwann">
Concluded all **animals** are made of cells. Together with Schleiden, established cell theory.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1855" title="Rudolf Virchow">
Added the third principle: **"Omnis cellula e cellula"** – all cells come from pre-existing cells.
</TimelineItem>
</Timeline>

## Bacterial (Prokaryotic) Cell Structure

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Average_prokaryote_cell-_en.svg/600px-Average_prokaryote_cell-_en.svg.png" alt="Diagram of a typical prokaryotic (bacterial) cell showing all major structures" caption="A typical bacterial cell – note the absence of a membrane-bound nucleus" width="400px" />

Bacteria are the simplest cells, but they're incredibly successful – they've been on Earth for over 3.5 billion years and live in virtually every environment.

### External Structures

<Tabs items={["🧱 Cell Wall", "🛡️ Capsule", "🏊 Flagella & Pili"]}>
<Tab label="🧱 Cell Wall">

### Cell Wall (الجدار الخلوي)

A rigid outer layer surrounding the plasma membrane, made of **peptidoglycan** (a sugar-protein polymer unique to bacteria).

- Provides **shape** and **structural support**
- Protects against **osmotic lysis** (bursting from water influx)
- Present in **all bacteria** (and all prokaryotes in general)
- **Gram staining** classifies bacteria by cell wall thickness:
  - **Gram-positive**: thick peptidoglycan layer → stains purple
  - **Gram-negative**: thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane → stains pink

<Callout type="default" emoji="💊">
Many **antibiotics** work by attacking the bacterial cell wall. Since human cells lack cell walls, these antibiotics are **selectively toxic** – they kill bacteria without harming your cells. This is a key exam concept.
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="🛡️ Capsule">

### Capsule (الكبسولة)

A sticky, gel-like layer **outside** the cell wall found in some bacteria.

- Made of **polysaccharides** or proteins
- Functions as a **shield** against the host's immune system – prevents phagocytosis (engulfing) by white blood cells
- Helps bacteria **stick** to surfaces and to each other (biofilm formation)
- Acts as a **virulence factor** – bacteria with capsules are often more dangerous

<Callout type="info" emoji="🔑">
The three layers from outside to inside: **Capsule → Cell Wall → Plasma Membrane**. Think of it as armour → skeleton → skin.
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="🏊 Flagella & Pili">

### Flagella (الأسواط)

Long, whip-like appendages used for **motility** (movement).

- Made of a protein called **flagellin**
- Enable bacteria to **swim** towards nutrients or away from danger (chemotaxis)
- Also serve as **antigenic factors** – the immune system can recognise flagella and mount an immune response

### Pili (الخمل)

Short, hair-like projections on the bacterial surface.

- Help bacteria **attach** to host cells and other surfaces
- **Sex pili** allow transfer of DNA between bacteria (conjugation)
- Important for **infection** – bacteria use pili to grip onto tissues

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Internal Structures

| Structure | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Nucleoid region** | The area where circular DNA is concentrated – NOT enclosed by a membrane |
| **Plasmids** | Small circular DNA molecules separate from the main chromosome; carry genes for antibiotic resistance |
| **Ribosomes (70S)** | Smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes; site of protein synthesis |
| **Cytoplasm** | Gel-like fluid containing all cellular components |
| **Cytoskeleton** | Protein filaments that help maintain cell shape (simpler than eukaryotic cytoskeleton) |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Bacteria have NO membrane-bound organelles** – no nucleus, no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi, no chloroplasts, no lysosomes. This is the fundamental difference from eukaryotic cells. Though bacteria lack mitochondria, they perform **aerobic respiration on the inner surface of their plasma membrane** – this becomes critical when you study the **Endosymbiotic Theory**.
</Callout>

## Eukaryotic Cell Overview

Eukaryotic cells are vastly more complex than prokaryotic cells. They are **compartmentalised** – different functions happen in different membrane-bound compartments (organelles), like departments in a company.

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Animal_cell_structure_en.svg/600px-Animal_cell_structure_en.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of an animal cell showing all major organelles" caption="A typical animal cell – note the nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi apparatus, and lysosomes" width="420px" />

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Plant_cell_structure_svg_labels.svg/600px-Plant_cell_structure_svg_labels.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of a plant cell showing all major organelles including chloroplast and cell wall" caption="A typical plant cell – note the cell wall, chloroplasts, and large central vacuole that animal cells lack" width="420px" />

### Five Types of Eukaryotic Organisms

| Type | Examples | Unicellular or Multicellular | Cell Wall? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Animals** | Humans, dogs, insects | Multicellular | **No** |
| **Plants** | Trees, grasses, ferns | Multicellular | **Yes** (cellulose) |
| **Fungi** | Mushrooms, yeast, moulds | Both | **Yes** (chitin) |
| **Protists** | Amoeba, Paramecium, algae | Mostly unicellular | Some do, some don't |
| **Algae** | Green algae, seaweed | Both | **Yes** (cellulose) |

## The Nucleus – The Control Centre

<Definition term="Nucleus (النواة)">
The largest organelle in eukaryotic cells. Contains the cell's genetic material (DNA) organised into **chromosomes**. Enclosed by a **double membrane** called the **nuclear envelope**. Functions as the "brain" of the cell – controlling gene expression, growth, metabolism, and reproduction.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/Diagram_human_cell_nucleus.svg/500px-Diagram_human_cell_nucleus.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of the cell nucleus showing nuclear envelope, nucleolus, chromatin, and nuclear pores" caption="Structure of the nucleus – note the double membrane (nuclear envelope), nuclear pores, chromatin, and nucleolus" width="360px" />

### Components of the Nucleus

<Steps>
<Step title="Nuclear Envelope (الغلاف النووي)">
A **double membrane** (two phospholipid bilayers) surrounding the nucleus. Continuous with the **rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)**. Perforated by thousands of **nuclear pores** that regulate molecular traffic in and out of the nucleus.
</Step>
<Step title="Nuclear Pores (الثقوب النووية)">
Tiny channels in the nuclear envelope. Allow **mRNA** to exit (carrying instructions for protein synthesis) and allow **proteins** and **ribosomes** to enter. Think of them as security-controlled gates.
</Step>
<Step title="Chromatin (الكروماتين)">
The combination of **DNA + histone proteins** inside the nucleus. Exists in two forms:
- **Euchromatin** (الكروماتين الحقيقي) – loosely packed, **active** DNA being transcribed into proteins
- **Heterochromatin** (الكروماتين المغاير) – tightly packed, **inactive** DNA (not currently being used)
</Step>
<Step title="Nucleolus (النُوَيّة)">
A dense region **inside** the nucleus (not membrane-bound). Responsible for producing **ribosomal RNA (rRNA)** and assembling **ribosome subunits**. The nucleolus is essentially a ribosome factory.
</Step>
<Step title="Nucleoplasm (البلازما النووية)">
The gel-like substance inside the nucleus; equivalent to the cytoplasm but within the nuclear envelope.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧬">
The nucleus occupies about **10%** of the cell's volume but controls **100%** of its activities. Without the nucleus, the cell loses its ability to make proteins, repair itself, or divide – it dies. **Red blood cells** and **platelets** have no nucleus, which is why they are not considered true cells and have a limited lifespan.
</Callout>

## Ribosomes – The Protein Factories

<Definition term="Ribosome (الريبوسوم)">
A non-membranous organelle responsible for **protein synthesis** (translation). Made of **rRNA** and protein. Found in ALL cells – prokaryotic and eukaryotic.
</Definition>

- **Free ribosomes**: Float in the cytoplasm → make proteins **used inside the cell** (e.g., enzymes for metabolism)
- **Bound ribosomes**: Attached to the rough ER → make proteins **destined for export**, insertion into membranes, or use in lysosomes

| Feature | Prokaryotic Ribosomes | Eukaryotic Ribosomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | **70S** (smaller) | **80S** (larger) |
| Location | Cytoplasm only | Cytoplasm, attached to RER, inside mitochondria (70S) & chloroplasts (70S) |
| Made by | Nucleoid region | **Nucleolus** |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own **70S ribosomes** (the same size as bacterial ribosomes) – this is powerful evidence for the **Endosymbiotic Theory** (discussed later in this lesson).
</Callout>

## Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) – The Highway System

<Definition term="Endoplasmic Reticulum (الشبكة الإندوبلازمية)">
An extensive network of **membrane-enclosed channels** extending from the nuclear envelope throughout the cytoplasm. The largest organelle by total membrane area. Comes in two types: **Rough ER** (RER) and **Smooth ER** (SER).
</Definition>

<Tabs items={["📦 Rough ER (RER)", "🧴 Smooth ER (SER)"]}>
<Tab label="📦 Rough ER (RER)">

### Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (الشبكة الإندوبلازمية الخشنة)

Called "rough" because its surface is **studded with ribosomes**.

**Structure:**
- Continuous with the **nuclear envelope**
- Flattened, membrane-bound sacs (cisternae) covered in ribosomes

**Functions:**
1. **Synthesises three types of proteins:**
   - **Membrane proteins** – incorporated into cell membranes
   - **Secretory proteins** – exported out of the cell (e.g., hormones, antibodies)
   - **Lysosomal enzymes** – sent to lysosomes for intracellular digestion
2. **Initial glycosylation** – begins adding carbohydrate groups to proteins (completed by Golgi)
3. **Quality control** – folds proteins into their correct 3D shape; misfolded proteins are destroyed

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**Protein pathway**: Ribosome on RER makes protein → protein enters RER lumen → protein is folded and partially glycosylated → packaged into a **transport vesicle** → sent to the **Golgi apparatus** for further processing.
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="🧴 Smooth ER (SER)">

### Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (الشبكة الإندوبلازمية الملساء)

Called "smooth" because it has **no ribosomes** on its surface.

**Structure:**
- Network of tubular membranes
- Continuous with the rough ER

**Functions:**
1. **Lipid synthesis** – makes phospholipids, steroids, and fatty acids
2. **Steroid hormone production** – abundant in cells that make hormones (testes, ovaries, adrenal glands)
3. **Detoxification** – breaks down drugs, alcohol, and toxins (liver cells are packed with SER)
4. **Calcium storage and release** – especially important in **muscle cells** (sarcoplasmic reticulum)
5. **Phospholipid synthesis** – makes the building blocks of all cell membranes

**Where is SER most abundant?**
- **Liver cells** – detoxification
- **Muscle cells** – calcium regulation
- **Adipose (fat) cells** – lipid synthesis
- **Testes/Ovaries** – steroid hormone production

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧪">
**Exam scenario**: "A person is found to have been drinking contaminated water with harmful chemicals. Which organelle would increase in the liver cells?" → **Smooth ER** – because it handles detoxification.
</Callout>

</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Golgi Apparatus – The Post Office

<Definition term="Golgi Apparatus (جهاز جولجي)">
A stack of flattened, membrane-bound sacs (cisternae) that **receives**, **modifies**, **sorts**, and **ships** proteins and lipids from the ER to their final destinations. Discovered by Italian physician **Camillo Golgi** in 1898.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Golgi_apparatus_%28borderless_version%29-en.svg/500px-Golgi_apparatus_%28borderless_version%29-en.svg.png" alt="Diagram of the Golgi apparatus showing cis face, trans face, and vesicle transport" caption="The Golgi apparatus – proteins arrive at the cis face and leave from the trans face" width="400px" />

### Structure

- **Cis face** (الوجه المستقبل) – the "receiving dock," faces the ER. Vesicles from the RER arrive here.
- **Trans face** (الوجه المرسل) – the "shipping dock," faces the plasma membrane. Modified products leave from here.
- **Cisternae** – the stacked, flattened membrane sacs in between where processing occurs.

### Functions

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Molecular Modification (التعديل الجزيئي)">
Adds **phosphate groups** to proteins – this acts as an **address label**, directing the protein to its correct destination (plasma membrane, lysosome, or secretion outside the cell).
</Step>
<Step title="2. Chemical Modification – Glycosylation (الغلوزة)">
Adds or modifies **carbohydrate (CHO) groups** on proteins → produces **glycoproteins**. This is crucial for cell recognition and signalling.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Sorting and Packaging">
Sorts modified proteins and lipids into **vesicles** for transport to:
- The **plasma membrane** (for secretion or membrane insertion)
- **Lysosomes** (for intracellular digestion)
- **Secretory vesicles** (for export outside the cell)
</Step>
<Step title="4. Polysaccharide Synthesis">
Synthesises **polysaccharides** like **cellulose** (for plant cell walls) and other complex carbohydrates.
</Step>
<Step title="5. Controls Membrane Composition">
Determines the **ratio of proteins to lipids** in the plasma membrane. This is a critical regulatory function.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**KEY EXAM CONCEPT**: Which organelle controls the ratio/percentage of proteins vs lipids in the plasma membrane? It's the **Golgi apparatus** – NOT the RER and NOT the SER. The Golgi is the final decision-maker on what gets sent to the membrane.
</Callout>

## Mitochondria – The Powerhouse

<Definition term="Mitochondria (الميتوكوندريا)">
Double-membraned organelles that generate most of the cell's **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) through **cellular respiration**. Often called the "powerhouse of the cell." Contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Animal_mitochondrion_diagram_en.svg/500px-Animal_mitochondrion_diagram_en.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of a mitochondrion showing outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, and matrix" caption="Structure of a mitochondrion – the inner membrane folds (cristae) greatly increase surface area for ATP production" width="380px" />

### Structure

| Component | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Outer membrane** | Smooth, permeable to small molecules |
| **Inner membrane** | Highly folded into **cristae** (singular: crista) – increases surface area for ATP production |
| **Intermembrane space** | Gap between outer and inner membranes |
| **Matrix** | The innermost compartment; contains enzymes for the Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA, and 70S ribosomes |

### Key Features – "So So So Rich"

Mitochondria are described as an organelle that is "so so so rich" in features:

1. **Double membrane** – outer and inner membranes (like the nucleus and chloroplast)
2. **Own DNA** – circular, like bacterial DNA
3. **Own ribosomes** – 70S, like bacterial ribosomes (not 80S like other eukaryotic ribosomes)
4. Can **self-replicate** independently by binary fission
5. **Autonomous organelle** – makes some of its own proteins independently of the nucleus

### Abundance Varies by Cell Activity

| Cell Type | Approx. Mitochondria Count | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Muscle cell** | ~1,000 | Extremely high energy demand for contraction |
| **Liver cell** | ~1,000–2,000 | High metabolic activity (detoxification, synthesis) |
| **Lymphocyte (white blood cell)** | ~5 | Relatively low energy demand |
| **Red blood cell** | **0** | No nucleus, no mitochondria – not a true cell |

<Callout type="info" emoji="⚡">
The chemical equation for cellular respiration:

**Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + ATP (energy)**

This is essentially the **reverse** of photosynthesis. Plants make glucose; mitochondria break it down to release energy.
</Callout>

## Chloroplasts & Plastids – The Solar Panels

<Definition term="Chloroplast (البلاستيدة الخضراء)">
A double-membraned organelle found in **plant cells** and **algae** that performs **photosynthesis** – converting solar energy into chemical energy (glucose/starch). Contains its own DNA and ribosomes. Like mitochondria, it is an autonomous "so so so rich" organelle.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Chloroplast_II.svg/500px-Chloroplast_II.svg.png" alt="Detailed diagram of a chloroplast showing thylakoids, grana, stroma, and double membrane" caption="Structure of a chloroplast – thylakoids stacked into grana are where light reactions occur" width="380px" />

### Structure

| Component | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Outer membrane** | Smooth outer boundary |
| **Inner membrane** | Encloses the stroma |
| **Thylakoid** | Flattened, disc-shaped membrane sacs where light reactions occur |
| **Granum** (plural: grana) | A stack of thylakoids – like a stack of coins |
| **Stroma** | The fluid-filled space between the inner membrane and thylakoids; site of the Calvin cycle |
| **Own DNA** | Circular, like bacterial and mitochondrial DNA |
| **Own ribosomes (70S)** | Like bacterial ribosomes – evidence for endosymbiosis |
| **Starch granules** | Storage of glucose as starch within the chloroplast |

### Pigments

- **Chlorophyll a** – the primary photosynthetic pigment (absorbs red and blue light, reflects green)
- **Chlorophyll b** – an accessory pigment that broadens the spectrum of light absorbed
- **Green algae and plants** share both **chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b** – this is evidence that plants evolved from green algae

<Callout type="info" emoji="☀️">
**Chloroplast converts solar energy → chemical energy** (stored as food/starch). This is NOT called an "energy function" – the correct term is **photosynthesis**. The "energy organelle" (which converts chemical energy to ATP) is the **mitochondrion**.
</Callout>

### The Three Types of Plastids

<Compare>
<Item label="Chloroplast (البلاستيدة الخضراء)">
Contains **chlorophyll** pigments (green). Performs **photosynthesis** to convert light energy into chemical energy (glucose/starch). Found in green parts of plants and green algae.
</Item>
<Item label="Chromoplast (البلاستيدة الملونة)">
Contains **carotenoid** pigments (red, orange, yellow). Gives colour to flowers, fruits, and autumn leaves. Main function: **attracting pollinators** and seed dispersers through colour signals.
</Item>
</Compare>

<Definition term="Leucoplast (البلاستيدة عديمة اللون)">
A **non-pigmented** (colourless) plastid used for **storage** – stores starch, lipids, or proteins. Found in roots, tubers, and seeds. Since it has no pigments, it is not involved in photosynthesis.
</Definition>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🌈">
**Exam tip**: Chromoplast's function is attracting **pollinators** through colour (not photosynthesis). Leucoplast's function is **storage** (not photosynthesis). Only the chloroplast performs photosynthesis.
</Callout>

## Lysosomes – The Digestive System

<Definition term="Lysosome (الجسم الحالّ)">
A membrane-bound organelle containing **hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes** capable of breaking down virtually any biological molecule. Functions as the cell's "digestive system" and "recycling centre." pH inside ≈ **2** (highly acidic). Present mainly in **animal cells**.
</Definition>

### How Lysosomes Are Made

**RER** (makes the hydrolytic enzymes) → **Golgi apparatus** (packages them into vesicles) → **Lysosomes** (the finished product, ready for action)

### Types of Lysosomes

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary lysosome** | Newly formed, has not yet fused with anything; contains inactive enzymes |
| **Secondary lysosome (Heterolysosome)** | Formed when a primary lysosome fuses with a food vacuole or foreign particle (phagocytosis) |
| **Autolysosome** | Formed when a lysosome fuses with a damaged or worn-out organelle → recycles cellular components |
| **Multivesicular body** | Contains multiple smaller vesicles inside – involved in sorting and recycling |

### Functions

1. **Intracellular digestion** – breaks down food particles, bacteria, and viruses engulfed by the cell
2. **Phagocytosis** – white blood cells (macrophages) use lysosomes to destroy pathogens
3. **Autophagy** – digests and recycles the cell's own damaged or old organelles
4. **Apoptosis** (programmed cell death) – lysosomes can release their enzymes to destroy the cell from within when it is no longer needed or is dangerously infected
5. **Recycling** – breaks down macromolecules into monomers that can be reused by the cell

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Tay-Sachs Disease** is a genetic disorder where lysosomes **lack a specific lipase enzyme** → lipids (gangliosides) accumulate in brain cells → progressive neurological damage → typically fatal in early childhood. This is a classic exam example of what happens when lysosomal function fails.
</Callout>

## Peroxisomes

<Definition term="Peroxisome (البيروكسيسوم)">
A single-membraned organelle that **breaks down fatty acids** and **detoxifies harmful substances**. Produces **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)** as a by-product, then uses the enzyme **catalase** to convert it to water and oxygen. Abundant in **liver** and **kidney** cells.
</Definition>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🔬">
Don't confuse peroxisomes with lysosomes! Lysosomes use **hydrolytic enzymes at acidic pH** to digest materials. Peroxisomes use **oxidative enzymes** to break down fatty acids and detoxify substances; their hallmark is producing and then neutralising H₂O₂.
</Callout>

## Vacuoles

<Tabs items={["🌿 Plant Cell Vacuole", "🔵 Animal Cell Vacuoles"]}>
<Tab label="🌿 Plant Cell Vacuole">

### Central Vacuole (الفجوة العصارية المركزية)

The most prominent feature of a mature plant cell – can occupy up to **90%** of the cell volume.

- Enclosed by a membrane called the **tonoplast**
- Filled with **cell sap** – a solution of water, enzymes, pigments, salts, and stored nutrients
- **Functions:**
  - Maintains **turgor pressure** (keeps the plant rigid and upright)
  - Stores water, nutrients, pigments, and waste products
  - Helps the cell grow by absorbing water and expanding

</Tab>
<Tab label="🔵 Animal Cell Vacuoles">

### Animal Cell Vacuoles

Animal cells have **small, numerous vacuoles** (or may have none at all) – unlike the single, large vacuole in plant cells.

- **Food vacuoles**: Formed during phagocytosis; fuse with lysosomes for digestion
- **Contractile vacuoles**: Found in freshwater protists; pump out excess water to prevent bursting

</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Cytoskeleton – The Internal Scaffolding

<Definition term="Cytoskeleton (الهيكل الخلوي)">
A dynamic network of **protein filaments** extending throughout the cytoplasm. Provides **structural support**, enables **cell movement**, facilitates **intracellular transport**, and plays a critical role in **cell division**.
</Definition>

### Three Types of Cytoskeletal Filaments

| Type | Protein | Diameter | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Microfilaments** | **Actin** | ~7 nm (thinnest) | Cell shape, **cytoplasmic streaming**, **cleavage furrow** during cell division, muscle contraction |
| **Intermediate filaments** | **Myosin** and others | ~10 nm | Mechanical strength, anchoring organelles, resisting tension |
| **Microtubules** | **Tubulin** | ~25 nm (thickest) | **Chromosomal movement** during division (spindle fibres), intracellular transport, form cilia and flagella, form **centrosomes** |

### Structures Formed by Microtubules

- **Cilia** (الأهداب) – short, hair-like projections; move fluids across the cell surface (e.g., lining of respiratory tract)
- **Flagella** (الأسواط) – long, whip-like projections; enable entire-cell movement (e.g., sperm cells)
- **Centrosomes** (المريكزات) – organise the **mitotic spindle** during cell division, pulling chromosomes apart. Found in **animal cells** but generally **absent in plant cells**

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧩">
**Exam reminders:**
- **Microfilaments (actin)** → responsible for the **cleavage furrow** in animal cell division and **cytoplasmic streaming**
- **Microtubules (tubulin)** → responsible for **chromosomal movement** via the spindle apparatus
- Centrosomes are found in animal cells, not in plant cells
</Callout>

## The Endomembrane System – A United Team

<Definition term="Endomembrane System (نظام الأغشية الداخلية)">
A group of interconnected organelles that work together to **synthesise, modify, package, and transport** proteins and lipids. The system includes: **nuclear envelope**, **endoplasmic reticulum** (RER & SER), **Golgi apparatus**, **lysosomes**, **vesicles/vacuoles**, and the **plasma membrane**.
</Definition>

### Members of the Endomembrane System

<Steps>
<Step title="Nuclear Envelope">
The starting point – continuous with the RER. Contains genes that code for proteins.
</Step>
<Step title="Rough ER (RER)">
Synthesises proteins → folds them → packages them into transport vesicles → sends to Golgi.
</Step>
<Step title="Smooth ER (SER)">
Synthesises lipids and steroids → sends to Golgi for processing.
</Step>
<Step title="Golgi Apparatus">
Receives products from ER → modifies (glycosylation, phosphorylation) → sorts → packages → ships to final destinations.
</Step>
<Step title="Lysosomes">
Receive digestive enzymes from the Golgi → carry out intracellular digestion and recycling.
</Step>
<Step title="Vesicles & Vacuoles">
Transport containers that shuttle materials between organelles and to/from the plasma membrane.
</Step>
<Step title="Plasma Membrane">
The final destination for many proteins and lipids. Composition controlled by the Golgi.
</Step>
</Steps>

### The Pathway of a Protein

<Callout type="info" emoji="🛤️">
**DNA** (in nucleus) → **Transcription** → **mRNA** → exits through nuclear pore → **Translation** on ribosomes (attached to RER) → **Polypeptide** enters RER lumen → folded & partially glycosylated → **Transport vesicle** → **Golgi apparatus** (cis face) → modified & sorted → **Golgi vesicle** (trans face) → shipped to:
- **Plasma membrane** (secretory proteins or membrane proteins)
- **Lysosomes** (hydrolytic enzymes)
- **Outside the cell** (secretion)
</Callout>

### Membership Criteria – ORIGIN, NOT Function

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**CRITICAL EXAM CONCEPT**: An organelle is part of the endomembrane system based on its **ORIGIN** (where it came from physically), NOT its function. To be a member, the organelle must have **originated from the ER, the Golgi, or be the nuclear envelope** itself.

**Mitochondria** are NOT part of the endomembrane system – they originated from **endosymbiosis** (an engulfed bacterium), not from the ER/Golgi.

**Chloroplasts** are NOT part of the endomembrane system – same reason as mitochondria.

**Ribosomes** are NOT part of the endomembrane system – they originate from the **nucleolus**, not from the ER/Golgi.
</Callout>

## Endosymbiotic Theory – How Eukaryotes Evolved

<Definition term="Endosymbiotic Theory (نظرية التعايش الداخلي)">
The theory that **mitochondria** and **chloroplasts** were once **free-living prokaryotes** that were engulfed by a larger ancestral cell. Over time, a **mutualistic relationship** developed, and the engulfed prokaryotes became permanent organelles.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/Endosymbiosis.svg/500px-Endosymbiosis.svg.png" alt="Diagram showing the endosymbiotic theory – a large cell engulfing a smaller prokaryote that becomes mitochondria or chloroplast" caption="Endosymbiotic Theory – a large prokaryotic cell engulfed smaller prokaryotes, which evolved into mitochondria and chloroplasts" width="420px" />

### How It Happened – Mitochondria

1. A **large prokaryotic cell** (could make food but could NOT make energy efficiently) **engulfed** a **small aerobic prokaryote** (could perform aerobic respiration on its inner plasma membrane)
2. Instead of digesting it, a **mutualistic relationship** formed:
   - The small prokaryote got **shelter and nutrients** from the large cell
   - The large cell got **ATP (energy)** from the small prokaryote's respiration
3. Over billions of years, the small prokaryote became a **permanent component** → evolved into the **mitochondrion**

### Evidence for Endosymbiosis

| Evidence | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Double membrane** | The inner membrane = original prokaryote's membrane; outer membrane = the host's engulfing membrane |
| **Own circular DNA** | Like bacterial DNA (not linear like nuclear DNA) |
| **Own 70S ribosomes** | Same size as bacterial ribosomes (not 80S like other eukaryotic ribosomes) |
| **Binary fission** | Mitochondria and chloroplasts divide by binary fission – just like bacteria |
| **Size** | Similar in size to bacteria |
| **Antibiotic sensitivity** | Antibiotics that kill bacteria also affect mitochondria/chloroplasts (because of similar ribosomes) |

<Callout type="info" emoji="🌿">
The same process explains **chloroplasts**: a large cell engulfed a **photosynthetic cyanobacterium** → the cyanobacterium became the chloroplast. This is why chloroplasts also have double membranes, own DNA, 70S ribosomes, and can self-replicate.
</Callout>

## Plant vs Animal Cell – The Complete Comparison

This is one of the most-tested topics in STEM Biology exams. Study this table carefully:

| Feature | Animal Cell | Plant Cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shape** | Round/irregular | Rectangular/fixed (due to cell wall) |
| **Cell wall** | **Absent** | **Present** (made of cellulose) |
| **Chloroplasts** | **Absent** | **Present** (photosynthesis) |
| **Central vacuole** | Small/absent | **Large** (up to 90% of cell volume) |
| **Centrosomes** | **Present** | Generally **absent** |
| **Lysosomes** | **Present** and prominent | Rare (vacuoles serve similar function) |
| **Plasma membrane** | Present | Present (inside the cell wall) |
| **Nucleus** | Present | Present |
| **Mitochondria** | Present | Present |
| **ER (RER & SER)** | Present | Present |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Present | Present |
| **Ribosomes** | Present (80S) | Present (80S) |
| **Cytoskeleton** | Present (with centrosomes) | Present (without centrosomes) |
| **Storage molecule** | **Glycogen** | **Starch** granules |
| **DNA location** | Nucleus + Mitochondria | Nucleus + Mitochondria + **Chloroplast** |

<Callout type="default" emoji="📌">
**Memory aids:**
- Both starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) are **polymers of glucose** – different storage forms of the same sugar
- **Cellulose** is also a polymer of glucose, but it serves a **structural** function (cell wall), NOT storage
- **Plants have 3 locations for DNA**: nucleus, mitochondria, AND chloroplasts
- **Animals have 2 locations for DNA**: nucleus and mitochondria only
</Callout>

### What About Cell Walls?

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧱">

**Cell wall present in:**
- All **prokaryotes** (bacteria and archaea)
- **Plants** (cellulose)
- **Algae** (cellulose)
- **Fungi** (chitin)

**Cell wall ABSENT in:**
- **Animal** cells
- Most **protists** (e.g., amoeba)

The cell wall provides **rigidity** and **protection** – this is why plant cells are rectangular and maintain their shape even when the cell membrane shrinks (plasmolysis). Animal cells, lacking a cell wall, are flexible and can change shape.
</Callout>

## The SA:V Ratio – Why Organisms Are Multicellular

<Definition term="Surface Area to Volume Ratio (SA:V)">
As a cell **grows larger**, its volume increases **faster** than its surface area. This means the cell membrane (surface) becomes insufficient to transport enough nutrients in and waste out for the increasing volume of cytoplasm. This is why cells stay small and organisms become **multicellular** instead of having one giant cell.
</Definition>

<Callout type="default" emoji="📐">
Imagine a cube with side length **1 cm**:
- Surface area = 6 cm², Volume = 1 cm³, SA:V = **6:1**

Now a cube with side length **2 cm**:
- Surface area = 24 cm², Volume = 8 cm³, SA:V = **3:1**

The SA:V ratio **decreased** – meaning less surface area per unit of volume. The larger cell would struggle to get enough nutrients and expel enough waste through its membrane. **Solution?** Divide into many smaller cells (→ multicellularity) or have highly folded membranes (→ cristae in mitochondria, microvilli in intestines).
</Callout>

## Tuberculosis (TB) – The Case Study

<Callout type="info" emoji="🦠">
TB is the real-world disease that connects cell biology to medicine. Everything you've learned about lysosomes, macrophages, and the immune system comes together here. TB is one of the **oldest** and most **deadly** diseases in human history – traces have been found in Egyptian mummies over 3,000 years old.
</Callout>

### What is Tuberculosis?

<Definition term="Tuberculosis (السُل)">
A **chronic infectious disease** caused by the bacterium ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis***. Primarily attacks the **lungs** (pulmonary TB) but can spread to other organs (extrapulmonary TB). TB is the #1 infectious disease killer worldwide – killing approximately **3 million people per year**.
</Definition>

### The Pathogen

<Definition term="Mycobacterium tuberculosis (المتفطرة السلية)">
The **causative agent** of TB. A rod-shaped **bacterium** (prokaryote) with an unusually thick, waxy cell wall containing **mycolic acids** – making it extremely resistant to drying, disinfectants, and many antibiotics. This waxy coat also prevents lysosomes from effectively killing it.
</Definition>

### Mode of Transmission

TB spreads through **inhalation** (airborne transmission) – breathing in tiny respiratory droplets containing the bacteria when an infected person **coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings**.

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
This is different from malaria (which spreads via a vector – the mosquito). TB spreads **directly from person to person** through the air. One infected person in a crowded room can expose everyone nearby.
</Callout>

### What Happens After Infection – Two Scenarios

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/Tuberculosis_symptoms.svg/500px-Tuberculosis_symptoms.svg.png" alt="Diagram showing the main symptoms of tuberculosis including cough, fever, night sweats, and weight loss" caption="Main symptoms of tuberculosis – chronic cough, fever, night sweats, and weight loss are hallmark signs" width="420px" />

When you inhale *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, your immune system responds by sending **macrophages** (a type of white blood cell) to the lungs to engulf and destroy the bacteria. What happens next depends on the strength of your immune system:

<Tabs items={["💪 Strong Immunity", "😷 Weak Immunity"]}>
<Tab label="💪 Strong Immunity">

### Latent TB Infection (عدوى سُل كامنة)

If your immune system is **strong**:

1. Macrophages arrive and **engulf** the TB bacteria (phagocytosis)
2. Lysosomes inside the macrophages release **hydrolytic enzymes**
3. But the TB bacteria's **waxy cell wall** resists digestion – the bacteria survive **inside the macrophages**
4. The macrophages **cannot kill** the bacteria but successfully **imprison** them
5. The bacteria remain alive but dormant – **trapped inside macrophages**

This is called a **latent infection** (عدوى كامنة) – the pathogen is present but "imprisoned." The person:
- Has **no symptoms**
- Is **not contagious**
- Can live their **entire life** with latent TB and never develop the disease
- May not even know they're infected

<Callout type="default" emoji="🔒">
Think of it as a **prison** – the bacteria are alive but locked up. As long as the guards (immune system) stay strong, the prisoner stays contained. But if the guards weaken...
</Callout>

</Tab>
<Tab label="😷 Weak Immunity">

### Active TB Infection (عدوى سُل نشطة)

If your immune system is **weak** (due to HIV/AIDS, diabetes, malnutrition, cancer, old age, etc.):

1. Macrophages arrive but **fail** to contain the bacteria
2. The TB bacteria **escape** the immune system's defences
3. The bacteria **multiply freely** in the lungs
4. **Active infection** develops immediately → **TB disease**

The person develops symptoms and becomes **contagious**.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### From Latent to Active – When the Prisoner Escapes

A person with latent TB can develop **active TB** later in life if their immune system weakens. The bacteria "escape" from the macrophages and begin multiplying.

**Risk factors that convert latent TB → active TB:**

| Risk Factor | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **HIV/AIDS** | Destroys the immune system. People with HIV are **50 times** more likely to develop active TB |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Weakens immune response |
| **Silicosis** | Occupational lung disease that damages lung tissue |
| **Immunosuppressive drugs** | Medications that deliberately weaken the immune system (e.g., after organ transplant) |
| **Old age** | Natural decline in immune function |
| **Malnutrition** | Weakened immune system due to lack of proper nutrition |
| **Cancer** | Both the disease and chemotherapy weaken immunity |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**CRITICAL EXAM CONCEPT**: An HIV-positive person is **50 times** more likely to develop active TB than an HIV-negative person. AIDS = Acquired Immuno**deficiency** Syndrome – the word "deficiency" tells you the immune system is weakened. The risk of converting latent → active TB is **highest in the first 2 years** after initial infection, then gradually decreases (but never reaches zero).
</Callout>

### Key Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary TB** | The initial infection – may be latent (contained) or active (immediate disease) |
| **Post-primary TB** (Secondary/Reactivation TB) | When latent TB **reactivates** into active TB – the bacteria escape from macrophages |
| **Cavitary TB** | Active TB that causes **cavities** (holes) in the lung tissue where bacteria escaped from macrophages – visible on **chest X-ray** |
| **Fibro-cavitary lung lesion** | The characteristic X-ray finding of post-primary TB – cavities surrounded by fibrous tissue |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📸">
**Cavitation** (formation of cavities in the lungs) is the hallmark of **post-primary TB** (reactivation from latent to active). It occurs at the site where bacteria escape from macrophages. If an exam asks: "Fibro-cavitary lesion on X-ray is a feature of ___?" → The answer is **post-primary (secondary) TB**, NOT primary TB.
</Callout>

### One-Way Street: Latent → Active (Never Backwards)

<Callout type="warning" emoji="🚫">
**Latent infection CAN become active infection** (if immunity drops). But **active infection CANNOT revert back to latent infection** – once the bacteria are free and multiplying, they can't be "re-imprisoned." Treatment with antibiotics is required.

Pathway: TB Bacteria → Infection → either **Latent** (no disease) or **Active** (TB disease)
- Latent → Active ✅ (possible, if immunity weakens)
- Active → Latent ❌ (impossible)
</Callout>

### Symptoms of Active TB

- **Chronic cough** (lasting more than 3 weeks, often with blood)
- **Night sweats** (sweating while sleeping)
- **Weight loss** and **loss of appetite**
- **Fatigue** and general weakness
- **Fever** (low-grade, persistent)
- **Chest pain**

### Diagnosis

| Method | Details |
| --- | --- |
| **Tuberculin skin test** (TST) | Injection of tuberculin protein under the skin. If **hypersensitivity** (swelling) develops → positive → person has TB bacteria. But this test **cannot distinguish between latent and active TB**. |
| **Chest X-ray** | Can reveal **cavitary lesions** (cavities in the lungs) characteristic of active or post-primary TB |
| **Sputum microscopy** | Direct examination of sputum (phlegm) under a microscope to find TB bacteria |

### Treatment – Antibiotics

Since TB is caused by a **bacterium**, it is treated with **antibiotics**. Two key antibiotics:

| Antibiotic | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Isoniazid** (أيزونيازيد) | Interferes with the bacteria's ability to **make their cell wall** → bacteria cannot maintain structural integrity → die |
| **Rifampicin** (ريفامبيسين) | Prevents the bacteria from **making proteins** (inhibits RNA polymerase) → bacteria cannot function → die |

- Treatment takes **3–6 months** of continuous antibiotic therapy
- **Key problem**: With prolonged use, TB bacteria can develop **antibiotic resistance** (just like mosquitoes developed resistance to DDT)
- This is why patients must **complete the full course** of antibiotics – stopping early allows resistant bacteria to survive and multiply

<Callout type="info" emoji="💊">
**Why can't you use an antibiotic that "disrupts the nuclear envelope"?** Because TB is caused by a **bacterium** – a prokaryotic cell that **has no nuclear envelope**! Any antibiotic targeting the nuclear envelope would be useless against bacteria. Exam writers love to test this connection between cell biology and medicine.
</Callout>

### TB vs Malaria – Comparison

| Feature | Tuberculosis | Malaria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Causative agent** | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (bacterium) | *Plasmodium* species (protozoan parasite) |
| **Mode of transmission** | **Inhalation** (airborne) | **Vector** (female *Anopheles* mosquito bite) or blood transfusion |
| **Target organ** | **Lungs** (primarily) | **Liver** (incubation) → **Red blood cells** (disease) |
| **Type of infection** | Can be **latent** or **active** | Always **active** (no latent form) |
| **Treatment** | **Antibiotics** (isoniazid, rifampicin) | **Antimalarial drugs** (quinine, chloroquine, artemisinin) |
| **Classification** | Endemic infectious disease | Endemic infectious disease |
| **Resistance problem** | Antibiotic-resistant TB (MDR-TB) | DDT-resistant mosquitoes |

## Sickle Cell Anaemia & Malaria Resistance

<Callout type="info" emoji="🩸">
This is a fascinating connection between genetics and infectious disease that ties back to cell structure.
</Callout>

<Definition term="Sickle Cell Anaemia (فقر الدم المنجلي)">
A **genetic disease** where red blood cells take on an abnormal **sickle (crescent) shape** instead of the normal biconcave disc shape, due to a mutation in the **haemoglobin** protein.
</Definition>

### The Malaria Connection

People with **sickle cell anaemia** have natural **resistance to malaria**. Why?

1. The Plasmodium **merozoites** (the form that attacks RBCs) cannot effectively invade **sickle-shaped** red blood cells
2. The abnormal shape of the RBCs makes them inhospitable to the parasite
3. This is why sickle cell trait is most common in **malaria-endemic regions** of Africa – natural selection favoured carriers who survived malaria

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧬">
This is a remarkable example of how one **disease** (sickle cell) can provide **protection** against another disease (malaria) – and how **cell structure** (the shape of RBCs) directly affects **cell function** (whether a parasite can invade).
</Callout>

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms – LO 2">
<Term word="Prokaryotic Cell (خلية بدائية النواة)">A cell that lacks a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. DNA floats in the cytoplasm (nucleoid region). Examples: bacteria, archaea.</Term>
<Term word="Eukaryotic Cell (خلية حقيقية النواة)">A cell that has a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Examples: animals, plants, fungi, protists.</Term>
<Term word="Nucleus (النواة)">The largest organelle; control centre of the cell. Contains DNA organised into chromosomes. Enclosed by a double membrane (nuclear envelope).</Term>
<Term word="Nuclear Envelope (الغلاف النووي)">A double membrane surrounding the nucleus, continuous with the RER, perforated by nuclear pores.</Term>
<Term word="Nuclear Pore (الثقب النووي)">Channel in the nuclear envelope that regulates molecular traffic – mRNA exits; proteins enter.</Term>
<Term word="Chromatin (الكروماتين)">DNA + histone proteins inside the nucleus. Euchromatin = active DNA; Heterochromatin = inactive DNA.</Term>
<Term word="Nucleolus (النُوَيّة)">Dense region inside the nucleus that produces ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and assembles ribosome subunits. A ribosome factory.</Term>
<Term word="Ribosome (الريبوسوم)">Non-membranous organelle for protein synthesis. 70S in prokaryotes; 80S in eukaryotes. Found in all cells.</Term>
<Term word="Rough ER (الشبكة الإندوبلازمية الخشنة)">ER studded with ribosomes. Synthesises membrane proteins, secretory proteins, and lysosomal enzymes. Continuous with nuclear envelope.</Term>
<Term word="Smooth ER (الشبكة الإندوبلازمية الملساء)">ER without ribosomes. Synthesises lipids, steroids; detoxifies substances; stores calcium in muscle cells.</Term>
<Term word="Golgi Apparatus (جهاز جولجي)">Stacked membrane sacs that receive, modify, sort, and ship proteins/lipids. Cis face receives; trans face ships.</Term>
<Term word="Glycosylation (الغلوزة)">Addition of carbohydrate (CHO) groups to proteins, producing glycoproteins. Begins in RER, completed in Golgi.</Term>
<Term word="Mitochondria (الميتوكوندريا)">Double-membraned organelles producing ATP via cellular respiration. Have own DNA and 70S ribosomes. 'Powerhouse of the cell.'</Term>
<Term word="Cristae (الأعراف)">The inner membrane folds of mitochondria. Increase surface area for ATP production.</Term>
<Term word="Matrix (المصفوفة)">The innermost compartment of mitochondria containing enzymes for the Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA, and ribosomes.</Term>
<Term word="Chloroplast (البلاستيدة الخضراء)">Double-membraned organelle in plant cells/algae that performs photosynthesis. Has own DNA and 70S ribosomes.</Term>
<Term word="Thylakoid">Flattened membrane sacs inside chloroplasts where light reactions of photosynthesis occur.</Term>
<Term word="Granum (plural: Grana)">A stack of thylakoids inside a chloroplast, like a stack of coins.</Term>
<Term word="Stroma">Fluid-filled space in the chloroplast between grana; site of the Calvin cycle (dark reactions).</Term>
<Term word="Chromoplast (البلاستيدة الملونة)">Plastid containing carotenoid pigments (red, orange, yellow). Attracts pollinators. No photosynthesis.</Term>
<Term word="Leucoplast (البلاستيدة عديمة اللون)">Colourless plastid for storage (starch, lipids, proteins). Found in roots, tubers. No pigments.</Term>
<Term word="Lysosome (الجسم الحالّ)">Membrane-bound organelle containing hydrolytic enzymes (pH ≈ 2). Digests waste, pathogens, and old organelles.</Term>
<Term word="Peroxisome (البيروكسيسوم)">Organelle that breaks down fatty acids and detoxifies substances. Produces H₂O₂, then neutralises it with catalase.</Term>
<Term word="Vacuole (الفجوة)">Membrane-bound sac for storage. Plants have one large central vacuole (tonoplast membrane); animals have small ones.</Term>
<Term word="Tonoplast">The membrane enclosing the plant cell's central vacuole.</Term>
<Term word="Cytoskeleton (الهيكل الخلوي)">Network of protein filaments providing structural support, enabling movement, and facilitating cell division.</Term>
<Term word="Microfilaments (الخيوط الدقيقة)">Thinnest cytoskeletal filaments made of actin. Involved in cleavage furrow, cytoplasmic streaming, and cell shape.</Term>
<Term word="Microtubules (الأنيبيبات الدقيقة)">Thickest cytoskeletal filaments made of tubulin. Form spindle fibres, cilia, flagella, and centrosomes.</Term>
<Term word="Centrosome (المريكز)">Microtubule-organising centre in animal cells. Organises the mitotic spindle during cell division. Absent in plant cells.</Term>
<Term word="Endomembrane System (نظام الأغشية الداخلية)">Interconnected organelles (nuclear envelope, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vesicles, plasma membrane) for protein/lipid synthesis and transport.</Term>
<Term word="Endosymbiotic Theory (نظرية التعايش الداخلي)">Theory that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from engulfed prokaryotes. Evidence: double membrane, own DNA, 70S ribosomes, binary fission.</Term>
<Term word="Cell Wall (الجدار الخلوي)">Rigid outer layer providing shape and protection. Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan (bacteria).</Term>
<Term word="Plasma Membrane (غشاء البلازما)">Selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer surrounding all cells. Controls what enters and exits.</Term>
<Term word="Phospholipid Bilayer">Two layers of phospholipids forming the membrane: hydrophilic heads face outward; hydrophobic tails face inward.</Term>
<Term word="Selectively Permeable (شبه نفاذ)">Property of the plasma membrane allowing only certain molecules to pass through while blocking others.</Term>
<Term word="SA:V Ratio (نسبة المساحة إلى الحجم)">Surface area to volume ratio. Explains why cells are small and organisms are multicellular: volume increases faster than surface area as size increases.</Term>
<Term word="Starch (النشا)">Storage polysaccharide in plant cells – polymer of glucose stored in chloroplasts and leucoplasts.</Term>
<Term word="Glycogen (الجلايكوجين)">Storage polysaccharide in animal cells – polymer of glucose stored in liver and muscle cells.</Term>
<Term word="Cellulose (السليلوز)">Structural polysaccharide making up plant cell walls. Polymer of glucose but used for structure, NOT storage.</Term>
<Term word="Tuberculosis – TB (السُل)">Chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Transmitted by inhalation. Attacks lungs primarily.</Term>
<Term word="Mycobacterium tuberculosis">The bacterium causing TB. Has a thick waxy cell wall (mycolic acids) resistant to lysosomes and many antibiotics.</Term>
<Term word="Latent Infection (عدوى كامنة)">TB bacteria imprisoned inside macrophages – alive but dormant. No symptoms, not contagious. Can last a lifetime.</Term>
<Term word="Active Infection (عدوى نشطة)">TB bacteria free and multiplying in the lungs – causes symptoms, patient is contagious. Can follow weak immunity or escape from latent state.</Term>
<Term word="Cavitation (التجويف)">Formation of cavities (holes) in lung tissue where TB bacteria escaped from macrophages. Feature of post-primary TB. Visible on chest X-ray.</Term>
<Term word="Post-Primary TB (السُل التالي)">Reactivation of latent TB into active disease. Characterised by cavitary lung lesions. Also called secondary TB.</Term>
<Term word="Isoniazid">Antibiotic that interferes with TB bacteria's ability to make cell walls.</Term>
<Term word="Rifampicin">Antibiotic that prevents TB bacteria from making proteins (inhibits RNA polymerase).</Term>
<Term word="Tay-Sachs Disease (مرض تاي-ساكس)">Genetic disorder where lysosomes lack lipase enzyme → lipid accumulation in brain → neurological damage → fatal.</Term>
<Term word="Sickle Cell Anaemia (فقر الدم المنجلي)">Genetic disease causing abnormal sickle-shaped RBCs. Provides resistance to malaria because merozoites cannot invade sickle-shaped cells.</Term>
<Term word="Antibiotic Resistance (مقاومة المضادات الحيوية)">Bacteria evolving to survive antibiotics through natural selection. Occurs with overuse/incomplete courses of antibiotics.</Term>
<Term word="Phagocytosis (البلعمة)">Process where macrophages engulf and attempt to destroy pathogens using lysosomes.</Term>
<Term word="Apoptosis (الموت المبرمج للخلية)">Programmed cell death – lysosomes release enzymes to destroy the cell when it is damaged beyond repair or infected.</Term>
<Term word="Autophagy">Process where lysosomes digest the cell's own damaged or old organelles for recycling.</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic**: Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound nucleus and organelles; eukaryotes have both. All prokaryotes are unicellular; eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular.</Point>
<Point>**Four universal cell components**: DNA, cytoplasm, plasma membrane, and ribosomes – found in ALL cells.</Point>
<Point>**Nucleus** contains chromatin (DNA + histones), nucleolus (ribosome factory), and is enclosed by a double membrane with nuclear pores.</Point>
<Point>**RER** = ribosomes attached, makes proteins (membrane, secretory, lysosomal). **SER** = no ribosomes, makes lipids, detoxifies, stores calcium.</Point>
<Point>**Golgi** receives from ER (cis face), modifies (glycosylation + phosphorylation), sorts, and ships (trans face). Controls membrane protein:lipid ratio.</Point>
<Point>**Mitochondria** = powerhouse (ATP via cellular respiration). Double membrane, own DNA, own 70S ribosomes, self-replicate. Abundant in active cells.</Point>
<Point>**Chloroplasts** = solar panels (photosynthesis: solar → chemical energy). Double membrane, own DNA, own 70S ribosomes. Only in plants and algae.</Point>
<Point>**Three plastid types**: Chloroplast (photosynthesis), Chromoplast (colour/pollination), Leucoplast (storage, no pigment).</Point>
<Point>**Lysosomes** = digestive system (pH ≈ 2, hydrolytic enzymes). Made by RER → Golgi. Perform phagocytosis, autophagy, apoptosis. Tay-Sachs = lysosome defect.</Point>
<Point>**Endomembrane system membership** = based on ORIGIN (from ER/Golgi), NOT function. Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and ribosomes are NOT members.</Point>
<Point>**Endosymbiotic Theory**: Mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from engulfed prokaryotes. Evidence: double membrane, own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, binary fission.</Point>
<Point>**Plant vs Animal**: Plants have cell wall + chloroplasts + large central vacuole + starch storage. Animals have centrosomes + lysosomes + glycogen storage.</Point>
<Point>**SA:V ratio** explains why cells stay small and organisms are multicellular – volume grows faster than surface area.</Point>
<Point>**TB** = chronic infectious disease by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. Spreads by inhalation. Strong immunity → latent (bacteria imprisoned in macrophages). Weak immunity → active disease.</Point>
<Point>**Latent → Active TB** triggered by HIV/AIDS (50× risk), diabetes, silicosis, immunosuppression. Causes cavitation visible on X-ray (post-primary TB).</Point>
<Point>**TB treatment** = antibiotics (isoniazid for cell wall, rifampicin for protein synthesis). 3–6 months. Bacteria can develop resistance.</Point>
<Point>**Sickle cell anaemia** provides resistance to malaria – merozoites cannot invade sickle-shaped RBCs.</Point>
<Point>**Antibiotics** work against bacteria, NOT viruses (viruses are non-living). Antibiotics are selectively toxic – they target bacterial structures absent in human cells.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
