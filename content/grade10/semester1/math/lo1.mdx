## 🏛️ Etymology & Origins: The Language of Triangles

The word "Trigonometry" comes from the Greek words *trigōnon* (triangle) and *metron* (measure). It is literally the science of measuring triangles. But the beauty of trigonometry lies in its interconnectedness. 

Consider the "Cosine". The prefix "co-" is not random. It stands for "complement's sine". In any right triangle, the two acute angles are complementary (they add up to $90^\circ$). The sine of an angle is exactly equal to the cosine of its complement.

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: Complementary Angle Identities">
  $$\sin \theta = \cos(90^\circ - \theta)$$
  $$\cos \theta = \sin(90^\circ - \theta)$$
  $$\tan \theta = \cot(90^\circ - \theta)$$
</Formula>

This explains why $\sin 30^\circ = \cos 60^\circ$. They are two sides of the exact same geometric coin.

## 📐 Part 1: Mastering the Right Triangle & Pythagorean Theorem

### 1.1 Anatomy of a Right Triangle

<Definition term="Right Triangle (مثلث قائم الزاوية)">
  A triangle that contains exactly one **90° angle** (right angle). The side opposite the right angle is always the longest side, called the **hypotenuse**.
</Definition>

Every right triangle has three sides with special names, and which side is "opposite" or "adjacent" **depends on the angle you're looking at** (we call it $\theta$):

<RightTriangle hypotenuse="Hypotenuse (الوتر)" opposite="Opposite (المقابل)" adjacent="Adjacent (المجاور)" angle="θ" orientation="right" />

Let's compare these three crucial sides to see how they behave depending on your perspective:

<Compare>
  <Item label="Hypotenuse (الوتر)">
    - Always **opposite the 90° angle**
    - Always the **longest** side of the triangle
    - **Never changes** regardless of which acute angle you pick
  </Item>
  <Item label="Opposite (المقابل)">
    - The side **directly across** from angle $\theta$
    - Changes when you switch which angle you're examining
  </Item>
  <Item label="Adjacent (المجاور)">
    - The side **next to** angle $\theta$ (that isn't the hypotenuse)
    - Changes when you switch which angle you're examining
  </Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Memory Trick:** Stand at angle $\theta$. The side **facing you** = Opposite. The side **beside you** = Adjacent. The **longest** side = Hypotenuse. Always.
</Callout>

### 1.2 The Pythagorean Theorem

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: Pythagorean Theorem (نظرية فيثاغورس)">
  $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

  Where $c$ is **always** the hypotenuse, and $a$, $b$ are the other two sides (the legs).
</Formula>

<Example title="Finding the Hypotenuse">
  A right triangle has legs of length **3** and **4**. Find the hypotenuse.

  $$3^2 + 4^2 = c^2$$
  $$9 + 16 = 25$$
  $$c = \sqrt{25} = 5$$
</Example>

<Example title="Finding a Missing Leg">
  A right triangle has a hypotenuse of **13** and one leg of **5**. Find the other leg.

  $$5^2 + b^2 = 13^2$$
  $$25 + b^2 = 169$$
  $$b^2 = 144$$
  $$b = \sqrt{144} = 12$$
</Example>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧠">
  **Pythagorean Triples**: Memorize these sets of whole numbers that satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. They save you time in exams!

  | Triple | × 2 | × 3 |
  |--------|-----|-----|
  | **3, 4, 5** | 6, 8, 10 | 9, 12, 15 |
  | **5, 12, 13** | 10, 24, 26 | 15, 36, 39 |
  | **8, 15, 17** | 16, 30, 34 | 24, 45, 51 |
  | **7, 24, 25** | 14, 48, 50 | 21, 72, 75 |

  Any whole-number multiple of a Pythagorean triple is also a Pythagorean triple!
</Callout>

### 1.3 The Euclidean Theorem (Geometric Mean Theorems)

When you drop an altitude from the right angle of a right triangle to its hypotenuse, you create three similar triangles. This unlocks powerful proportional relationships known as the Euclidean Theorem.

Let $\triangle ABC$ be a right triangle at $A$, and let $AD$ be the altitude drawn to the hypotenuse $BC$.

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: The Euclidean Theorem">
  **The Altitude Rule:** The altitude squared is the product of the two segments of the hypotenuse.
  $$AD^2 = BD \times CD$$

  **The Leg Rule:** Each leg squared is the product of the adjacent hypotenuse segment and the entire hypotenuse.
  $$AB^2 = BD \times BC$$
  $$AC^2 = CD \times BC$$
</Formula>

These formulas provide a brilliant shortcut for finding missing lengths without calculating any angles.

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="🧠 For Curious Minds: Why do these rules work?">
    When you drop the altitude $AD$, it cuts the large triangle into two smaller triangles that are **similar** to each other and to the original large triangle. Because their angles match exactly, their sides are proportional. Setting up a cross-multiplication of those side ratios gives you these exact formulas!
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

## 🔑 Part 2: Unlocking the Three Basic Ratios (SOH-CAH-TOA)

### 2.1 Sine, Cosine & Tangent

The three fundamental trigonometric ratios connect the **sides** of a right triangle to its **angles**:

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: The Big Three: SOH-CAH-TOA">
  $$\sin \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} \qquad \cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} \qquad \tan \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}$$
</Formula>

To memorize these formulas easily, mathematicians created a famous mnemonic that you can rely on during exams:

| Function | Mnemonic | Ratio | Memory Sentence |
|----------|----------|-------|-----------------|
| **S**ine | **S**OH | **O**pp / **H**yp | **S**ome **O**ld **H**orses |
| **C**osine | **C**AH | **A**dj / **H**yp | **C**an **A**lways **H**ear |
| **T**angent | **T**OA | **O**pp / **A**dj | **T**heir **O**wner **A**pproaching |

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧠">
  **SOH-CAH-TOA**: Say it out loud: "So-Cah-Toe-Ah." Once it's stuck in your head, you'll never forget which ratio is which.
</Callout>

<Example title="Calculating All Three Ratios">
  <RightTriangle hypotenuse="5" opposite="4 ← Opposite" adjacent="3 (Adjacent)" angle="θ" orientation="right" />

  $$\sin \theta = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 \qquad \cos \theta = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6 \qquad \tan \theta = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$$
</Example>

### 2.2 Finding Missing Sides Using Trig Ratios

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify what you know">
    Label the sides relative to the given angle: which is Opposite, Adjacent, Hypotenuse?
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose the right ratio">
    Pick the trig function that connects your **known side** to your **unknown side**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set up the equation">
    Write the ratio and substitute your known values.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Solve algebraically">
    Isolate the unknown variable and calculate.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tabs items={["Example: Using Tangent", "Example: Using Cosine", "Example: Using Sine"]}>
  <Tab label="Example: Using Tangent">
    A right triangle has an angle of **38°** and the adjacent side is **42**. Find the opposite side $x$.

    We have Adjacent and need Opposite → use **Tangent**:

    $$\tan 38^\circ = \frac{x}{42}$$

    $$x = 42 \times \tan 38^\circ = 42 \times 0.7813 \approx 32.81$$
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Example: Using Cosine">
    A right triangle has an angle of **54°** and the hypotenuse is **26**. Find the adjacent side $x$.

    We have Hypotenuse and need Adjacent → use **Cosine**:

    $$\cos 54^\circ = \frac{x}{26}$$

    $$x = 26 \times \cos 54^\circ = 26 \times 0.5878 \approx 15.28$$
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="Example: Using Sine">
    A right triangle has an angle of **30°** and the hypotenuse is **10**. Find the opposite side $x$.

    We have Hypotenuse and need Opposite → use **Sine**:

    $$\sin 30^\circ = \frac{x}{10}$$

    $$x = 10 \times \sin 30^\circ = 10 \times 0.5 = 5$$
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

### 2.3 Finding Missing Angles: Inverse Trig Functions

<Definition term="Inverse Trigonometric Functions (الدوال المثلثية العكسية)">
  When you know the ratio of two sides and need to find the **angle**, use the **inverse** (arc) functions:
  - $\theta = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{Opp}}{\text{Hyp}}\right)$, also written $\arcsin$
  - $\theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{Adj}}{\text{Hyp}}\right)$, also written $\arccos$
  - $\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\text{Opp}}{\text{Adj}}\right)$, also written $\arctan$
</Definition>

<Example title="Finding a Missing Angle">
  In a right triangle, the opposite side is **5** and the adjacent side is **4**. Find $\theta$.

  $$\tan \theta = \frac{5}{4} = 1.25$$

  $$\theta = \tan^{-1}(1.25) = 51.34^\circ$$
</Example>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **CALCULATOR CHECK:** Always make sure your calculator is in **DEGREE mode** (not radians) when solving these problems! Look for "DEG" on your screen. If you see "RAD", switch it before calculating.
</Callout>

<Callout type="success" emoji="✅">
  **Checkpoint: What You Know So Far**
  - You know how to identify the Hypotenuse, Opposite, and Adjacent sides.
  - You've mastered the Pythagorean Theorem for finding missing sides.
  - You've unlocked the magic of SOH-CAH-TOA to connect angles and ratios.

  <details>
    <summary>🧠 Quick Mental Check</summary>

  </details>
</Callout>

## 🧩 Part 3: Discovering the Six Trigonometric Functions

### 3.1 Meet the Full Family

Beyond the Big Three (sin, cos, tan), there are three more functions, each is simply the **reciprocal** (flip) of one of the originals:

<Tabs items={["The Primary Three", "The Reciprocal Three", "All Six Together"]}>
  <Tab label="The Primary Three">
    | Function | Symbol | Ratio |
    |----------|--------|-------|
    | Sine | $\sin \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$ |
    | Cosine | $\cos \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}$ |
    | Tangent | $\tan \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}$ |

    These are the **building blocks**. Master these first.
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="The Reciprocal Three">
    | Function | Symbol | Ratio | Reciprocal of |
    |----------|--------|-------|---------------|
    | Cosecant | $\csc \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Opposite}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\sin \theta}$ |
    | Secant | $\sec \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Adjacent}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\cos \theta}$ |
    | Cotangent | $\cot \theta$ | $\dfrac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Opposite}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\tan \theta}$ |

    **Watch out!** The names are deceptive: **Co**secant is the reciprocal of **S**ine (not cosine!).
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="All Six Together">
    <RightTriangle hypotenuse="hyp" opposite="opp" adjacent="adj" angle="θ" orientation="right" />

    | Primary | Formula | Reciprocal | Formula |
    |---------|---------|------------|---------|
    | $\sin \theta$ | $\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$ | $\csc \theta$ | $\frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{opp}}$ |
    | $\cos \theta$ | $\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$ | $\sec \theta$ | $\frac{\text{hyp}}{\text{adj}}$ |
    | $\tan \theta$ | $\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$ | $\cot \theta$ | $\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{opp}}$ |

    Notice: each pair has the **same two numbers, just flipped**!
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

Learning these new functions can be confusing at first, especially because of the tricky names. Here is a helpful tip to keep them straight:

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Memory Trick: The "co-" Crossover:**
  - **Co**secant flips **S**ine ← the names are **crossed**!
  - **S**ecant flips **Co**sine ← also crossed!
  - **Co**tangent flips **T**angent ← this one is straightforward

  The "co-" prefix and the function it flips **never match** (except cotangent).
</Callout>

<Example title="All Six Functions: Complete Example">
  Triangle with sides **5**, **12**, **13** where $\theta$ is opposite the side of length **12**:

  - Opposite = 12 &nbsp;&nbsp; Adjacent = 5 &nbsp;&nbsp; Hypotenuse = 13

  | Function | Calculation | Value |
  |----------|-------------|-------|
  | $\sin \theta$ | $12 \div 13$ | $\dfrac{12}{13}$ |
  | $\cos \theta$ | $5 \div 13$ | $\dfrac{5}{13}$ |
  | $\tan \theta$ | $12 \div 5$ | $\dfrac{12}{5}$ |
  | $\csc \theta$ | $13 \div 12$ | $\dfrac{13}{12}$ |
  | $\sec \theta$ | $13 \div 5$ | $\dfrac{13}{5}$ |
  | $\cot \theta$ | $5 \div 12$ | $\dfrac{5}{12}$ |

  **Pattern:** Once you know the primary three, just flip each fraction to get its reciprocal!
</Example>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **COMMON MISCONCEPTION: Inverse vs. Reciprocal**

  $\sin^{-1}$ is **NOT** the same as $\csc$!

  - $\sin^{-1}(x)$ = inverse sine = "what angle has a sine of $x$?" → gives you an **angle**
  - $\csc \theta = \frac{1}{\sin \theta}$ = reciprocal of sine → gives you a **number**
</Callout>

## ⭕ Part 4: Mastering the Unit Circle

### 4A: What Is the Unit Circle?

<Definition term="Unit Circle (دائرة الوحدة)">
  A circle centered at the origin $(0, 0)$ with a radius of exactly **1**. Every point on this circle can be described using trigonometric functions, making it the single most important tool for understanding trigonometry beyond right triangles.
</Definition>

Take a look at the basic structure of this circle on the coordinate plane:

<UnitCircle showTriangle={false} />

**Why radius = 1?** Because it makes everything beautifully simple. Watch:

### The Golden Connection

<Callout type="default" emoji="🥇">
  **The Golden Rule of the Unit Circle:**

  For any angle $\theta$, draw a ray from the origin making that angle with the positive x-axis. The point where this ray intersects the circle has coordinates:

  $$\boxed{\text{Point on unit circle} = (\cos \theta, \;\; \sin \theta)}$$

  **x-coordinate = $\cos \theta$** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; and &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **y-coordinate = $\sin \theta$**
</Callout>

**Why does this work?** Draw a right triangle inside the unit circle:

<UnitCircle showTriangle={true} pointLabel="(cos θ, sin θ)" />

Since the hypotenuse (radius) = 1:
$$\sin \theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}} = \frac{\text{opp}}{1} = \text{opp} = y$$
$$\cos \theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}} = \frac{\text{adj}}{1} = \text{adj} = x$$

And from these, all six functions on the unit circle:

| Function | Unit Circle Definition |
|----------|----------------------|
| $\sin \theta$ | $y$ |
| $\cos \theta$ | $x$ |
| $\tan \theta$ | $\dfrac{y}{x}$ (where $x \neq 0$) |
| $\csc \theta$ | $\dfrac{1}{y}$ (where $y \neq 0$) |
| $\sec \theta$ | $\dfrac{1}{x}$ (where $x \neq 0$) |
| $\cot \theta$ | $\dfrac{x}{y}$ (where $y \neq 0$) |

<Callout type="default" emoji="⚡">
  **10-Second Recall:** On the unit circle, which coordinate gives you the Cosine of the angle?
  <details>
    <summary>👀 Show Answer</summary>
    The **x-coordinate**.
  </details>
</Callout>

### 4C: The Four Quadrants (Where Signs Live)

<Definition term="Quadrants (الأرباع)">
  The coordinate plane is divided into four regions called quadrants, numbered I through IV starting from the top-right and going counter-clockwise.
</Definition>

Here is how those four quadrants are laid out visually:

<Quadrants />

Depending on which quadrant your angle lands in, the trigonometric functions will be either positive or negative. Here is the complete breakdown:

| Quadrant | Angle Range | What's Positive | What's Negative |
|----------|------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **I** | 0° → 90° | **All** functions | Nothing |
| **II** | 90° → 180° | **Sin** & csc | cos, tan, sec, cot |
| **III** | 180° → 270° | **Tan** & cot | sin, cos, csc, sec |
| **IV** | 270° → 360° | **Cos** & sec | sin, tan, csc, cot |

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧠">

  | **S**tudents | **II** (top-left) | **S**in and its reciprocal csc |
  | **T**ake | **III** (bottom-left) | **T**an and its reciprocal cot |
  | **C**alculus | **IV** (bottom-right) | **C**os and its reciprocal sec |
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **COMMON MISTAKE:** Students sometimes start ASTC from Quadrant II. Remember: **start from Quadrant I** (top-right) and go **counter-clockwise**!
</Callout>

## 🕵️‍♂️ Part 5: Decoding Reference Angles

### 5.1 What Is a Reference Angle?

<Definition term="Reference Angle (الزاوية المرجعية)">
  The **acute angle** (between 0° and 90°) formed between the terminal side of your angle and the **x-axis**. It's the "Quadrant I shadow" of any angle, the simple version that tells you the magnitude of the trig value, before you apply the sign.
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Why do reference angles matter?** Because $\sin 150^\circ$ has the **same numerical value** as $\sin 30^\circ$, the only thing that might change is the **sign** (+ or −). The reference angle gives you the base value; the quadrant gives you the sign.
</Callout>

### 5.2 How to Calculate the Reference Angle

| Quadrant | Angle Range | Reference Angle Formula |
|----------|------------|------------------------|
| **I** | 0° – 90° | $\alpha = \theta$ (already acute!) |
| **II** | 90° – 180° | $\alpha = 180^\circ - \theta$ |
| **III** | 180° – 270° | $\alpha = \theta - 180^\circ$ |
| **IV** | 270° – 360° | $\alpha = 360^\circ - \theta$ |

### 5.3 The Master Method: Three Steps to Any Trig Value

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find the Quadrant">
    Determine which quadrant your angle falls in (I, II, III, or IV).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Calculate the Reference Angle">
    Use the appropriate formula from the table above to find the acute reference angle.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply the Sign">
    Evaluate the trig function at the reference angle (from the famous angles table), then apply **+ or −** based on ASTC ("All Students Take Calculus").
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tabs items={["sin 150°", "cos 240°", "tan 315°", "sin 330°"]}>
  <Tab label="sin 150°">
    **Step 1:** 150° is in **Quadrant II** (between 90° and 180°)

    **Step 2:** Reference angle = $180^\circ - 150^\circ = 30^\circ$

    **Step 3:** $\sin 30^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$. In QII, sin is **positive** ("Students").

    $$\boxed{\sin 150^\circ = +\frac{1}{2}}$$
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="cos 240°">
    **Step 1:** 240° is in **Quadrant III** (between 180° and 270°)

    **Step 2:** Reference angle = $240^\circ - 180^\circ = 60^\circ$

    **Step 3:** $\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$. In QIII, cos is **negative**.

    $$\boxed{\cos 240^\circ = -\frac{1}{2}}$$
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="tan 315°">
    **Step 1:** 315° is in **Quadrant IV** (between 270° and 360°)

    **Step 2:** Reference angle = $360^\circ - 315^\circ = 45^\circ$

    **Step 3:** $\tan 45^\circ = 1$. In QIV, tan is **negative**.

    $$\boxed{\tan 315^\circ = -1}$$
  </Tab>
  <Tab label="sin 330°">
    **Step 1:** 330° is in **Quadrant IV** (between 270° and 360°)

    **Step 2:** Reference angle = $360^\circ - 330^\circ = 30^\circ$

    **Step 3:** $\sin 30^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$. In QIV, sin is **negative**.

    $$\boxed{\sin 330^\circ = -\frac{1}{2}}$$
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Example title="Using Reference Angles with All Multiples of 45°">
  Since 45° has reference value $\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, we can find values at 135°, 225°, and 315°:

  | Angle | Quadrant | Reference | $\sin$ | $\cos$ |
  |-------|----------|-----------|--------|--------|
  | **45°** | I | 45° | $+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ |
  | **135°** | II | 45° | $+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ |
  | **225°** | III | 45° | $-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ |
  | **315°** | IV | 45° | $-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ |

  Notice the beautiful symmetry! The **numbers** stay the same, only the **signs** change based on the quadrant.
</Example>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🥇">
  **This is the power of reference angles:** If you memorize the values for 30°, 45°, and 60° in Quadrant I, you automatically know the values for **twelve** different angles across all four quadrants. That's the entire unit circle from just three values!
</Callout>

## 🧭 Part 6: Navigating Directed & Co-terminal Angles

### 6.1 Directed Angles

<Definition term="Directed Angle (الزاوية الموجهة)">
  An angle with a specified **direction of rotation** from its initial side to its terminal side. The direction determines whether the angle is positive or negative.
</Definition>

<Compare>
  <Item label="Positive Angle (+) ↺">
    - Rotates **counter-clockwise**
    - This is the **standard** direction
    - Example: $+90^\circ$ goes from the positive x-axis **up** to the positive y-axis
  </Item>
  <Item label="Negative Angle (−) ↻">
    - Rotates **clockwise**
    - Example: $-90^\circ$ goes from the positive x-axis **down** to the negative y-axis
    - $-90^\circ$ lands at the same position as $+270^\circ$
  </Item>
</Compare>

Let's visualize how these positive and negative rotations look on the coordinate plane:

<DirectedAngles />

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Initial Side vs. Terminal Side:**
  - **Initial side** = where the angle starts (always the positive x-axis in standard position)
  - **Terminal side** = where the angle ends after rotation
  - The amount and direction of rotation define the directed angle
</Callout>

### 6.2 Co-terminal Angles

<Definition term="Co-terminal Angles (الزوايا المشتركة في الضلع الانتهائي)">
  Two or more angles that share the **same terminal side**, they end at the exact same position on the unit circle, even though they traveled different amounts or directions to get there.
</Definition>

To find these mathematically equivalent angles, you can use a simple addition or subtraction process:

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: Co-terminal Angle Formula">
  $$\theta_{\text{co-terminal}} = \theta \pm 360^\circ \times n$$

  Where $n$ is any positive integer $(1, 2, 3, \ldots)$

  Adding 360° = one more full counter-clockwise rotation.
  Subtracting 360° = one more full clockwise rotation.
</Formula>

<Example title="Finding Co-terminal Angles">
  | Original Angle | + 360° | − 360° | More examples |
  |---------------|--------|--------|---------------|
  | **30°** | 390° | −330° | 750°, −690° |
  | **120°** | 480° | −240° | 840°, −600° |
  | **−45°** | 315° | −405° | 675°, −765° |
</Example>

### 6.3 Key Skills with Co-terminal Angles

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="🔍 Finding the Smallest Positive Co-terminal Angle">
    Keep **adding 360°** to a negative angle (or **subtracting 360°** from a large positive angle) until your result is between 0° and 360°.

    **Example:** What's the smallest positive co-terminal angle for $-50^\circ$?

    $$-50^\circ + 360^\circ = 310^\circ$$

    Since $0^\circ \leq 310^\circ < 360^\circ$, the answer is **310°**. <svg style={{display:'inline-block'}} width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" strokeWidth="3" strokeLinecap="round" strokeLinejoin="round"><polyline points="20 6 9 17 4 12"></polyline></svg>
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="🔍 Finding the Largest Negative Co-terminal Angle">
    Keep **subtracting 360°** until your result is between $-360^\circ$ and $0^\circ$.

    **Example:** What's the largest negative co-terminal angle for $40^\circ$?

    $$40^\circ - 360^\circ = -320^\circ$$

    Since $-360^\circ < -320^\circ \leq 0^\circ$, the answer is **−320°**. <svg style={{display:'inline-block'}} width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" strokeWidth="3" strokeLinecap="round" strokeLinejoin="round"><polyline points="20 6 9 17 4 12"></polyline></svg>
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="🔍 Checking if Two Angles are Co-terminal">
    Calculate the **difference** between the two angles. If the difference is a **multiple of 360°**, they're co-terminal!

    **Example:** Are $1000^\circ$ and $-80^\circ$ co-terminal?

    $$1000^\circ - (-80^\circ) = 1080^\circ = 3 \times 360^\circ$$ <svg style={{display:'inline-block'}} width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" strokeWidth="3" strokeLinecap="round" strokeLinejoin="round"><polyline points="20 6 9 17 4 12"></polyline></svg>

    **Yes!** The difference is exactly 3 full rotations, so they end at the same position.
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **COMMON MISTAKE:** Students confuse co-terminal angles with reference angles.
  - **Reference angle** = the acute angle to the x-axis (always between 0° and 90°)
  - **Co-terminal angle** = any angle that ends at the same position (can be any value)

  For example: 30° and 390° are **co-terminal** (same terminal side), but 30° and 150° have the same **reference angle** (both reference to 30°, but different terminal sides).
</Callout>

## ⚔️ Part 7: Conquering the Sine & Cosine Laws

### 7.1 Beyond Right Triangles

Everything so far required a **right triangle** (one 90° angle). But real life is messy, most triangles in architecture, surveying, and navigation **don't have a right angle**. For these **oblique triangles**, we need two powerful laws.

<ObliqueTriangle a="a" b="b" c="c" A="A" B="B" C="C" showHeight={false} />

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Naming Convention:** Lowercase = **side**, Uppercase = **angle opposite that side**. So side $a$ is always opposite angle $A$, side $b$ opposite $B$, and side $c$ opposite $C$.
</Callout>

### 7.2 The Law of Sines (Sine Law)

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: Law of Sines (قانون الجيب)">
  $$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$

  Each side divided by the sine of its **opposite angle** gives the same constant value.
</Formula>

**When to use Sine Law:**

| Given Information | Case Name | What You Can Find |
|-------------------|-----------|-------------------|
| 2 angles + 1 side | **AAS** or **ASA** | Remaining sides and angle |
| 2 sides + angle opposite one of them | **SSA** | The opposite angle (⚠️ ambiguous case possible) |

<Example title="Sine Law: AAS Case">
  In triangle ABC: $A = 40^\circ$, $B = 75^\circ$, $a = 10$. Find $b$.

  **Step 1:** Find the third angle: $C = 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 75^\circ = 65^\circ$

  **Step 2:** Apply Sine Law:

  $$\frac{10}{\sin 40^\circ} = \frac{b}{\sin 75^\circ}$$

  $$b = \frac{10 \times \sin 75^\circ}{\sin 40^\circ} = \frac{10 \times 0.9659}{0.6428} \approx 15.02$$
</Example>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **The Ambiguous Case (SSA): A Mathematical Trap**

  When you are given two sides and an angle that is NOT between them (Side-Side-Angle), the Sine Law can be dangerous. This is known as the **Ambiguous Case**. Because $\sin \theta = \sin(180^\circ - \theta)$, the math alone cannot tell you which angle is correct. 

  Depending on the given lengths, SSA can result in:
  1. **Zero triangles:** The given side is too short to reach the base.
  2. **Exactly one triangle:** It forms a perfect right triangle or a single oblique triangle.
  3. **Two valid triangles:** The swinging side can connect to the base in two different places, creating two entirely different valid geometries.

  Always check if the second possible angle ($180^\circ - \text{first angle}$) makes sense within the triangle's constraints (all angles must sum to $180^\circ$).
</Callout>

To see exactly how this ambiguous case plays out in a real problem, follow this step-by-step example:

<Example title="Detecting the Second Triangle (SSA)">
  Given $\triangle ABC$ with $a = 12$, $b = 15$, and $\angle A = 40^\circ$. Find $\angle B$.

  Using the Sine Law:
  $$\frac{12}{\sin 40^\circ} = \frac{15}{\sin B}$$
  $$\sin B = \frac{15 \times \sin 40^\circ}{12} \approx 0.8035$$
  $$B_1 = \sin^{-1}(0.8035) \approx 53.5^\circ$$

  Now, check for the **Ambiguous Case** (the second possible angle):
  $$B_2 = 180^\circ - 53.5^\circ = 126.5^\circ$$

  Is $B_2$ valid? Add it to the given angle $A$:
  $$126.5^\circ + 40^\circ = 166.5^\circ$$
  Since $166.5^\circ < 180^\circ$, there is enough room for a third angle. Thus, **two valid triangles** exist!
</Example>

### 7.3 Deriving the Sine Law

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="🧠 For Curious Minds: The Proof Behind the Magic">
    Consider triangle ABC. Drop a perpendicular of height $h$ from vertex $B$ to side $b$:

    <ObliqueTriangle a="a" b="" c="c" A="A" B="B" C="C" showHeight={true} />

    From the **left** right triangle: $\sin A = \frac{h}{c}$ → so $h = c \sin A$

    From the **right** right triangle: $\sin C = \frac{h}{a}$ → so $h = a \sin C$

    Since both expressions equal $h$:

    $$c \sin A = a \sin C$$

    $$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$

    Dropping a perpendicular from a different vertex and repeating the process gives us the complete law:

    $$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

### 7.4 The Law of Cosines (Cosine Law)

<Formula name="⭐ CORE TRUTH: Law of Cosines (قانون جيب التمام)">
  $$a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc \cos A$$

  Can be rewritten for any side:

  $$b^2 = a^2 + c^2 - 2ac \cos B$$
  $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C$$
</Formula>

This law might look complex, but it contains a brilliant hidden secret that connects it directly back to the very first concept we learned:

<Callout type="info" emoji="🥇">
  **Beautiful Connection:** When $A = 90^\circ$, we get $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so:

  $$a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc(0) = b^2 + c^2$$

  That's the **Pythagorean Theorem**! The Cosine Law is the generalized version, it works for **every** triangle, not just right triangles.
</Callout>

**When to use Cosine Law:**

| Given Information | Case Name | What You Can Find |
|-------------------|-----------|-------------------|
| 3 sides, no angles | **SSS** | Any angle |
| 2 sides + the angle **between** them | **SAS** | The third side |

<Example title="Cosine Law: SAS Case">
  In triangle ABC: $a = 8$, $b = 6$, $C = 60^\circ$. Find $c$.

  $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$
  $$c^2 = 8^2 + 6^2 - 2(8)(6)\cos 60^\circ$$
  $$c^2 = 64 + 36 - 96 \times 0.5$$
  $$c^2 = 100 - 48 = 52$$
  $$c = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$$
</Example>

<Example title="Cosine Law: SSS Case (Finding an Angle)">
  In triangle ABC: $a = 5$, $b = 7$, $c = 8$. Find angle $C$.

  Rearrange the Cosine Law to solve for $\cos C$:

  $$\cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$$

  $$\cos C = \frac{25 + 49 - 64}{2(5)(7)} = \frac{10}{70} = \frac{1}{7}$$

  $$C = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{7}\right) \approx 81.79^\circ$$
</Example>

### 7.5 Deriving the Cosine Law

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="🧠 For Curious Minds: The Proof Behind the Magic">
    Place the triangle with vertex $C$ at the origin and side $b$ along the x-axis.

    **Coordinates:**
    - $C = (0, 0)$
    - $A = (b, 0)$
    - $B = (a\cos C, \; a\sin C)$

    Using the **distance formula** for side $c$ (from $A$ to $B$):

    $$c^2 = (a\cos C - b)^2 + (a\sin C - 0)^2$$

    Expanding:

    $$c^2 = a^2\cos^2 C - 2ab\cos C + b^2 + a^2\sin^2 C$$

    Since $\cos^2 C + \sin^2 C = 1$:

    $$c^2 = a^2(\cos^2 C + \sin^2 C) + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$

    $$\boxed{c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C}$$
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

### 7.6 The Decision Flowchart: Which Law Do I Use?

<Callout type="default" emoji="🧠">
  **The One Question That Decides Everything:**

  *"Do I have a **complete pair**, a side AND its opposite angle?"*

  - **YES** → Use **Sine Law**
  - **NO** → Use **Cosine Law**
</Callout>

| Scenario | What You Have | Which Law | Why |
|----------|---------------|-----------|-----|
| **AAS / ASA** | 2 angles + 1 side | Sine Law | You can find the 3rd angle, then you have a complete pair |
| **SSA** | 2 sides + opposite angle | Sine Law | You already have a side-angle pair |
| **SAS** | 2 sides + included angle | Cosine Law | The known angle is **between** the known sides, no complete pair |
| **SSS** | 3 sides, 0 angles | Cosine Law | No angles at all, so no pair exists |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
  **Why can't SSS use Sine Law?**

  With $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$, if you know $a$, $b$, but **neither** $A$ nor $B$, the equation has **two unknowns**, it's impossible to solve! You must use Cosine Law first to find at least one angle.
</Callout>

<Callout type="success" emoji="✅">
  **Checkpoint: What You Know So Far**
  - You know how reference angles unlock values across all quadrants.
  - You understand directed angles and how to find co-terminal angles.
  - You've conquered oblique triangles using the Sine and Cosine Laws.

  <details>
    <summary>🧠 Quick Mental Check</summary>
    If you are given all three sides of a triangle (SSS) but no angles, which law must you use first?<br/>**Answer:** The Law of Cosines.
  </details>
</Callout>

<Callout type="error" emoji="🚨" title="Exam Traps & Tricks">
  **Veteran Teacher Warning: How they will test you here:**
  1. **The Hidden Triangle (SSA):** When you are given two sides and an angle not between them, the exam is **begging** you to forget the second possible angle ($180^\circ - \theta$). Always check it!
  2. **The SSS Trap:** If you try to use Sine Law on three sides, you will stare at your paper forever because there are two unknown angles. **No complete pair = Cosine Law!**
  3. **Rounding Too Early:** Keep all decimal places in your calculator until the very final step. Rounding in the middle of a Cosine Law calculation will ruin your final answer.
</Callout>

## 🏗️ Part 8: Applying Trigonometry to the Real World (Elevation & Depression)

### 8.1 Trigonometry Meets the Real World

This is where trigonometry stops being abstract and starts solving problems you can see with your own eyes, from measuring building heights to calculating flight paths.

<Compare>
  <Item label="Angle of Elevation (زاوية الارتفاع)">
    - Looking **UP** from the horizontal
    - From your eye level to an object **above** you
    - Example: Looking up at an airplane, the top of a building, or a mountain peak
    <ElevationDepression type="elevation" objectLabel="* Object" observerLabel="You (horizontal)" angle="θ (elevation)" height="" distance="" />
  </Item>
  <Item label="Angle of Depression (زاوية الانخفاض)">
    - Looking **DOWN** from the horizontal
    - From your eye level to an object **below** you
    - Example: Looking down from a lighthouse at a ship, or from a cliff to the ground
    <ElevationDepression type="depression" objectLabel="* Object" observerLabel="You" angle="θ (depression)" height="" distance="" />
  </Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🥇">
  **The Rule of Equality:**

  The angle of elevation from point A looking **up** to point B **always equals** the angle of depression from point B looking **down** to point A.

  **Why?** Because they are **alternate interior angles** formed between two parallel horizontal lines cut by a transversal (your line of sight).

  <ElevationDepression type="elevation" objectLabel="Observer above" observerLabel="Observer below" angle="θ" height="" distance="(line of sight)" />
</Callout>

<Example title="Finding the Height of a Building">
  You stand **50 meters** from the base of a building. The angle of elevation to the top is **35°**. Find the building's height.

  <ElevationDepression type="elevation" objectLabel="Top of building" observerLabel="You" angle="35°" height="h = ?" distance="50m" />

  You have the Adjacent side (50m) and need the Opposite side ($h$) → use **Tangent**:

  $$\tan 35^\circ = \frac{h}{50}$$
  $$h = 50 \times \tan 35^\circ \approx 50 \times 0.7002 = 35.01 \text{ meters}$$
</Example>

<Example title="Angle of Depression Problem">
  A person stands on top of an **80-meter** building and sees a car on the street at an angle of depression of **25°**. Find the horizontal distance $d$ between the building and the car.

  <ElevationDepression type="depression" objectLabel="Car" observerLabel="Top" angle="25°" height="80m" distance="d" />

  The angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the car (alternate interior angles), so:

  $$\tan 25^\circ = \frac{80}{d}$$
  $$d = \frac{80}{\tan 25^\circ} = \frac{80}{0.4663} \approx 171.56 \text{ meters}$$
</Example>

<Callout type="info" emoji="💡">
  **Problem-Solving Strategy for Elevation/Depression:**

  1. **Draw the diagram**, always! Even if it's rough.
  2. **Mark the right angle**, these problems always form a right triangle.
  3. **Label the sides** as Opposite, Adjacent, or Hypotenuse relative to the given angle.
  4. **Choose SOH, CAH, or TOA** based on which sides you know and need.
  5. **Solve**, and don't forget units!

  Most problems use **tangent** because you typically know the horizontal distance and need the height (or vice versa), and $\tan = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$ connects height to distance.
</Callout>

### Right Triangles
- **Pythagorean Theorem:** $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$
- **Primary Ratios:** $\sin \theta = \frac{\text{Opp}}{\text{Hyp}}$, $\cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adj}}{\text{Hyp}}$, $\tan \theta = \frac{\text{Opp}}{\text{Adj}}$
- **Reciprocal Ratios:** $\csc \theta = \frac{\text{Hyp}}{\text{Opp}}$, $\sec \theta = \frac{\text{Hyp}}{\text{Adj}}$, $\cot \theta = \frac{\text{Adj}}{\text{Opp}}$
- **Euclidean Altitude Rule:** $AD^2 = BD \times CD$
- **Euclidean Leg Rule:** $AB^2 = BD \times BC$, $AC^2 = CD \times BC$
- **Complementary Angles:** $\sin \theta = \cos(90^\circ - \theta)$, $\cos \theta = \sin(90^\circ - \theta)$, $\tan \theta = \cot(90^\circ - \theta)$

### The Unit Circle
- **Unit Circle Coordinates:** $(x, y) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$

### Oblique Triangles
- **Law of Sines:** $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$
- **Law of Cosines:** $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$
- **Co-terminal Angles:** $\theta_{\text{new}} = \theta \pm 360^\circ n$

## 🎯 Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You MUST Remember for the Exam 🎯">
  <Point>**SOH-CAH-TOA**: $\sin = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\cos = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\tan = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$. The reciprocals (csc, sec, cot) are just the fractions flipped.</Point>
  <Point>**Unit Circle**: Every point is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. The radius is 1, so $x = \cos\theta$ and $y = \sin\theta$ directly.</Point>
  <Point>**Famous Angles** (0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°): For sin, use the pattern $\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2}$ through $\frac{\sqrt{4}}{2}$. For cos, reverse the order. For tan, divide sin by cos.</Point>
  <Point>**ASTC**: "All Students Take Calculus": QI: All positive, QII: Sin positive, QIII: Tan positive, QIV: Cos positive.</Point>
  <Point>**Reference Angles**: QI = $\theta$, QII = $180^\circ-\theta$, QIII = $\theta-180^\circ$, QIV = $360^\circ-\theta$. Get the numerical value from the reference angle, get the sign from ASTC.</Point>
  <Point>**Directed Angles**: Counter-clockwise is positive (+), Clockwise is negative (-).</Point>
  <Point>**Sine Law**: Use when you have a complete side-angle pair. Beware of the Ambiguous Case (SSA) which can create two triangles.</Point>
  <Point>**Cosine Law**: Use when you do not have a complete pair (SAS, SSS). It reduces to the Pythagorean Theorem when the angle is 90°.</Point>
  <Point>**Elevation & Depression**: Both form right triangles. The angle of elevation from below is equal to the angle of depression from above. Most problems use tangent.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
