# Forces, Newton's Laws & Circular Motion

<Callout type="info" emoji="🍎">
You're in a car. The driver slams the brakes. Your body lurches forward - but **WHY?** You didn't push yourself. Nobody shoved you. Yet your body flew toward the dashboard as if possessed. The answer lives in three laws written by a young genius during a plague lockdown in 1665. These laws - **Newton's Laws of Motion** - explain everything from why seatbelts save lives to why the International Space Station doesn't fall out of the sky. Master them, and you hold the key to predicting the motion of **everything in the universe**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="default" emoji="🎯">
**Learning Outcome:** PH.1.03 - Predict an object's motion based on the forces acting on it.

**Essential Questions:**
- 🏗️ How can you design a tall structure that doesn't blow over in heavy winds?
- 🌲 How can you control the direction a large tree falls when chopping it down?
- 📄 Why can a sheet of paper be dragged from beneath a heavy body without the body moving?

**Big Idea:** Every change in motion - speeding up, slowing down, turning, orbiting - is caused by **forces**. Newton's three laws give us the complete mathematical framework to predict and control that motion.
</Callout>

## Part 1 - The Foundation: Forces & Reference Frames

Before we can predict motion or state the laws that govern the universe, we must first understand what a **force** is - its magnitude and direction - and the **perspective** (reference frame) from which we measure it.

### 1.1 The Nature of Force

<Definition term="Force (القوة)">
A **push or pull** acting on an object that can cause it to accelerate, decelerate, or change direction. Force is measured in **Newtons (N)**, where **1 N = 1 kg·m/s²**. Force is a **vector quantity** - it has both magnitude AND direction.
</Definition>

Forces come in two fundamental categories:

<Compare>
<Item label="Contact Forces (قوى التلامس)">
- Require **physical contact** between objects
- Examples: **Normal force**, **friction**, **tension**, **applied force**, **spring force**
- The track pushing a dragster's tires, a rope pulling a sled, your hand pushing a door
</Item>
<Item label="Field Forces (قوى المجال)">
- Act **at a distance** - no contact needed
- Examples: **Gravity**, **electromagnetic force**, **nuclear force**
- Earth pulling an apple down, magnets attracting iron filings
</Item>
</Compare>

### 1.2 Scalars vs. Vectors - The Critical Difference

To solve force problems, you must classify physical quantities into two categories:

| Scalar Quantities (كميات قياسية) | Vector Quantities (كميات متجهة) |
| --- | --- |
| Have **magnitude only** | Have **magnitude + direction** |
| Mass [kg] | Weight [N] ↓ |
| Time [s] | Force [N] → |
| Distance [m] | Displacement [m] → |
| Speed [m/s] | Velocity [m/s] → |
| Energy [J] | Acceleration [m/s²] → |
| Temperature [°C] | Momentum [kg·m/s] → |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - Mass ≠ Weight**

Students frequently use "mass" and "weight" interchangeably, but they are **entirely different quantities**:
- **Mass (m)** is a **scalar** - the amount of matter in an object [kg]. It **NEVER changes**, regardless of location.
- **Weight (W)** is a **vector** - the gravitational force on that mass, always pointing **downward**: **W = mg**.
- A 70 kg person weighs **686 N on Earth** but only **113 N on the Moon** - same mass, different weight!
</Callout>

### 1.3 Vector Mathematics & Components

Because forces are vectors, you **cannot** simply add them like regular numbers if they point in different directions. For example, **5 N North + 5 N East ≠ 10 N**. Instead, forces must be resolved into an **x–y coordinate system**.

<Steps>
<Step title="Choose a Coordinate System">
Draw standard x (horizontal) and y (vertical) axes. For inclined planes, tilt the axes so x runs **parallel** to the surface.
</Step>
<Step title="Resolve Vectors into Components">
Use trigonometry: **Fₓ = F cos θ** (horizontal) and **Fᵧ = F sin θ** (vertical). The component **adjacent** to the angle gets **cosine**; the component **opposite** gets **sine**.
</Step>
<Step title="Sum the x-direction Forces">
Add all horizontal components: **ΣFₓ** (Right = positive, Left = negative).
</Step>
<Step title="Sum the y-direction Forces">
Add all vertical components: **ΣFᵧ** (Up = positive, Down = negative).
</Step>
<Step title="Calculate the Resultant Magnitude">
Use the Pythagorean theorem: **R = √(ΣFₓ² + ΣFᵧ²)**
</Step>
<Step title="Calculate the Angle">
**θ = tan⁻¹(|ΣFᵧ| / |ΣFₓ|)** - then determine the quadrant based on the signs of ΣFₓ and ΣFᵧ.
</Step>
</Steps>

<ImageWithText src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Vector_components.svg" alt="Vector decomposition into horizontal and vertical components" caption="Any diagonal vector can be resolved into unique x and y components." width="300px" />

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - Trig Functions**

Students often assume the x-component **always** uses cosine and y **always** uses sine. This is only true if θ is measured **from the horizontal x-axis**. If the angle is given from the vertical, the functions **flip**. **Always draw your right triangle first** and identify which side is adjacent (cos) and which is opposite (sin) to the given angle.
</Callout>

<Example title="Worked Example - Adding 2D Force Vectors">
**Scenario:** A box is subjected to two forces: F₁ = 45 N pushing horizontally to the right (+x), and F₂ = 60 N acting vertically downward (−y). Find the resultant net force vector and its angle.

**Step 1 - Components:** Both forces lie on the axes, so no decomposition needed. ΣFₓ = +45 N, ΣFᵧ = −60 N.

**Step 2 - Magnitude:** R = √(45² + 60²) = √(2025 + 3600) = √5625 = **75 N**

**Step 3 - Angle:** θ = tan⁻¹(60/45) = **53.1° below the positive x-axis** (Standard position: 360° − 53.1° = 306.9°)
</Example>

### 1.4 Frames of Reference & Relative Motion

<Definition term="Frame of Reference (الإطار المرجعي)">
The **perspective** from which an observer measures position, velocity, and acceleration. An object can be "moving" in one frame and "at rest" in another - motion is **relative**.
</Definition>

<Steps>
<Step title="Identify the Object's velocity">
V_Object - the velocity of the thing you're observing.
</Step>
<Step title="Identify the Frame's velocity">
V_Frame - the velocity of YOUR observation point.
</Step>
<Step title="Assign signs based on direction">
e.g., East = positive (+), West = negative (−).
</Step>
<Step title="Calculate relative velocity">
**V_Relative = V_Object − V_Frame**
</Step>
</Steps>

<Example title="Worked Example - Relative Velocity">
**Scenario:** Car A moves East at +20 m/s. Car B moves West at −70 m/s. Find the speed of Car A relative to Car B.

V_Relative = V_A − V_B = (+20) − (−70) = 20 + 70 = **90 m/s**

From Car B's perspective, Car A approaches at **90 m/s**.
</Example>

### 1.5 Inertial Reference Frames

<Definition term="Inertial Reference Frame (إطار مرجعي قصوري)">
A reference frame that is **at rest** or moving at **constant velocity** (zero acceleration). Newton's Laws are **only valid** in inertial reference frames. A frame accelerating or rotating is **non-inertial** - fictitious forces appear.
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🛗">
**REAL-WORLD ANCHOR - The Accelerating Elevator**

- **Stationary or constant velocity** (a = 0): Scale reads **N = mg** → true weight. This IS an inertial frame.
- **Accelerating upward** (a > 0): Scale reads **N = m(g + a)** → you feel **heavier**.
- **Accelerating downward** (a > 0): Scale reads **N = m(g − a)** → you feel **lighter**.
- **Free-falling** (a = g): Scale reads **N = 0** → apparent **weightlessness**!

A moving bus at constant speed is a valid inertial frame. But the moment it accelerates or brakes, it becomes non-inertial - which is why you lurch!
</Callout>

## Part 2 - Visualizing the Physics: Free-Body Diagrams & Net Force

If you can accurately identify forces, draw a **Free-Body Diagram (FBD)**, and calculate the **net force**, applying Newton's Laws becomes straightforward. This is your most important tool.

### 2.1 The Common Force Types

| Force | Symbol | Direction | Formula | When It Appears |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight / Gravity** | W or Fg | Always **straight down** | W = mg | Always (every object has weight) |
| **Normal Force** | N or Fₙ | **Perpendicular** to the contact surface, outward | N = mg (flat); N = mg cos θ (incline) | Object rests on or is pressed against a surface |
| **Applied Force** | Fₐₚₚ | Direction of the push/pull | Given in problem | Someone/something pushes or pulls |
| **Friction** | f or Ff | **Opposite** to direction of motion (or intended motion) | f = μN | Rough surfaces in contact |
| **Tension** | T | **Along** the rope/string, away from the object | From FBD analysis | Ropes, strings, cables |
| **Centripetal** | Fᴄ | **Toward** the center of circular path | Fᴄ = mv²/r | Object moves in a circle |

### 2.2 Drawing Free-Body Diagrams (FBDs)

<Definition term="Free-Body Diagram (مخطط الجسم الحر)">
A pictorial tool used to analyze forces acting on a body. The object is **isolated** from its environment and replaced by **force arrows** showing every external force. It is the **first step** in solving any force problem.
</Definition>

<Steps>
<Step title="Isolate the Object">
Draw a simple **box or dot** to represent the object. Remove everything else - no ground, no ropes, no other objects. Just the body.
</Step>
<Step title="Identify ALL External Forces">
List every force acting **on** the body: Weight? Normal? Tension? Applied? Friction? Do NOT include internal forces or forces the object exerts on other things.
</Step>
<Step title="Draw Force Vectors">
Draw an arrow for each force pointing **away from the center** of the object. Arrow length ≈ relative magnitude. Arrowhead points in the **exact direction** of the force.
</Step>
<Step title="Assign a Coordinate System">
Superimpose x–y axes. On a **ramp**, tilt the axes so the x-axis runs **parallel** to the surface and the y-axis is **perpendicular** to it.
</Step>
<Step title="Label Everything">
Label each arrow with its symbol (Fg, Fₙ, T, Fₐₚₚ, f) and any given values or angles.
</Step>
</Steps>

<ImageWithText src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Free_body.svg" alt="Free body diagram of a block on an inclined plane" caption="A standard Free-Body Diagram. The object is isolated, and all external forces are drawn as arrows originating from the center." width="250px" />

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - Normal Force ≠ mg Always**

Students frequently assume **Fₙ = mg** in every scenario. This is **only true on a perfectly flat, horizontal surface** with no other vertical forces acting. Here's what actually happens:
- **On a ramp:** Fₙ = mg cos θ
- **Upward-accelerating elevator:** Fₙ = m(g + a)
- **Extra downward force applied:** Fₙ > mg
- **Upward pull applied:** Fₙ < mg

Always calculate the normal force from **ΣFᵧ = 0** (or ΣFᵧ = ma if accelerating vertically).
</Callout>

### 2.3 Determining Net Force (ΣF)

<Definition term="Net Force - ΣF (محصلة القوى)">
The **vector sum** of all individual forces acting on a body. It is the single equivalent force that produces the same effect as all the individual forces combined. Also called the **resultant force**.
</Definition>

<Formula name="Net Force Magnitude">
**F_net = √( (ΣFₓ)² + (ΣFᵧ)² )**

If all forces are along one axis: **F_net = ΣF** (just add/subtract with signs).
</Formula>

<Example title="Worked Example - Sled on a Snow-Covered Hill">
**Scenario:** A sled is tied to a tree on a **frictionless** hill (θ = 30.0°). The sled weighs 77.0 N. It is in **static equilibrium**. Find the tension (T) and normal force (N).

**Step 1 - FBD:** Tilt axes so x is parallel to the hill. Three forces act: Tension (up the incline, +x), Normal (perpendicular to hill, +y), Weight (straight down - must be decomposed).

**Step 2 - Weight Components:**
- Down the ramp (−x): Wₓ = W sin 30° = 77.0 × 0.500 = 38.5 N
- Into the ramp (−y): Wᵧ = W cos 30° = 77.0 × 0.866 = 66.7 N

**Step 3 - ΣFₓ = 0:** T − W sin 30° = 0 → **T = 38.5 N**

**Step 4 - ΣFᵧ = 0:** N − W cos 30° = 0 → **N = 66.7 N**
</Example>

## Part 3 - State of Balance: Newton's 1st Law & Equilibrium

We start our journey into Newton's Laws with the simplest state: when the **net force equals zero** (ΣF = 0). Understanding **inertia** and **equilibrium** is essential before we introduce acceleration.

### 3.1 Newton's First Law - The Law of Inertia

<Definition term="Newton's First Law - Law of Inertia (قانون نيوتن الأول - قانون القصور الذاتي)">
An object **at rest stays at rest** and an object **in motion stays in motion** with the same speed and in the same direction, **unless acted upon by an unbalanced (net) force**. Objects tend to "keep on doing what they're doing."

**Mathematical form:** If **ΣF = 0**, then **a = 0** (velocity is constant or zero).
</Definition>

<Definition term="Inertia (القصور الذاتي)">
The natural tendency of an object to **resist changes** in its state of motion. An object resists any attempt to speed it up, slow it down, or change its direction. Inertia is **directly proportional to mass** - the more massive an object, the harder it is to change its motion.
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🪙">
**REAL-WORLD ANCHOR - The Tablecloth Trick**

Place a heavy coin on a piece of paper over a cup. **Yank** the paper quickly - the coin drops **straight into the cup** instead of flying with the paper. Why? The coin's large mass gives it enormous **inertia**. The friction force acts for too little time (**impulse J = F·Δt ≈ 0**) to overcome that inertia. The coin stays stationary while the paper slides out beneath it. This is **Newton's First Law in action** - and it's also the answer to our essential question about dragging paper from beneath a heavy body!
</Callout>

### 3.2 The Two States of Equilibrium

When all forces perfectly balance (**ΣF = 0**), the object does **not accelerate** - this state is called **Equilibrium**.

<Compare>
<Item label="Static Equilibrium (الاتزان السكوني)">
- Object is **at rest** (v = 0 m/s)
- Requirements: **a = 0**, **ΣF = 0**, **F_net = 0**
- Example: A book resting on a table, a bridge standing still
- The object was stationary and **stays stationary**
</Item>
<Item label="Dynamic Equilibrium (الاتزان الحركي)">
- Object moves at **constant velocity** (v ≠ 0 m/s, but constant)
- Requirements: **a = 0**, **ΣF = 0**, **F_net = 0**
- Example: A parachutist at terminal velocity, a car cruising at constant speed
- The object is moving but **not accelerating**
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - "Motion Requires a Constant Force"**

Many students assume that if an object is moving to the right, there **must** be a force pushing it to the right. **WRONG.** Newton's First Law says a force is **NOT needed** to keep a moving object moving. A book on a frictionless surface slides **forever**. It only slows in the real world because of **unbalanced friction** - not because "motion needs force."
</Callout>

### 3.3 Solving Equilibrium Problems

When an object is in equilibrium, you automatically know **ΣFₓ = 0** and **ΣFᵧ = 0**. This gives you two powerful equations to solve for unknown forces.

<Steps>
<Step title="Confirm the State">
Verify the object is **at rest** or moving at **constant velocity** (a = 0 m/s²). Keywords: "at rest," "constant speed," "uniform velocity," "equilibrium."
</Step>
<Step title="Draw the Free-Body Diagram">
Represent the object as a point or box. Draw ALL force vectors with correct directions and labels.
</Step>
<Step title="Resolve Components">
Break any diagonal forces into x and y components using trigonometry (F cos θ, F sin θ).
</Step>
<Step title="Set Up Equations">
Write: **ΣFₓ = 0** and **ΣFᵧ = 0**
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for the Unknown">
Use algebra to isolate the missing variable - tension, normal force, friction, applied force, etc.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🏗️">
**CAPSTONE CONNECTION - Designing Wind-Resistant Structures**

To ensure a tall solar tower (15,000 kg) doesn't blow over in a 45,000 N wind, engineers apply **ΣFₓ = 0**: F_wind − F_friction = 0 → **F_friction = 45,000 N**. The foundation must provide **exactly 45,000 N** of shear resistance to maintain static equilibrium. A low center of gravity and wide base of support ensure the structure won't topple. This is Newton's 1st Law applied to real engineering!
</Callout>

## Part 4 - State of Change: Newton's 2nd Law & Acceleration

In Part 3 we established the rules for equilibrium (**ΣF = 0**). Now we **break** the equilibrium - what happens when the net force is **NOT** zero?

### 4.1 Newton's Second Law - The Law of Acceleration

<Definition term="Newton's Second Law (قانون نيوتن الثاني)">
The **acceleration** of an object is **directly proportional** to the net force acting on it, in the **same direction** as the net force, and **inversely proportional** to the object's mass.

**ΣF = m × a** → **a = F_net / m**
</Definition>

<Formula name="Newton's Second Law">
**ΣF = ma**

Where:
- **ΣF** = net force (sum of ALL forces) [N]
- **m** = mass [kg]
- **a** = acceleration [m/s²]
</Formula>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - "Use Any Force"**

Students often plug a **single applied force** directly into F = ma. Newton's 2nd Law requires the **NET force** (F_net = ΣF) - the vector sum of **ALL** forces. You must **subtract opposing forces** (like friction) before calculating acceleration!
</Callout>

### 4.2 The Three Laws - At a Glance

| Law | Name | Statement | Equation | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1st** | Law of Inertia | An object at rest stays at rest; in motion stays in motion, unless acted on by an unbalanced force | ΣF = 0 → a = 0 | Balanced forces |
| **2nd** | Law of Acceleration | Acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass | **ΣF = ma** | Unbalanced forces |
| **3rd** | Law of Action-Reaction | For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force | F₁₂ = −F₂₁ | All interactions |

### 4.3 Proportionality Relationships

<Tabs items={["📈 Force & Acceleration", "📉 Mass & Acceleration"]}>
<Tab label="📈 Force & Acceleration">

### Direct Proportionality

From **a = F_net / m** (with mass constant):

- If net force **doubles** → acceleration **doubles**
- If net force **triples** → acceleration **triples**
- They are **directly proportional**: a ∝ F_net

**Graph:** A straight line through the origin when plotting a vs F (at constant mass).

</Tab>
<Tab label="📉 Mass & Acceleration">

### Inverse Proportionality

From **a = F_net / m** (with force constant):

- If mass **doubles** → acceleration **halves**
- If mass **triples** → acceleration becomes **one-third**
- They are **inversely proportional**: a ∝ 1/m

**Graph:** A curved (hyperbolic) line when plotting a vs m.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

### 4.4 Weight, Mass & Universal Gravitation

<Formula name="Weight">
**W = mg**

Where g = 9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface. Weight is a **force** [N], not a mass [kg].
</Formula>

<Formula name="Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation">
**Fg = G · m₁ · m₂ / r²**

Where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass.
</Formula>

### 4.5 The 5-Step Universal Method for Force Problems

<Callout type="info" emoji="🔑">
This method works for **ANY force problem** - equilibrium or accelerating, flat or inclined, simple or complex. Master these 5 steps and you can solve any question the exam throws at you.
</Callout>

<Steps>
<Step title="Draw a Free-Body Diagram">
Represent the object as a dot or box. Draw **every external force** as an arrow. Label each with its symbol and given values.
</Step>
<Step title="Break Forces into Components">
Resolve any diagonal forces: **Fₓ = F cos θ** (parallel to surface) and **Fᵧ = F sin θ** (perpendicular to surface).
</Step>
<Step title="Redraw with Components Only">
Create a clean version of your FBD showing only the pure x and y vectors. This prevents calculation errors.
</Step>
<Step title="Sum Forces per Axis">
Add/subtract all x-forces (Right = +, Left = −) and all y-forces (Up = +, Down = −). Get **ΣFₓ** and **ΣFᵧ**.
</Step>
<Step title="Apply F_net = ma">
For acceleration: **ΣF = ma → a = ΣF/m**. For equilibrium: **ΣF = 0** → solve for the unknown force.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Example title="Worked Example - The Log Drag">
**Scenario:** A 50 N applied force drags an 8.16 kg log to the right. A 40.0 N friction force resists the motion. What is the acceleration?

**Step 1 - Forces:** Applied (+50 N right), Friction (−40 N left), Weight (down), Normal (up).

**Step 2 - Components:** All forces lie on the axes - no trig needed.

**Step 3 - ΣFₓ:** 50 − 40 = **+10 N** (net rightward)

**Step 4 - Apply F = ma:** 10 = 8.16 × a → **a = 10/8.16 = 1.23 m/s²** to the right.
</Example>

<Example title="Worked Example - Airboat Acceleration">
**Scenario:** An airboat (mass 350 kg) has an engine producing a net horizontal force of 770 N. (a) Find the acceleration. (b) Starting from rest, how long to reach 12.0 m/s?

**(a)** a = F_net / m = 770 / 350 = **2.20 m/s²**

**(b)** Using kinematics: v = v₀ + at → 12.0 = 0 + (2.20)t → **t = 5.45 s**
</Example>

## Part 5 - Multi-Body Systems & Newton's Third Law

The real world does not occur in isolated textbook problems. Here we apply Newton's Laws to **multi-body systems** and **action-reaction pairs**.

### 5.1 Newton's Third Law - Action and Reaction

<Definition term="Newton's Third Law (قانون نيوتن الثالث)">
For every **action force**, there is an **equal and opposite reaction force**. These forces act on **DIFFERENT objects**.

**F₁₂ = −F₂₁** (equal magnitudes, opposite directions, different objects)
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🍈">
**Key Insight:** It is critical to distinguish between **balanced forces** and **action-reaction pairs**:
- **Balanced Forces:** Two forces act on a **SINGLE body** (e.g., gravity and normal force on a cantaloupe resting on a table). These CAN result in equilibrium.
- **Action-Reaction Pairs:** Forces act on **DIFFERENT interacting bodies** (e.g., Earth pulls cantaloupe down AND cantaloupe pulls Earth up). These NEVER cancel each other out because they act on different objects!
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - Normal Force is NOT the Reaction to Gravity**

Students often think that the normal force on an object is the "reaction" to gravity because they are equal and opposite. **Impossible** - they both act on the **same object**! The true reaction to Earth's gravity pulling the cantaloupe **down** is the cantaloupe's gravity pulling **Earth up**. They act on different objects (cantaloupe → Earth, Earth → cantaloupe).
</Callout>

### 5.2 Multi-Body System Formulas

When multiple objects are connected (by ropes, pulleys, or contact), they accelerate **together** as a single system.

| System | Setup | Acceleration Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vertical Atwood Machine** | Two masses over a frictionless pulley; m₂ > m₁ | a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂) |
| **Horizontal Table System** | m₁ on smooth table, m₂ hanging off the edge | a = m₂g / (m₁ + m₂) |
| **Inclined Plane + Hanging Mass** | m₁ on incline (angle θ), m₂ hanging; m₂ > m₁ sin θ | a = (m₂ − m₁ sin θ)g / (m₁ + m₂) |
| **Blocks in Contact** | Two blocks pushed by external force F on a smooth surface | a = F / (m₁ + m₂) |

<ImageWithText src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Atwood_machine.svg" alt="Atwood machine diagram with two masses and a pulley" caption="An Atwood Machine: two masses connected by a string over a frictionless pulley. They accelerate as a single system." width="200px" />

**Additional Useful Formulas:**

<Formula name="Atwood Machine Tension">
**T = 2m₁m₂g / (m₁ + m₂)**
</Formula>

<Formula name="Contact Force Between Blocks">
**F_contact = m₂F / (m₁ + m₂)**

Where F is the applied force on block 1, and F_contact is the force block 1 exerts on block 2.
</Formula>

### 5.3 Center of Mass & Structural Stability

<Definition term="Center of Mass / Center of Gravity (مركز الكتلة / مركز الثقل)">
The theoretical point where **all the mass** of an object is considered concentrated. An object remains in **stable equilibrium** as long as the center of gravity falls **vertically within** its base of support.
</Definition>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - "Heavier = More Stable"**

Adding mass to the **TOP** of a structure **raises** its center of gravity, making it **LESS stable**. True stability comes from a **LOW center of gravity** and a **WIDE base of support**, not just total mass.
</Callout>

<ImageWithText src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/The_Leaning_Tower_of_Pisa_SB.jpeg" alt="The Leaning Tower of Pisa" caption="The Leaning Tower of Pisa remains standing solely because its center of mass still falls vertically within its base footprint." width="250px" />

<Callout type="info" emoji="🏛️">
**REAL-WORLD ANCHOR - The Leaning Tower of Pisa**

Despite its severe tilt, the tower remains standing because its center of gravity still falls **vertically within** its wide foundation base. Engineers also added lead counterweights to the base and removed soil from one side to correct the lean - all applications of center-of-mass physics.
</Callout>

## Part 6 - Uniform Circular Motion & Centripetal Forces

Up to now, we treated acceleration primarily as a change in **speed**. But because velocity is a **vector**, a change in **direction** ALSO constitutes acceleration. Objects can move at constant speed while **continuously accelerating**.

### 6.1 The "Constant Speed but Accelerating" Paradox

<Definition term="Uniform Circular Motion (الحركة الدائرية المنتظمة)">
Motion in a **circle** at **constant speed**. Although speed is constant, the **velocity continuously changes direction** - therefore the object is **always accelerating** toward the center.
</Definition>

Three key vectors in circular motion:

| Vector | Direction | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Instantaneous Velocity (v)** | Always **TANGENT** to the circular path (pointing "outward") | v |
| **Centripetal Acceleration (aᴄ)** | Always **TOWARD the center** (perpendicular to v) | aᴄ |
| **Centripetal Force (Fᴄ)** | Always **TOWARD the center** (same direction as aᴄ) | Fᴄ |

<ImageWithText src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Centripetal_force_diagram.svg" alt="Diagram showing centripetal force and tangental velocity" caption="Notice how the velocity vector is always tangent to the circle, while acceleration and force point inwards." width="300px" />

<Callout type="info" emoji="🔄">
**WHY A CIRCLE REQUIRES FORCE**

Newton's 1st Law says an object moves in a **straight line** unless a net force acts on it. If you release a rock on a string, it flies off in a **straight tangent** - not a circle. Therefore, to maintain circular motion, a **continuous inward force** must constantly pull the object away from that straight-line path. That inward force is the **centripetal force**.
</Callout>

### 6.2 The Mathematics of Circular Motion

<Formula name="Linear (Tangential) Speed">
**v = 2πr / T**

Where r = radius [m] and T = period (time for one complete revolution) [s].
</Formula>

<Formula name="Centripetal Acceleration">
**aᴄ = v² / r = 4π²r / T²**

Always directed **toward the center** of the circular path.
</Formula>

<Formula name="Centripetal Force">
**Fᴄ = m·aᴄ = m·v² / r**

This is NOT a new type of force - it is the **net inward force** (could be tension, gravity, friction, normal force, or a combination) that causes circular motion.
</Formula>

<Callout type="error" emoji="🚫">
**COMMON MISCONCEPTION - "Centrifugal Force"**

When riding in a turning car, you feel "pushed" outward. There is **NO force pushing you out**. Your body's **inertia** (Newton's 1st Law) wants to continue in a straight line while the car turns away from you. **NEVER draw a "centrifugal force" on a free-body diagram.** Centripetal force is strictly an **inward** net force.
</Callout>

<Example title="Worked Example - The Rock on a String">
**Scenario:** A 0.150 kg rock is whirled in a horizontal circle (radius = 0.600 m) with period T = 0.500 s. What is the tension in the string?

**Step 1 - Find Speed:** v = 2πr/T = 2π(0.600)/0.500 = **7.54 m/s**

**Step 2 - Identify Radial Force:** Only the string's **tension** acts horizontally toward the center.

**Step 3 - Apply ΣFᵣ = mv²/r:**
F_T = (0.150)(7.54²)/(0.600) = (0.150)(56.85)/(0.600) = **14.2 N**

**Conclusion:** The string exerts **14.2 N** of inward centripetal force to maintain the circular orbit.
</Example>

<Callout type="info" emoji="🌍">
**GRAND CHALLENGE CONNECTION**

**Exponential Population Growth:** As cities grow taller and denser, every high-rise must be engineered to remain in **static equilibrium** under wind loads, seismic forces, and occupant weight. Newton's Laws - especially **ΣF = 0** for stability and **ΣF = ma** for dynamic loading - are the core tools.

**Efficient Use of Arid Land:** Wind turbine blades spinning at high speed are governed by **centripetal force** (Fᴄ = mv²/r). Solar arrays in deserts must withstand forces exceeding 60,000 N in sandstorms - equilibrium analysis determines the minimum anchor force.
</Callout>

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms - PH.1.03">
<Term word="Force (القوة)">A push or pull on an object, measured in Newtons (N). A vector quantity requiring both magnitude and direction. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².</Term>
<Term word="Vector Quantity (كمية متجهة)">A physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction. Examples: force, velocity, acceleration, displacement.</Term>
<Term word="Scalar Quantity (كمية قياسية)">A physical quantity that has magnitude only. Examples: mass, time, speed, distance, energy.</Term>
<Term word="Net Force / Resultant Force (القوة المحصلة)">The vector sum of all forces acting on an object. Determines whether the object accelerates (ΣF ≠ 0) or is in equilibrium (ΣF = 0).</Term>
<Term word="Free-Body Diagram (مخطط الجسم الحر)">A visual tool showing all external forces acting on an isolated body as arrows. The first step in solving any force problem.</Term>
<Term word="Normal Force (القوة العمودية)">The perpendicular contact force exerted by a surface on an object resting on it. A reaction force to the component of weight pressing into the surface.</Term>
<Term word="Tension (قوة الشد)">The pulling force transmitted through a rope, string, or cable. Direction: along the rope, away from the object.</Term>
<Term word="Friction (قوة الاحتكاك)">A contact force opposing the direction of motion (or intended motion) between two surfaces. f = μN.</Term>
<Term word="Weight (الوزن)">The gravitational force on an object: W = mg. A vector pointing straight down. Measured in Newtons, not kilograms.</Term>
<Term word="Mass (الكتلة)">The amount of matter in an object, measured in kg. A scalar that never changes regardless of location.</Term>
<Term word="Inertia (القصور الذاتي)">An object's natural resistance to changes in its state of motion. Directly proportional to mass.</Term>
<Term word="Newton's 1st Law (قانون نيوتن الأول)">Law of Inertia: an object maintains its state of rest or uniform motion unless acted on by a net external force. ΣF = 0 → a = 0.</Term>
<Term word="Newton's 2nd Law (قانون نيوتن الثاني)">Net force equals mass times acceleration: ΣF = ma. Acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass.</Term>
<Term word="Newton's 3rd Law (قانون نيوتن الثالث)">For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force acting on a different object: F₁₂ = −F₂₁.</Term>
<Term word="Equilibrium (الاتزان)">State where ΣF = 0 and a = 0. Can be static (at rest) or dynamic (constant velocity).</Term>
<Term word="Static Equilibrium (الاتزان السكوني)">Object at rest with zero net force and zero acceleration.</Term>
<Term word="Dynamic Equilibrium (الاتزان الحركي)">Object moving at constant velocity with zero net force and zero acceleration.</Term>
<Term word="Centripetal Acceleration (التسارع المركزي)">Acceleration directed toward the center of a circular path: aᴄ = v²/r. Caused by a change in velocity direction, not speed.</Term>
<Term word="Centripetal Force (القوة المركزية)">The net inward force that causes circular motion: Fᴄ = mv²/r. Not a new force - it's the resultant of existing forces (tension, gravity, friction, etc.).</Term>
<Term word="Uniform Circular Motion (حركة دائرية منتظمة)">Motion in a circle at constant speed. Velocity changes direction continuously, so the object accelerates toward the center.</Term>
<Term word="Inertial Reference Frame (إطار مرجعي قصوري)">A reference frame at rest or moving at constant velocity (a = 0). Newton's Laws are valid only in inertial frames.</Term>
<Term word="Center of Mass (مركز الكتلة)">The point where all mass is considered concentrated. Stability requires the center of mass to fall within the base of support.</Term>
<Term word="Universal Gravitation (الجاذبية الكونية)">Every mass attracts every other mass: Fg = Gm₁m₂/r². G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Force** is a vector quantity (magnitude + direction), measured in Newtons. **Mass** (scalar, kg) ≠ **Weight** (vector force, N). Weight = mg.</Point>
<Point>**Scalar vs Vector**: Scalars have magnitude only (mass, time, speed). Vectors have magnitude AND direction (force, velocity, acceleration, displacement).</Point>
<Point>**Vector components**: Fₓ = F cos θ, Fᵧ = F sin θ (when θ is from the horizontal). Resultant: R = √(Fₓ² + Fᵧ²). Angle: θ = tan⁻¹(Fᵧ/Fₓ).</Point>
<Point>**Free-Body Diagrams** are your #1 tool. Isolate the object → draw ALL forces → choose axes → resolve components → sum per axis.</Point>
<Point>**Newton's 1st Law (Inertia)**: ΣF = 0 → a = 0. Objects resist changes in motion. No force needed to maintain constant velocity.</Point>
<Point>**Equilibrium** means ΣF = 0, a = 0. **Static** = at rest. **Dynamic** = constant velocity. Both have zero acceleration.</Point>
<Point>**Newton's 2nd Law**: ΣF = ma. Use NET force, not individual forces. Force and acceleration are directly proportional; mass and acceleration are inversely proportional.</Point>
<Point>**Newton's 3rd Law**: Action-reaction pairs act on DIFFERENT objects, have equal magnitude, and opposite directions. They never cancel each other.</Point>
<Point>**Normal force ≠ mg** always. On a ramp: N = mg cos θ. In an elevator: N = m(g ± a). With extra vertical forces: adjust accordingly.</Point>
<Point>**Circular motion**: Constant speed but changing direction = acceleration toward center. aᴄ = v²/r. Fᴄ = mv²/r. Velocity is tangent; force and acceleration point inward.</Point>
<Point>**"Centrifugal force" does NOT exist** - it's your inertia resisting the turn. Never draw it on an FBD.</Point>
<Point>**Inertial frames**: Newton's Laws hold only in frames with a = 0 (at rest or constant velocity). Accelerating/rotating frames are non-inertial.</Point>
<Point>**Stability** requires a LOW center of gravity and WIDE base of support. Adding mass to the top makes structures LESS stable.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
