# Science, Measurement & Matter

<Callout type="info" emoji="🔬">
Welcome to the **starting line** of Chemistry. This LO covers the **scientific method**, **measurement**, **significant figures**, **dimensional analysis**, and **matter classification**. These aren't "easy intro topics" – they're the **tools** you'll use in every single lab, calculation, and exam question for the rest of the year. Master them now, and Chemistry becomes logical. Skip them, and you'll struggle everywhere.
</Callout>

## What is Science?

<Definition term="Science (العلم)">
A systematic, evidence-based approach to understanding the natural world through **observation**, **experimentation**, and **logical reasoning**. From the Latin ***scientia*** = knowledge.
</Definition>

Science isn't just a collection of facts in a textbook – it's a **process**. A way of thinking. A method for turning "I wonder why..." into "Now I know because..."

### Branches of Science

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Natural Sciences">
Study the **physical and natural world**. Split into two main categories:

**Physical Sciences** – deal with non-living matter and energy:
- **Chemistry** (الكيمياء) – the study of matter, its properties, composition, and transformations
- **Physics** (الفيزياء) – the study of energy, forces, and the fundamental laws governing the universe

**Life Sciences** – deal with living organisms:
- **Biology** (علم الأحياء) – the study of living organisms
- **Geology** (الجيولوجيا) – the study of Earth's structure, materials, and processes
- **Environmental Science** (علوم البيئة) – the study of interactions between organisms and their environment
</Tab>
<Tab label="Social Sciences">
Study **human behaviour and societies**:
- **Psychology** – the study of the mind and behaviour
- **Sociology** – the study of human social relationships
- **Economics** – the study of production, distribution, and consumption of goods

Social sciences use the scientific method but often deal with less controllable variables than natural sciences.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Formal Sciences">
Study **abstract systems and logic**:
- **Mathematics** – the study of numbers, quantities, and shapes
- **Computer Science** – the study of computation and information
- **Statistics** – the study of data collection, analysis, and interpretation

Formal sciences provide the **tools** (equations, models, algorithms) used by all other sciences.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
Chemistry is sometimes called the **"Central Science"** because it bridges physics (which explains *why* atoms behave as they do) and biology (which studies the *living systems* built from molecules). You can't fully understand biology without chemistry, and you can't fully understand chemistry without physics.
</Callout>

## The Scientific Method

The scientific method is the **backbone** of all scientific discovery. It's not a rigid recipe – it's a **cyclical, self-correcting process** that scientists use to investigate questions about the natural world.

### The Father of the Scientific Method

<Timeline>
<TimelineItem date="965–1040 CE" title="Hassan Ibn al-Haytham (الحسن بن الهيثم) – Father of the Scientific Method">
Arab scientist **Hassan Ibn al-Haytham** (known in the West as **Alhazen**) was the first to establish the modern scientific method. In his groundbreaking **Book of Optics** (*Kitab al-Manazir*), he insisted that theories must be verified through **systematic experimentation** – not just philosophical reasoning. He proved that light travels in straight lines and enters the eye (rather than the eye emitting light), disproving the Greek emission theory. He is considered the **father of modern optics** and the **pioneer of the scientific method**.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1620" title="Francis Bacon – Formalisation of the Method">
English philosopher **Francis Bacon** published *Novum Organum*, formalising the **inductive method** – drawing general conclusions from specific observations. He emphasised that science must be based on empirical evidence, not on ancient authority.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1637" title="René Descartes – Rationalism and Deduction">
French philosopher **René Descartes** published *Discourse on the Method*, establishing a framework of systematic doubt and **deductive reasoning**. Together with Bacon's inductive approach, this formed the foundation of modern scientific inquiry.
</TimelineItem>
</Timeline>

### Steps of the Scientific Method

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Observation (الملاحظة)">
Notice something interesting in the natural world. Use your senses or instruments to gather information. Good science starts with **careful, detailed observation**.

**Example:** You notice that a metal spoon left in hot soup becomes hot at the handle, but a wooden spoon does not.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Question (السؤال)">
Ask a specific, testable question about your observation.

**Example:** "Does the material a spoon is made from affect how quickly heat travels through it?"
</Step>
<Step title="3. Hypothesis (الفرضية)">
Propose a **testable, falsifiable** explanation – an educated guess based on existing knowledge. A good hypothesis makes a **prediction** that can be tested.

**Example:** "If the material affects heat transfer, then metal spoons will transfer heat faster than wooden spoons."
</Step>
<Step title="4. Experiment (التجربة)">
Design a **controlled experiment** to test your hypothesis. Change only the **independent variable**, measure the **dependent variable**, and keep everything else constant (**controlled variables**).

**Example:** Place metal, wooden, and plastic spoons in identical cups of hot water. Measure handle temperature at regular intervals.
</Step>
<Step title="5. Analyse Data (تحليل البيانات)">
Collect, organise, and analyse your results. Use tables, graphs, and statistical tools. Look for **patterns** and determine whether the data supports or refutes your hypothesis.
</Step>
<Step title="6. Conclusion (الاستنتاج)">
State whether the results **support** or **refute** your hypothesis. A hypothesis is never "proven" – it is either supported by evidence or not. If refuted, **modify the hypothesis** and test again.
</Step>
<Step title="7. Communicate (النشر)">
Share your findings through publication, peer review, and replication. Other scientists must be able to **repeat** your experiment and get similar results. This is how science builds trust and knowledge.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Common exam trap:** A hypothesis is **never proven** – it is either **supported** or **refuted** by evidence. Even after thousands of supporting experiments, it remains a hypothesis until it becomes part of a broader theory. Science is about evidence, not proof.
</Callout>

## Scientific Law vs Scientific Theory

These two terms are often confused – even by adults. They are NOT the same thing, and one is NOT "better" than the other.

<Compare>
<Item label="Scientific Law (القانون العلمي)">
A **concise statement** (often mathematical) that describes **what** happens in nature under specific conditions. A law tells you the **pattern** but NOT why it happens.

**Example:** Newton's Law of Gravity tells you that all objects with mass attract each other – but it doesn't explain *why* mass creates gravitational pull.

**Key traits:**
- Describes a consistent pattern in nature
- Usually expressed as a mathematical equation
- Does NOT explain the mechanism
- Can be disproven by a single contradictory observation
</Item>
<Item label="Scientific Theory (النظرية العلمية)">
A **well-tested, comprehensive explanation** of **why** something happens in nature. A theory is supported by a **large body of evidence** from multiple experiments and observations.

**Example:** Einstein's Theory of General Relativity explains *why* gravity works – it's the curvature of spacetime caused by mass.

**Key traits:**
- Explains the mechanism behind observed patterns
- Supported by extensive, reproducible evidence
- Can make predictions about new situations
- Can be modified as new evidence emerges
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam-critical distinction:** A law describes **what** happens. A theory explains **why** it happens. A theory does NOT become a law when it's "proven enough." They are fundamentally different types of scientific knowledge. A theory can *contain* laws, but a law never *becomes* a theory.
</Callout>

| Feature | Scientific Law | Scientific Theory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Answers** | What happens? | Why does it happen? |
| **Form** | Usually a mathematical equation | A detailed explanation |
| **Scope** | Narrow – specific phenomenon | Broad – explains many related phenomena |
| **Example** | Boyle's Law: $PV = k$ | Kinetic Molecular Theory |
| **Can be disproven?** | Yes | Yes |

## Variables and Relationships

Understanding variables is **essential** – every lab, every experiment, every graph question on your exam depends on this.

### Types of Variables

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Independent Variable">
**The variable YOU change** in an experiment. It's the **cause** – the factor you deliberately manipulate.

Also called: **manipulated variable** (المتغير المستقل)

**How to identify it:** Ask "What am I changing on purpose?" That's your independent variable.

**On a graph:** Always plotted on the **x-axis** (horizontal).

**Example:** In an experiment testing how temperature affects reaction rate, **temperature** is the independent variable – you control it.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Dependent Variable">
**The variable you MEASURE** in response to changes in the independent variable. It's the **effect** – the outcome that depends on what you changed.

Also called: **responding variable** (المتغير التابع)

**How to identify it:** Ask "What am I measuring or observing?" That's your dependent variable.

**On a graph:** Always plotted on the **y-axis** (vertical).

**Example:** In the same experiment, **reaction rate** is the dependent variable – it changes in response to temperature changes.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Controlled Variables">
**All other variables that must remain CONSTANT** to ensure a fair test. If these change, you can't be sure whether the independent variable caused the observed effect.

Also called: **constants** or **fixed variables** (المتغيرات الثابتة)

**Example:** In the temperature-vs-reaction-rate experiment, you must keep the **concentration**, **volume**, **type of reactant**, and **pressure** constant.

**Rule:** Change ONE variable (independent), measure ONE variable (dependent), and keep EVERYTHING else constant.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Types of Relationships Between Variables

When you plot independent vs dependent variables on a graph, you'll see one of these patterns:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Direct (Linear)">
As the independent variable **increases**, the dependent variable **increases** proportionally.

**Graph shape:** Straight line going **up** from left to right.

<Formula>

$$y = kx$$

</Formula>

Where $k$ is a positive constant (the slope).

**Example:** Hooke's Law – the more **force** you apply to a spring, the more it **stretches**: $F = kx$
</Tab>
<Tab label="Inverse">
As the independent variable **increases**, the dependent variable **decreases**.

**Graph shape:** Curve going **down** from left to right (hyperbola).

<Formula>

$$y = \frac{k}{x}$$

</Formula>

**Example:** **Boyle's Law** – as the **pressure** on a gas increases, its **volume** decreases (at constant temperature): $PV = k$

If you double the pressure, the volume halves. Triple it, the volume drops to a third.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Direct Quadratic">
As the independent variable increases, the dependent variable increases **by the square**.

**Graph shape:** Parabola curving **upward**.

<Formula>

$$y = kx^2$$

</Formula>

**Example:** Kinetic energy – if you **double** the speed of an object, its kinetic energy **quadruples**: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
</Tab>
<Tab label="No Relationship">
Changes in the independent variable produce **no predictable change** in the dependent variable.

**Graph shape:** Scattered points with **no pattern**, or a **horizontal line**.

**Example:** The colour of your shirt and your exam grade – no relationship exists.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Correlation vs Causation

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Correlation does NOT mean causation.** Just because two variables change together doesn't mean one *causes* the other.

**Classic example:** Ice cream sales and drowning rates both increase in summer. Does ice cream cause drowning? No – both are caused by a **third variable**: hot weather.

In science, you can only claim causation if you've conducted a **controlled experiment** with proper variable management. Observational data can only show correlation.
</Callout>

## Measurement and the SI System

Science depends on **precise, universal measurement**. That's why we use the **SI system** (Système International d'Unités) – the international standard for measurement used by scientists worldwide.

### The Seven Base SI Units

These are the **foundation** – every other unit in science is derived from these seven:

| Quantity | SI Unit | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Length** (الطول) | metre | m |
| **Mass** (الكتلة) | kilogram | kg |
| **Time** (الزمن) | second | s |
| **Temperature** (درجة الحرارة) | kelvin | K |
| **Amount of Substance** (كمية المادة) | mole | mol |
| **Electric Current** (شدة التيار) | ampere | A |
| **Luminous Intensity** (شدة الإضاءة) | candela | cd |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam tip:** The kilogram is the **only** base SI unit that includes a prefix (kilo-). This is a historical quirk – the gram was too small for practical use, so the kilogram became the standard. When converting, always convert to **grams** first, then to your target unit.
</Callout>

### Derived Units

Derived units are **combinations** of base units. Some common ones:

| Quantity | Derived Unit | Symbol | In Base Units |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Area** | square metre | m² | m × m |
| **Volume** | cubic metre | m³ | m × m × m |
| **Density** | kg per cubic metre | kg/m³ | kg × m⁻³ |
| **Velocity** | metre per second | m/s | m × s⁻¹ |
| **Force** | newton | N | kg × m × s⁻² |
| **Pressure** | pascal | Pa | kg × m⁻¹ × s⁻² |
| **Energy** | joule | J | kg × m² × s⁻² |

### SI Prefixes

SI prefixes let you express very large or very small numbers efficiently:

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| tera- | T | $10^{12}$ | 1 TB = $10^{12}$ bytes |
| giga- | G | $10^{9}$ | 1 GHz = $10^{9}$ Hz |
| mega- | M | $10^{6}$ | 1 MW = $10^{6}$ W |
| kilo- | k | $10^{3}$ | 1 km = $10^{3}$ m |
| hecto- | h | $10^{2}$ | 1 hL = $10^{2}$ L |
| deca- | da | $10^{1}$ | 1 dam = 10 m |
| – | – | $10^{0}$ | base unit |
| deci- | d | $10^{-1}$ | 1 dm = 0.1 m |
| centi- | c | $10^{-2}$ | 1 cm = 0.01 m |
| milli- | m | $10^{-3}$ | 1 mm = 0.001 m |

| pico- | p | $10^{-12}$ | 1 pm = $10^{-12}$ m |

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
**Memory trick:** For the most common prefixes, remember **"King Henry Doesn't Usually Drink Chocolate Milk"** – **K**ilo, **H**ecto, **D**eca, **U**nit, **D**eci, **C**enti, **M**illi. Each step is a factor of 10.
</Callout>

### Mass vs Weight

<Compare>
<Item label="Mass (الكتلة)">
The **amount of matter** in an object. Mass is a **scalar** quantity – it has magnitude only.

- **Never changes** regardless of location
- Measured in **kilograms (kg)**
- Measured using a **balance** (ميزان)
- A 70 kg person has 70 kg of mass on Earth, the Moon, and in space
</Item>
<Item label="Weight (الوزن)">
The **gravitational force** acting on an object's mass. Weight is a **vector** quantity – it has both magnitude and direction (always toward the centre of gravity).

- **Changes** depending on gravitational field strength
- Measured in **newtons (N)**
- Measured using a **spring scale** (ميزان زنبركي)
- A 70 kg person weighs ~686 N on Earth but only ~114 N on the Moon
</Item>
</Compare>

<Formula>

$$W = mg$$

</Formula>

Where $W$ = weight (N), $m$ = mass (kg), and $g$ = gravitational acceleration ($9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$ on Earth).

## Uncertainty in Measurement

No measurement is **perfectly exact**. Every measurement has some degree of **uncertainty** – and a good scientist always reports it.

### Reporting Measurements

When you read an instrument (ruler, thermometer, graduated cylinder), you should:

1. **Read all the certain digits** – the digits you can read directly from the scale markings
2. **Estimate one uncertain digit** – the last digit, estimated between the smallest markings
3. **Report the measurement including the estimated digit**

<Example>
A ruler has markings every **0.1 cm**. You measure an object and see it falls between 2.3 cm and 2.4 cm, closer to the 2.3 side. You estimate the last digit as 2.34 cm.

- **Certain digits:** 2.3 (you're sure about these)
- **Uncertain digit:** 4 (this is your best estimate)
- **Reported measurement:** 2.34 cm ± 0.01 cm
</Example>

### Types of Errors

<Compare>
<Item label="Systematic Error (الخطأ المنتظم)">
Errors that are **consistent and repeatable** – they push all measurements in the **same direction** (always too high or always too low).

**Causes:**
- Faulty/uncalibrated equipment
- Parallax error (reading a meniscus from wrong angle)
- Consistent personal bias in reading

**Key trait:** Can be **identified and corrected** by calibrating equipment or improving technique.

**Example:** A scale that reads 0.5 g too high – every measurement will be 0.5 g above the true value.
</Item>
<Item label="Random Error (الخطأ العشوائي)">
Errors that are **unpredictable** – they cause measurements to scatter **both above and below** the true value.

**Causes:**
- Slight variations in reading instruments
- Environmental fluctuations (temperature, vibrations)
- Human reaction time

**Key trait:** Cannot be eliminated, but can be **reduced** by taking **multiple measurements and averaging**.

**Example:** Timing a reaction by hand – sometimes you start the stopwatch slightly early, sometimes slightly late.
</Item>
</Compare>

### Percentage Error

Percentage error tells you how far off your measurement is from the **accepted (true) value**:

<Formula>

$$\text{Percentage Error} = \frac{|\text{Experimental Value} - \text{Accepted Value}|}{\text{Accepted Value}} \times 100\%$$

</Formula>

<Example>
You measure the density of aluminium as 2.85 g/cm³. The accepted value is 2.70 g/cm³.

$$\text{Percentage Error} = \frac{|2.85 - 2.70|}{2.70} \times 100\% = \frac{0.15}{2.70} \times 100\% = 5.56\%$$
</Example>

### Error Propagation

When you combine measurements in calculations, errors propagate:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Addition / Subtraction">
When **adding or subtracting** measurements, add the **absolute uncertainties**:

<Formula>

$$\Delta R = \Delta A + \Delta B$$

</Formula>

**Example:**
- Length A = $25.3 \pm 0.1$ cm
- Length B = $10.1 \pm 0.1$ cm
- Total = $35.4 \pm 0.2$ cm
</Tab>
<Tab label="Multiplication / Division">
When **multiplying or dividing** measurements, add the **percentage (relative) uncertainties**:

<Formula>

$$\frac{\Delta R}{R} = \frac{\Delta A}{A} + \frac{\Delta B}{B}$$

</Formula>

**Example:**
- Length = $5.0 \pm 0.1$ cm → relative uncertainty = $\frac{0.1}{5.0} = 2\%$
- Width = $3.0 \pm 0.1$ cm → relative uncertainty = $\frac{0.1}{3.0} = 3.3\%$
- Area → total relative uncertainty = $2\% + 3.3\% = 5.3\%$
- Area = $15.0 \text{ cm}^2 \pm 5.3\% = 15.0 \pm 0.8 \text{ cm}^2$
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Accuracy and Precision

These two words sound similar, but they mean **completely different things** in science.

<Compare>
<Item label="Accuracy (الدقة)">
How **close** your measurement is to the **true (accepted) value**. Accuracy answers the question: "Did I hit the target?"

- Affected by **systematic errors**
- Improved by **calibrating equipment** and using correct technique
- You need the **accepted value** to judge accuracy
</Item>
<Item label="Precision (الإحكام)">
How **close** your repeated measurements are to **each other**. Precision answers the question: "Are my shots clustered together?"

- Affected by **random errors**
- Improved by taking **more measurements** and using more precise instruments
- Does NOT require knowing the true value
</Item>
</Compare>

### The Basketball Analogy

Imagine shooting a basketball at a hoop:

| Scenario | What it looks like | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accurate AND Precise** | All shots go through the hoop, clustered tightly | Close to true value, consistent with each other |
| **Accurate but NOT Precise** | Shots scattered around the hoop – some left, some right – but centre on the basket | Average is close to true value, but individual shots are spread out |
| **Precise but NOT Accurate** | All shots hit the same spot – but it's the wrong spot (e.g., all hit the backboard) | Far from true value, but consistent with each other |
| **Neither Accurate nor Precise** | Shots are scattered everywhere, none near the hoop | Far from true value, inconsistent |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam trap:** Precision does NOT guarantee accuracy. You can have a very precise set of measurements that are all **wrong** (systematic error). In the basketball analogy, hitting the same wrong spot every time = precise but not accurate.
</Callout>

<Example>
Three students measure the boiling point of water (accepted value: 100.0 °C):

| Student | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 | Average | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | 99.8 | 100.1 | 100.0 | 100.0 | Accurate AND Precise |
| **B** | 95.0 | 100.0 | 105.0 | 100.0 | Accurate, NOT Precise |
| **C** | 97.2 | 97.3 | 97.1 | 97.2 | NOT Accurate, Precise |
| **D** | 94.0 | 101.0 | 108.0 | 101.0 | NOT Accurate, NOT Precise |
</Example>

## Significant Figures

Significant figures (sig figs) tell you the **precision** of a measurement. They include **all certain digits plus one estimated digit**.

### Rules for Counting Significant Figures

<Steps>
<Step title="Rule 1: Non-zero digits are ALWAYS significant">
Every digit from 1–9 counts.

**Examples:**
- 123 → **3** sig figs
- 45.67 → **4** sig figs
</Step>
<Step title="Rule 2: Zeros BETWEEN non-zero digits are significant (captive zeros)">
Zeros sandwiched between non-zero digits always count.

**Examples:**
- 1002 → **4** sig figs
- 3.08 → **3** sig figs
- 50.04 → **4** sig figs
</Step>
<Step title="Rule 3: Leading zeros are NEVER significant">
Zeros that come before the first non-zero digit are just placeholders – they don't count.

**Examples:**
- 0.0025 → **2** sig figs (only the 2 and 5 count)
- 0.100 → **3** sig figs (the 1 and two trailing zeros count – but the leading 0.0 doesn't)
</Step>
<Step title="Rule 4: Trailing zeros AFTER a decimal point ARE significant">
If a number has a decimal point, trailing zeros after the last non-zero digit are significant – they indicate measured precision.

**Examples:**
- 2.50 → **3** sig figs (the trailing 0 was measured)
- 100.0 → **4** sig figs
- 0.0300 → **3** sig figs (leading zeros don't count, but trailing zeros after decimal do)
</Step>
<Step title="Rule 5: Trailing zeros WITHOUT a decimal point are AMBIGUOUS">
Trailing zeros in a whole number with no decimal point are generally considered NOT significant.

**Examples:**
- 1500 → **2** sig figs (ambiguous – could be 2, 3, or 4)
- 1500. → **4** sig figs (the decimal point signals that all zeros are measured)

**Solution:** Use scientific notation to remove ambiguity:
- $1.5 \times 10^3$ → **2** sig figs
- $1.50 \times 10^3$ → **3** sig figs
- $1.500 \times 10^3$ → **4** sig figs
</Step>
<Step title="Rule 6: Exact numbers have UNLIMITED significant figures">
Exact numbers (defined quantities, counting numbers) have infinite precision and never limit your sig fig count.

**Examples:**
- 1 km = **exactly** 1000 m (unlimited sig figs)
- 12 eggs in a dozen (unlimited sig figs)
- π used in calculations (unlimited sig figs – use as many decimals as needed)
</Step>
</Steps>

### Significant Figures in Calculations

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Multiplication / Division">
**Rule:** Your answer can have only as many significant figures as the measurement with the **fewest sig figs**.

**Example:**

$$4.56 \times 1.4 = 6.384 \rightarrow \text{round to } 6.4$$

Why? 4.56 has 3 sig figs. 1.4 has **2** sig figs. The answer is limited to **2** sig figs.

**More examples:**
- $2.5 \times 3.42 = 8.55 \rightarrow 8.6$ (2 sig figs)
- $\frac{0.305}{1.2} = 0.25416... \rightarrow 0.25$ (2 sig figs)
</Tab>
<Tab label="Addition / Subtraction">
**Rule:** Your answer can have only as many **decimal places** as the measurement with the **fewest decimal places**.

**Example:**

$$12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.123 \rightarrow \text{round to } 31.1$$

Why? 18.0 has **1** decimal place – the fewest. So the answer is rounded to **1** decimal place.

**More examples:**
- $3.14 + 0.001 = 3.141 \rightarrow 3.14$ (2 decimal places)
- $100.0 - 99.55 = 0.45 \rightarrow 0.5$ (1 decimal place)
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Rounding Rules

<Steps>
<Step title="If the digit to drop is < 5">
**Round down** – keep the preceding digit the same.

Example: 3.44 rounded to 2 sig figs = 3.4
</Step>
<Step title="If the digit to drop is > 5">
**Round up** – increase the preceding digit by 1.

Example: 3.46 rounded to 2 sig figs = 3.5
</Step>
<Step title="If the digit to drop is exactly 5">
**Round to the nearest EVEN number** (banker's rounding). This eliminates the systematic upward bias of always rounding 5 up.

Examples:
- 2.35 → 2.4 (round up to even)
- 2.45 → 2.4 (round down to even)
- 3.25 → 3.2 (round down to even)
- 3.35 → 3.4 (round up to even)
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam tip:** Some textbooks always round 5 up (the "round half up" rule). In STEM exams, check which rule your teacher follows. The **banker's rounding** rule (round to even) is scientifically more accurate, but both may be accepted.
</Callout>

## Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis (also called the **factor-label method** or **unit-factor method**) is a powerful technique for converting between units. The core idea: multiply by **conversion factors** that equal 1.

### What is a Conversion Factor?

A conversion factor is a fraction where the numerator and denominator represent the **same quantity** in different units:

<Formula>

$$\frac{1 \text{ km}}{1000 \text{ m}} = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{1000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} = 1$$

</Formula>

Since the fraction equals 1, multiplying by it **changes the unit without changing the value**.

### The Method

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Start with the given quantity and unit">
Write down what you're given, including the unit.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Set up the conversion factor">
Arrange the conversion factor so the **unwanted unit cancels out** (appears in both numerator and denominator).
</Step>
<Step title="3. Cancel units and calculate">
Cross out matching units, then multiply/divide the numbers.
</Step>
<Step title="4. Check: does the answer make sense?">
Verify the unit is correct and the magnitude is reasonable.
</Step>
</Steps>

### Worked Examples

<Accordion>
<AccordionItem title="Example 1: Convert 2.5 km to metres">

$$2.5 \text{ km} \times \frac{1000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} = 2500 \text{ m}$$

The "km" cancels, leaving "m".
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Example 2: Convert 4500 mg to grams">

$$4500 \text{ mg} \times \frac{1 \text{ g}}{1000 \text{ mg}} = 4.5 \text{ g}$$

The "mg" cancels, leaving "g".
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Example 3: Convert 72 km/h to m/s (multi-step)">

$$72 \frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1000 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ km}} \times \frac{1 \text{ h}}{3600 \text{ s}} = 20 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$$

Both "km" and "h" cancel, leaving "m/s".

**Shortcut:** To convert km/h to m/s, **divide by 3.6**. To convert m/s to km/h, **multiply by 3.6**.
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Example 4: Convert 0.75 kg to milligrams (two-step)">

$$0.75 \text{ kg} \times \frac{1000 \text{ g}}{1 \text{ kg}} \times \frac{1000 \text{ mg}}{1 \text{ g}} = 750{,}000 \text{ mg} = 7.5 \times 10^5 \text{ mg}$$

First convert kg to g, then g to mg.
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Example 5: Convert 250 cm³ to litres">

Key relationship: **1 L = 1000 cm³** (or equivalently, 1 mL = 1 cm³)

$$250 \text{ cm}^3 \times \frac{1 \text{ L}}{1000 \text{ cm}^3} = 0.250 \text{ L}$$
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Example 6: Convert 5.0 m² to cm²">

**Careful!** Since area is length², you must square the conversion factor:

$$5.0 \text{ m}^2 \times \left(\frac{100 \text{ cm}}{1 \text{ m}}\right)^2 = 5.0 \text{ m}^2 \times \frac{10{,}000 \text{ cm}^2}{1 \text{ m}^2} = 50{,}000 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Common mistake:** Students multiply by 100 instead of 10,000. Remember: $1 \text{ m}^2 = 100^2 \text{ cm}^2 = 10{,}000 \text{ cm}^2$.
</AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Common exam mistake:** When converting **squared** or **cubed** units, you must **square or cube the entire conversion factor**:
- $1 \text{ m}^2 = (100 \text{ cm})^2 = 10{,}000 \text{ cm}^2$ (not 100 cm²)
- $1 \text{ m}^3 = (100 \text{ cm})^3 = 1{,}000{,}000 \text{ cm}^3$ (not 100 cm³)
</Callout>

## Temperature Scales and Conversions

Temperature measures the **average kinetic energy** of particles in a substance. There are three scales you need to know:

<Compare>
<Item label="Celsius (°C)">
**Based on water:**
- 0 °C = freezing point of water
- 100 °C = boiling point of water

Most commonly used scale in daily life and science (outside the US). Named after Swedish astronomer **Anders Celsius**.
</Item>
<Item label="Kelvin (K)">
**The SI unit** of temperature. Based on **absolute zero** – the lowest possible temperature where all particle motion theoretically stops.

- 0 K = absolute zero = −273.15 °C
- 273.15 K = 0 °C
- 373.15 K = 100 °C

**Note:** Kelvin uses NO degree symbol. Write "300 K" not "300 °K".
</Item>
<Item label="Fahrenheit (°F)">
**Based on brine and body temperature:**
- 32 °F = freezing point of water
- 212 °F = boiling point of water

Used primarily in the **United States**. Named after German physicist **Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit**.
</Item>
</Compare>

### Conversion Formulas

<Formula>

$$K = °C + 273.15$$

</Formula>

<Formula>

$$°C = K - 273.15$$

</Formula>

<Formula>

$$°F = \frac{9}{5}(°C) + 32$$

</Formula>

<Formula>

$$°C = \frac{5}{9}(°F - 32)$$

</Formula>

### Key Reference Points

| Point | Celsius | Kelvin | Fahrenheit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Absolute Zero** | −273.15 °C | 0 K | −459.67 °F |
| **Water Freezes** | 0 °C | 273.15 K | 32 °F |
| **Human Body** | 37 °C | 310.15 K | 98.6 °F |
| **Water Boils** | 100 °C | 373.15 K | 212 °F |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam shortcut:** For quick conversions, you can use 273 instead of 273.15. Most exam questions accept this approximation:
- $K \approx °C + 273$
- $°C \approx K - 273$
</Callout>

<Example>
Convert 25 °C to Kelvin and Fahrenheit:

**To Kelvin:**
$$K = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 \text{ K}$$

**To Fahrenheit:**
$$°F = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77 \text{ °F}$$
</Example>

## Density and Specific Gravity

### Density

<Definition term="Density (الكثافة)">
The **mass per unit volume** of a substance. It tells you how tightly packed the matter is in a given space.
</Definition>

<Formula>

$$d = \frac{m}{V}$$

</Formula>

Where $d$ = density, $m$ = mass, and $V$ = volume.

| System | Common Units |
| --- | --- |
| **SI** | kg/m³ |
| **Lab/CGS** | g/cm³ or g/mL |

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
**Key relationship:** 1 g/cm³ = 1 g/mL = 1000 kg/m³

The density of pure water at 4 °C = exactly **1.000 g/cm³** (or 1000 kg/m³). This is a reference value you should memorise.
</Callout>

<Example>
A metal block has a mass of 156.0 g and a volume of 57.8 cm³. What is its density?

$$d = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{156.0 \text{ g}}{57.8 \text{ cm}^3} = 2.70 \text{ g/cm}^3$$

This density matches **aluminium** (2.70 g/cm³) – so the block is likely aluminium.
</Example>

### Specific Gravity

<Definition term="Specific Gravity (الكثافة النوعية)">
The **ratio** of a substance's density to the density of a reference substance (usually **water at 4 °C**). Since it's a ratio of two densities, specific gravity is **dimensionless** – it has **no units**.
</Definition>

<Formula>

$$\text{Specific Gravity} = \frac{d_{\text{substance}}}{d_{\text{water}}} = \frac{d_{\text{substance}}}{1.000 \text{ g/cm}^3}$$

</Formula>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
Since the density of water = 1.000 g/cm³, the specific gravity of a substance is **numerically equal** to its density in g/cm³ – but **without units**.

**Example:** Aluminium has a density of 2.70 g/cm³, so its specific gravity = 2.70 (no units).
</Callout>

**Practical rule:**
- Specific gravity **> 1** → substance is **denser** than water → it **sinks**
- Specific gravity **< 1** → substance is **less dense** than water → it **floats**
- Specific gravity **= 1** → substance has the **same density** as water → neutral buoyancy

## Classification of Matter

Everything in the universe is made of **matter** – anything that has **mass** and occupies **space** (volume).

### States of Matter

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Solid (صلب)">
- **Fixed shape** and **fixed volume**
- Particles are **tightly packed** in a regular arrangement
- Particles **vibrate in place** but don't move past each other
- **Strong intermolecular forces**
- **Incompressible** (cannot be squeezed into smaller volume)

**Examples:** ice, iron, diamond, salt crystals
</Tab>
<Tab label="Liquid (سائل)">
- **No fixed shape** (takes the shape of its container) but **fixed volume**
- Particles are **close together** but can slide past each other
- **Moderate intermolecular forces**
- **Virtually incompressible**

**Examples:** water, mercury, oil, ethanol
</Tab>
<Tab label="Gas (غاز)">
- **No fixed shape** and **no fixed volume** (expands to fill its container)
- Particles are **far apart** and move **rapidly and randomly**
- **Weak intermolecular forces** (nearly negligible)
- **Highly compressible**

**Examples:** oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, helium
</Tab>
<Tab label="Plasma (بلازما)">
- **Ionised gas** – atoms have lost electrons, creating a mixture of positive ions and free electrons
- Most **common state of matter** in the universe (stars, lightning, neon signs)
- Conducts electricity
- Responds to magnetic fields

**Examples:** the Sun's corona, lightning bolts, fluorescent lights, auroras
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### The Matter Classification Tree

<Steps>
<Step title="Matter">
Anything with **mass** and **volume**. All matter is classified as either a **pure substance** or a **mixture**.
</Step>
<Step title="Pure Substance (مادة نقية)">
Has a **fixed, definite composition** and **consistent properties** throughout. Two types:

- **Element (عنصر):** Cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. Made of one type of atom. Examples: gold (Au), oxygen (O₂), carbon (C).
- **Compound (مركب):** Made of two or more elements chemically bonded in a **fixed ratio**. Can be broken down by chemical (not physical) means. Examples: water (H₂O), table salt (NaCl), carbon dioxide (CO₂).
</Step>
<Step title="Mixture (مخلوط)">
A combination of two or more substances where each **retains its original properties**. The components can be separated by **physical means**. Two types:

- **Homogeneous Mixture (مخلوط متجانس):** Uniform composition throughout – you **can't** see the individual components. Also called a **solution**. Examples: salt water, air, alloys (bronze, steel).
- **Heterogeneous Mixture (مخلوط غير متجانس):** Non-uniform composition – you **can** see the individual components. Examples: salad, sand in water, granite, oil and water.
</Step>
</Steps>

### Physical vs Chemical Properties

<Compare>
<Item label="Physical Properties (خواص فيزيائية)">
Properties that can be observed or measured **without changing** the substance's chemical identity.

**Examples:**
- Colour, odour, taste
- Melting point, boiling point
- Density, solubility
- State of matter
- Conductivity, hardness
- Malleability, ductility
</Item>
<Item label="Chemical Properties (خواص كيميائية)">
Properties that describe a substance's ability to **undergo chemical changes** – to transform into a **different substance**.

**Examples:**
- Flammability (ability to burn)
- Reactivity with acids
- Corrosion/rusting ability
- Toxicity
- Oxidation tendency
- Decomposition
</Item>
</Compare>

### Physical vs Chemical Changes

<Compare>
<Item label="Physical Change (تغير فيزيائي)">
A change that alters a substance's **form or appearance** but NOT its **chemical identity**. No new substance is formed.

**Key indicators:**
- Usually **reversible**
- No new substance formed
- Same chemical formula before and after
- Change in state, shape, or size

**Examples:**
- Melting ice (H₂O solid → H₂O liquid)
- Dissolving sugar in water
- Cutting paper
- Bending a wire
- Evaporation
</Item>
<Item label="Chemical Change (تغير كيميائي)">
A change that transforms a substance into a **completely new substance** with different properties.

**Key indicators:**
- Usually **irreversible** (or very difficult to reverse)
- New substance formed
- Energy change (heat, light, sound released or absorbed)
- Colour change, gas production, precipitate formation

**Examples:**
- Burning wood (produces CO₂, H₂O, ash)
- Rusting iron (Fe → Fe₂O₃)
- Cooking an egg
- Digesting food
- Milk turning sour
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam trap:** Dissolving salt in water is a **physical** change – the NaCl molecules separate but remain NaCl. You can get the salt back by evaporating the water. But dissolving a metal in acid is a **chemical** change – a new substance (a salt) forms.
</Callout>

### Separation Techniques

Since mixtures are held together by **physical** means (not chemical bonds), they can be separated using **physical** techniques:

<Accordion>
<AccordionItem title="Filtration (الترشيح)">
**Separates:** An insoluble solid from a liquid

**How it works:** The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. The liquid passes through (filtrate) while the solid is trapped (residue).

**Example:** Separating sand from water
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Evaporation (التبخير)">
**Separates:** A dissolved solid from a liquid (solution)

**How it works:** Heat the solution until the liquid evaporates, leaving the solid behind.

**Example:** Obtaining salt from salt water
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Distillation (التقطير)">
**Separates:** Liquids with **different boiling points** from each other, or a dissolved solid from a liquid (while **keeping the liquid**)

**How it works:** Heat the mixture. The liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates first, then condenses in a separate container when cooled.

**Types:**
- **Simple distillation** – for liquids with boiling points differing by > 25 °C
- **Fractional distillation** – for liquids with close boiling points (uses a fractionating column)

**Example:** Separating ethanol from water; desalination of seawater
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Chromatography (الكروماتوغرافيا)">
**Separates:** Dissolved substances based on differences in their **attraction to a stationary phase** vs **mobility in a mobile phase**

**How it works:** The mixture is carried by a mobile phase (solvent) over a stationary phase (paper or column). Components that are more attracted to the mobile phase travel faster and further.

**Types:**
- **Paper chromatography** – stationary phase is paper
- **Column chromatography** – stationary phase is packed in a column
- **Gas chromatography** – mobile phase is a gas

**Example:** Separating ink dyes; identifying amino acids
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Crystallisation (التبلور)">
**Separates:** A dissolved solid from a solution (produces pure crystals)

**How it works:** The solution is heated until it becomes **saturated**, then cooled slowly. As it cools, the dissolved substance comes out of solution as **pure crystals**.

**Example:** Making pure salt or sugar crystals
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Magnetism (الفصل المغناطيسي)">
**Separates:** Magnetic materials from non-magnetic materials

**How it works:** A magnet is passed over the mixture, attracting iron, nickel, cobalt, or other magnetic substances.

**Example:** Separating iron filings from sand
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Decantation (الترسيب والصب)">
**Separates:** A liquid from a heavier, settled solid or from another immiscible liquid

**How it works:** Allow the heavier component to settle to the bottom, then carefully pour off the lighter liquid from the top.

**Example:** Pouring water off settled mud; separating oil and water after settling
</AccordionItem>
<AccordionItem title="Centrifugation (الطرد المركزي)">
**Separates:** Suspended particles from a liquid using high-speed spinning

**How it works:** The mixture is spun in a centrifuge. Denser particles are forced to the bottom (pellet) while the lighter liquid stays on top (supernatant).

**Example:** Separating blood components; separating cream from milk
</AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms – LO 1">
<Term word="Science (العلم)">A systematic, evidence-based approach to understanding the natural world through observation, experimentation, and logical reasoning.</Term>
<Term word="Chemistry (الكيمياء)">The study of matter, its properties, composition, structure, and the changes it undergoes during chemical reactions.</Term>
<Term word="Scientific Method (المنهج العلمي)">A systematic process of observation, hypothesis formation, experimentation, and conclusion used to investigate natural phenomena.</Term>
<Term word="Observation (الملاحظة)">Using your senses or instruments to gather information about the natural world – the first step of the scientific method.</Term>
<Term word="Hypothesis (الفرضية)">A testable, falsifiable proposed explanation for an observation – an educated guess that can be tested through experimentation.</Term>
<Term word="Experiment (التجربة)">A controlled procedure designed to test a hypothesis by manipulating one variable and measuring the effect on another.</Term>
<Term word="Scientific Law (القانون العلمي)">A concise statement (often mathematical) describing what happens in nature under specific conditions – does not explain why.</Term>
<Term word="Scientific Theory (النظرية العلمية)">A well-tested, comprehensive explanation of why something happens in nature, supported by a large body of evidence.</Term>
<Term word="Independent Variable (المتغير المستقل)">The variable deliberately changed by the experimenter – the cause. Plotted on the x-axis.</Term>
<Term word="Dependent Variable (المتغير التابع)">The variable measured in response to changes in the independent variable – the effect. Plotted on the y-axis.</Term>
<Term word="Controlled Variables (المتغيرات الثابتة)">All variables kept constant in an experiment to ensure only the independent variable affects the dependent variable.</Term>
<Term word="Correlation (الارتباط)">A statistical relationship between two variables – does not imply that one causes the other.</Term>
<Term word="Causation (السببية)">A relationship where changes in one variable directly cause changes in another – established only by controlled experiments.</Term>
<Term word="Direct Relationship (علاقة طردية)">As one variable increases, the other increases proportionally. Graph: straight line upward. Formula: y = kx.</Term>
<Term word="Inverse Relationship (علاقة عكسية)">As one variable increases, the other decreases. Graph: hyperbola. Formula: y = k/x.</Term>
<Term word="SI System (النظام الدولي للوحدات)">The International System of Units – the universal standard for scientific measurement, based on seven base units.</Term>
<Term word="Base Unit (وحدة أساسية)">One of the seven fundamental SI units from which all other units are derived: metre, kilogram, second, kelvin, mole, ampere, candela.</Term>
<Term word="Derived Unit (وحدة مشتقة)">A unit formed by combining base SI units through multiplication or division (e.g., m/s, kg/m³, N).</Term>
<Term word="Mass (الكتلة)">The amount of matter in an object – a scalar quantity measured in kilograms that does not change with location.</Term>
<Term word="Weight (الوزن)">The gravitational force on an object – a vector quantity measured in newtons that changes with gravitational field strength.</Term>
<Term word="Uncertainty (عدم اليقين)">The range of possible values within which the true measurement lies – every measurement has some uncertainty.</Term>
<Term word="Systematic Error (الخطأ المنتظم)">A consistent, repeatable error that shifts all measurements in one direction – caused by faulty equipment or technique.</Term>
<Term word="Random Error (الخطأ العشوائي)">An unpredictable error causing measurements to scatter above and below the true value – reduced by averaging multiple trials.</Term>
<Term word="Percentage Error (نسبة الخطأ)">The absolute difference between experimental and accepted values, divided by the accepted value, multiplied by 100%.</Term>
<Term word="Accuracy (الدقة)">How close a measurement is to the true (accepted) value – affected by systematic errors.</Term>
<Term word="Precision (الإحكام)">How close repeated measurements are to each other – affected by random errors.</Term>
<Term word="Significant Figures (الأرقام المعنوية)">The digits in a measurement that carry meaning – all certain digits plus one estimated digit.</Term>
<Term word="Leading Zeros">Zeros before the first non-zero digit – they are NOT significant (e.g., 0.0025 has 2 sig figs).</Term>
<Term word="Trailing Zeros">Zeros after the last non-zero digit – significant only if a decimal point is present.</Term>
<Term word="Captive Zeros">Zeros between non-zero digits – they are ALWAYS significant (e.g., 1002 has 4 sig figs).</Term>
<Term word="Dimensional Analysis (تحليل الأبعاد)">A method for converting between units by multiplying by conversion factors that equal 1.</Term>
<Term word="Conversion Factor (معامل التحويل)">A fraction equal to 1 used to convert from one unit to another (e.g., 1000 m / 1 km).</Term>
<Term word="Temperature (درجة الحرارة)">A measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.</Term>
<Term word="Celsius (°C)">A temperature scale based on water: 0 °C = freezing point, 100 °C = boiling point.</Term>
<Term word="Kelvin (K)">The SI unit of temperature based on absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C). No degree symbol used.</Term>
<Term word="Fahrenheit (°F)">A temperature scale where water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F – used mainly in the United States.</Term>
<Term word="Absolute Zero (الصفر المطلق)">The lowest possible temperature (0 K = −273.15 °C) where all particle motion theoretically ceases.</Term>
<Term word="Density (الكثافة)">Mass per unit volume of a substance, calculated as d = m/V. Common units: g/cm³ or kg/m³.</Term>
<Term word="Specific Gravity (الكثافة النوعية)">The ratio of a substance's density to the density of water at 4 °C – a dimensionless quantity with no units.</Term>
<Term word="Matter (المادة)">Anything that has mass and occupies space (volume).</Term>
<Term word="Pure Substance (مادة نقية)">A type of matter with a fixed, definite composition – either an element or a compound.</Term>
<Term word="Element (عنصر)">A pure substance made of one type of atom that cannot be broken down by chemical means.</Term>
<Term word="Compound (مركب)">A pure substance made of two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio.</Term>
<Term word="Mixture (مخلوط)">A combination of two or more substances not chemically bonded, each retaining its original properties.</Term>
<Term word="Homogeneous Mixture (مخلوط متجانس)">A mixture with uniform composition throughout – also called a solution.</Term>
<Term word="Heterogeneous Mixture (مخلوط غير متجانس)">A mixture with non-uniform composition – individual components are visibly distinguishable.</Term>
<Term word="Solution (محلول)">A homogeneous mixture of a solute dissolved in a solvent – completely uniform at the molecular level.</Term>
<Term word="Physical Property (خاصية فيزيائية)">A property observed without changing the substance's chemical identity (e.g., colour, density, melting point).</Term>
<Term word="Chemical Property (خاصية كيميائية)">A property describing a substance's ability to undergo a chemical change (e.g., flammability, reactivity).</Term>
<Term word="Physical Change (تغير فيزيائي)">A change in form or appearance without altering chemical identity – usually reversible.</Term>
<Term word="Chemical Change (تغير كيميائي)">A change producing a new substance with different properties – usually irreversible.</Term>
<Term word="Filtration (الترشيح)">A separation technique using filter paper to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid.</Term>
<Term word="Distillation (التقطير)">A separation technique based on differences in boiling points – vaporises then condenses a liquid.</Term>
<Term word="Chromatography (الكروماتوغرافيا)">A separation technique based on differential attraction to stationary and mobile phases.</Term>
<Term word="Crystallisation (التبلور)">A separation technique that produces pure crystals from a saturated solution by slow cooling.</Term>
<Term word="Centrifugation (الطرد المركزي)">A separation technique using high-speed spinning to separate suspended particles from a liquid.</Term>
<Term word="Evaporation (التبخير)">A separation technique where a liquid is heated until it vaporises, leaving a dissolved solid behind.</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Science** is a systematic, evidence-based process – not a collection of facts. The scientific method is its backbone.</Point>
<Point>**Hassan Ibn al-Haytham** is the father of the scientific method – he insisted on evidence and experimentation over philosophical reasoning.</Point>
<Point>**Scientific law** describes **what** happens (pattern). **Scientific theory** explains **why** it happens (mechanism). A theory never "becomes" a law.</Point>
<Point>**Independent variable** = what you change. **Dependent variable** = what you measure. **Controlled variables** = what you keep constant.</Point>
<Point>**Correlation does NOT equal causation** – just because two variables change together doesn't mean one causes the other.</Point>
<Point>**Seven base SI units:** metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), kelvin (K), mole (mol), ampere (A), candela (cd).</Point>
<Point>**Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter and doesn't change with location. **Weight** (N) is the gravitational force and changes with location. $W = mg$.</Point>
<Point>**Systematic errors** are consistent (same direction) and correctable. **Random errors** are unpredictable and reduced by averaging.</Point>
<Point>**Accuracy** = closeness to true value. **Precision** = closeness of repeated measurements to each other. Precise doesn't mean accurate.</Point>
<Point>**Sig fig rules:** Non-zero digits always count. Captive zeros count. Leading zeros never count. Trailing zeros count only with a decimal point.</Point>
<Point>**Multiplication/division:** answer gets the **fewest sig figs**. **Addition/subtraction:** answer gets the **fewest decimal places**.</Point>
<Point>**Dimensional analysis:** multiply by conversion factors that equal 1 to cancel unwanted units. Square/cube the factor for area/volume conversions.</Point>
<Point>**Temperature conversions:** $K = °C + 273.15$, $°F = \frac{9}{5}(°C) + 32$. Kelvin has no degree symbol. 0 K = absolute zero.</Point>
<Point>**Density** = mass ÷ volume ($d = m/V$). **Specific gravity** = substance density ÷ water density – dimensionless. SG > 1 sinks, SG < 1 floats.</Point>
<Point>**Matter classification:** Pure substances (elements, compounds) vs Mixtures (homogeneous, heterogeneous). Separated by physical methods: filtration, distillation, chromatography, crystallisation, evaporation, centrifugation.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
