# Atomic Structure & Subatomic Particles

<Callout type="info" emoji="⚛️">
This LO covers the **historical development of atomic theory**, the **structure of the atom**, **subatomic particles**, **isotopes, isobars & isotones**, and **radioactive radiation** (alpha, beta, gamma). Understanding how scientists discovered what atoms look like – through experiments, failures, and brilliant insights – is one of the most exciting stories in all of science. Master this, and the periodic table, bonding, and nuclear chemistry will all make sense.
</Callout>

<Callout type="default" emoji="❓">
**Essential Question:** *"How is an atom similar to and different from a solar system?"*
</Callout>

## The Story of the Atom – Historical Development

The idea that matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles is **thousands of years old**. But it took over **2,400 years** of debate, experimentation, and revolutionary thinking to get from ancient philosophy to the modern model of the atom.

### Ancient Ideas About Matter

<Timeline>
<TimelineItem date="~460–370 BCE" title="Democritus (ديموقريطس) – The First Atomist">
Greek philosopher **Democritus** proposed that all matter is made of **tiny, indivisible particles** he called ***atomos*** (Greek: ἄτομος = "uncuttable"). He believed atoms were:
- **Indestructible** – they cannot be created or destroyed
- **Infinite in number** – different shapes and sizes
- **Always in motion** – moving through empty space (the void)
- **Different for different substances** – water atoms are smooth and round; iron atoms are rough and hooked

Democritus had no experimental evidence – this was **pure philosophical reasoning**. But his core idea – that matter is not continuous but made of discrete particles – turned out to be remarkably correct.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="~384–322 BCE" title="Aristotle (أرسطو) – Rejection of Atoms">
Greek philosopher **Aristotle** rejected the idea of atoms and the void entirely. He believed:
- Matter is **continuous** – you can always divide it further
- All matter is made of **four elements**: earth, water, air, and fire
- There is **no empty space** (no vacuum) in nature

Because Aristotle was the most influential philosopher of the ancient world, his views dominated for **nearly 2,000 years** – pushing atomism into the shadows until the Scientific Revolution.
</TimelineItem>
</Timeline>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam point:** Democritus' idea was a **philosophical hypothesis**, not a scientific theory – he had no experimental evidence. Aristotle rejected it and his influence delayed the development of atomic theory by ~2,000 years. Science requires **evidence**, not just reasoning.
</Callout>

### Modern Atomic Theory – From Dalton to the Quantum Model

<Timeline>
<TimelineItem date="1803" title="John Dalton (جون دالتون) – The Solid Sphere Model">
English scientist **John Dalton** published the first **scientific atomic theory**, based on experimental evidence from studying chemical reactions and gas behaviour. His key postulates:

1. All matter is made of **atoms** – tiny, indivisible, indestructible particles
2. All atoms of the **same element** are identical (same mass and properties)
3. Atoms of **different elements** differ in mass and properties
4. Atoms combine in **simple, fixed whole-number ratios** to form compounds
5. In chemical reactions, atoms are **rearranged** – not created or destroyed

**Dalton's model:** The atom is a **solid, indivisible sphere** – like a billiard ball. No internal structure.

**What he got right:** Atoms exist, they combine in fixed ratios, and reactions rearrange atoms.
**What he got wrong:** Atoms are NOT indivisible – they have internal structure. Not all atoms of the same element have exactly the same mass (isotopes).
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1897" title="J.J. Thomson (طومسون) – Discovery of the Electron">
British physicist **Joseph John Thomson** discovered the **electron** using a **cathode ray tube** experiment. He observed that:

- When high voltage is applied across a gas at low pressure, a beam of particles (cathode rays) travels from the **cathode (−)** to the **anode (+)**
- These rays are **deflected by electric and magnetic fields**, proving they are charged particles
- The deflection showed the particles are **negatively charged**
- The **charge-to-mass ratio** ($e/m$) was the same regardless of the gas used – proving these particles exist in ALL atoms

Thomson calculated: $\frac{e}{m} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}$ C/kg

**Thomson's model: The Plum Pudding Model (نموذج حلوى البرقوق)**

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/Plum_pudding_atom.svg/400px-Plum_pudding_atom.svg.png" alt="Thomson's plum pudding model showing electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge" caption="Thomson's Plum Pudding Model – negative electrons (−) embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge (+), like raisins in a pudding" width="280px" />

The atom is a **sphere of positive charge** with **negative electrons embedded** throughout – like raisins (plums) scattered in a pudding. The positive and negative charges balance out, making the atom electrically neutral.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1909" title="Robert Millikan (ميليكان) – Oil Drop Experiment">
American physicist **Robert Millikan** measured the **exact charge of an electron** using his famous **oil drop experiment**:

$$e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$$

Combined with Thomson's $e/m$ ratio, this allowed calculation of the **mass of the electron**:

$$m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$$

This confirmed electrons are **incredibly light** – about $\frac{1}{1836}$ the mass of a hydrogen atom.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1911" title="Ernest Rutherford (رذرفورد) – The Gold Foil Experiment">
New Zealand-born physicist **Ernest Rutherford**, along with his students **Hans Geiger** and **Ernest Marsden**, performed the **gold foil experiment** – one of the most important experiments in the history of science.

**The experimental setup:**

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Geiger-Marsden_experiment.svg/500px-Geiger-Marsden_experiment.svg.png" alt="Diagram of the Geiger-Marsden gold foil experiment apparatus showing alpha particle source, gold foil, and detecting screen" caption="The gold foil experiment apparatus – alpha particles are fired at a thin gold foil, and a detecting screen records where they hit" width="380px" />

**Procedure:**
1. Fire a beam of **alpha particles** (α) at a very thin gold foil (only a few atoms thick)
2. Use a **fluorescent screen** around the foil to detect where the alpha particles land after hitting the gold
3. Count the flashes at different angles

**Expected results (based on Thomson's plum pudding model):**
If atoms are soft, uniform spheres of positive charge, all alpha particles should pass straight through with only slight deflections.

**Actual results:**

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Geiger-Marsden_experiment_expectation_and_result.svg/600px-Geiger-Marsden_experiment_expectation_and_result.svg.png" alt="Comparison of expected vs actual results of the gold foil experiment – expected: all pass through; actual: most pass through but some deflect at large angles and a few bounce back" caption="Left: what Thomson's model predicted (all particles pass through). Right: what actually happened (most pass through, some deflect, a few bounce back)" width="480px" />

- **Most** alpha particles (~99%) passed straight through the foil → most of the atom is **empty space**
- **Some** were deflected at **large angles** → they encountered something small but very positive and dense
- **A very few** (~1 in 20,000) **bounced straight back** → they hit something incredibly dense head-on

Rutherford famously said: *"It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."*

**Rutherford's Nuclear Model:**

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Rutherford_atom.svg/400px-Rutherford_atom.svg.png" alt="Rutherford's atomic model showing a small dense nucleus in the centre with electrons orbiting around it" caption="Rutherford's nuclear model – a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by electrons in mostly empty space" width="300px" />

- The atom has a **tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus** at the centre (containing most of the mass)
- **Electrons** orbit the nucleus at a distance, like planets orbiting the sun
- Most of the atom is **empty space**
- The nucleus is ~$10^{-15}$ m in diameter; the whole atom is ~$10^{-10}$ m – the nucleus is **100,000 times smaller** than the atom

**Limitation:** Rutherford's model couldn't explain **why electrons don't spiral into the nucleus** (a moving charged particle should radiate energy and collapse into the centre according to classical physics).
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1913" title="Niels Bohr (بور) – The Planetary Model">
Danish physicist **Niels Bohr** improved Rutherford's model by introducing the concept of **quantised energy levels**:

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Bohr_atom_model.svg/400px-Bohr_atom_model.svg.png" alt="Bohr model of the atom showing electrons in fixed circular orbits (energy levels) around the nucleus, with a photon being emitted when an electron drops to a lower level" caption="Bohr's model – electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels (n = 1, 2, 3...). When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, it emits a photon of specific energy" width="350px" />

**Key postulates:**
1. Electrons orbit the nucleus in **fixed circular paths** called **energy levels** (shells), numbered $n = 1, 2, 3, ...$
2. Each energy level has a **specific, quantised energy** – electrons can only exist at these specific distances, not in between
3. Electrons can **jump** between energy levels by **absorbing or emitting** photons of specific energy:

$$\Delta E = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}} = hf$$

where $h$ is Planck's constant and $f$ is the frequency of the emitted/absorbed photon.

4. Electrons in their lowest possible energy level are in the **ground state (الحالة المستقرة)**.

**What Bohr explained:** The hydrogen emission spectrum – the specific colours of light emitted by excited hydrogen atoms. His model correctly predicted the wavelengths of hydrogen's spectral lines.

**Limitation:** Bohr's model only works well for **hydrogen** (one electron). It fails for multi-electron atoms because it doesn't account for electron-electron repulsion.
</TimelineItem>
<TimelineItem date="1932" title="James Chadwick (تشادويك) – Discovery of the Neutron">
British physicist **James Chadwick** discovered the **neutron** – an electrically neutral particle inside the nucleus.

**The problem:** Scientists knew the mass of the nucleus was roughly **double** what could be explained by protons alone. For example, helium has 2 protons (mass ≈ 2 amu) but a nuclear mass of ≈ 4 amu. Something else was in the nucleus.

**The experiment:**
Chadwick bombarded **beryllium** with **alpha particles**. The beryllium emitted radiation that could knock protons out of paraffin wax. This radiation was electrically neutral (not deflected by electric or magnetic fields) but had significant mass. Chadwick identified these as **neutrons**.

$$^{9}_{4}\text{Be} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He} \rightarrow ^{12}_{6}\text{C} + ^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$

**Properties of the neutron:**
- Mass ≈ mass of a proton ($1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ kg ≈ 1 amu)
- Charge = **0** (neutral)
- Located in the **nucleus** alongside protons

The discovery of the neutron completed the picture of the **three fundamental subatomic particles**: proton, neutron, and electron.
</TimelineItem>
</Timeline>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam summary – Evolution of Atomic Models:**

| Scientist | Year | Model | Key Discovery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dalton** | 1803 | Solid sphere | Atoms exist; combine in fixed ratios |
| **Thomson** | 1897 | Plum pudding | Discovered electron; atom has internal structure |
| **Rutherford** | 1911 | Nuclear model | Tiny dense positive nucleus; mostly empty space |
| **Bohr** | 1913 | Planetary model | Quantised energy levels; electron orbits |
| **Chadwick** | 1932 | (Refined nuclear) | Discovered neutron |
</Callout>

## Atomic Structure

Now that we know the history, let's look at the **modern understanding** of atomic structure.

### Subatomic Particles

Every atom is made of three types of subatomic particles:

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Relative Charge | Mass (kg) | Relative Mass (amu) | Location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proton** | $p^+$ | $+1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C | +1 | $1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ | ~1 | Nucleus |
| **Neutron** | $n^0$ | 0 | 0 | $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ | ~1 | Nucleus |
| **Electron** | $e^-$ | $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C | −1 | $9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ | ~$\frac{1}{1836}$ ≈ 0 | Electron cloud |

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
- The **proton** and **neutron** have nearly the same mass (~1 amu each). Together they are called **nucleons** (نيوكليونات) because they reside in the nucleus.
- The **electron** is ~1,836 times lighter than a proton – so light that its mass is often approximated as **zero** in atomic mass calculations.
- The atom is electrically **neutral** because the number of protons (+) equals the number of electrons (−).
</Callout>

### Atomic Number and Mass Number

<Definition term="Atomic Number (العدد الذري) – Z">
The number of **protons** in the nucleus of an atom. The atomic number **defines** the element – change Z and you change the element. For a neutral atom, $Z$ also equals the number of electrons.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Mass Number (العدد الكتلي) – A">
The **total number of protons and neutrons** (nucleons) in the nucleus: $A = Z + N$, where $N$ is the number of neutrons.
</Definition>

### Notation

An atom is represented as:

$$^{A}_{Z}X$$

where $X$ is the element symbol, $A$ is the mass number (top), and $Z$ is the atomic number (bottom).

<Example>
**Carbon-12:** $^{12}_{6}\text{C}$
- Protons ($Z$) = 6
- Mass number ($A$) = 12
- Neutrons ($N$) = $A - Z = 12 - 6 = 6$
- Electrons = 6 (in a neutral atom)

**Oxygen-16:** $^{16}_{8}\text{O}$
- Protons = 8
- Neutrons = $16 - 8 = 8$
- Electrons = 8
</Example>

### Calculating Subatomic Particles

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Find the number of protons">
**Protons = Atomic number (Z)**

Look up the element on the periodic table. The atomic number IS the number of protons.
</Step>
<Step title="2. Find the number of neutrons">
**Neutrons = Mass number − Atomic number = A − Z**

Subtract the atomic number from the mass number.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Find the number of electrons">
**For a neutral atom:** Electrons = Protons = Z

**For an ion:** Electrons = Protons − Charge
- **Cation (كاتيون)** = positive ion → lost electrons → fewer electrons than protons
- **Anion (أنيون)** = negative ion → gained electrons → more electrons than protons
</Step>
</Steps>

### Ions – Cations and Anions

<Definition term="Ion (أيون)">
An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost one or more electrons, giving it a **net electrical charge**.
</Definition>

<Compare>
<Item label="Cation (كاتيون) – Positive Ion">
An atom that has **lost** one or more electrons.

**Result:** More protons than electrons → **positive charge**

**Example:** $\text{Na}^+$ – Sodium atom (11p, 11e) loses 1 electron → $\text{Na}^+$ (11p, 10e)

**Who forms cations?** Mainly **metals** – they have few valence electrons and lose them easily.
</Item>
<Item label="Anion (أنيون) – Negative Ion">
An atom that has **gained** one or more electrons.

**Result:** More electrons than protons → **negative charge**

**Example:** $\text{Cl}^-$ – Chlorine atom (17p, 17e) gains 1 electron → $\text{Cl}^-$ (17p, 18e)

**Who forms anions?** Mainly **non-metals** – they need a few more electrons to fill their outer shell.
</Item>
</Compare>

<Example>
Calculate the subatomic particles for $^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}^{3+}$ (Iron(III) ion):

- **Protons** = $Z$ = 26
- **Neutrons** = $A - Z$ = $56 - 26$ = 30
- **Electrons** = Protons − Charge = $26 - (+3)$ = 23

The $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ion has **lost 3 electrons**, so it has 23 electrons instead of 26.
</Example>

<Example>
Calculate the subatomic particles for $^{32}_{16}\text{S}^{2-}$ (Sulfide ion):

- **Protons** = 16
- **Neutrons** = $32 - 16$ = 16
- **Electrons** = $16 - (-2)$ = 18

The $\text{S}^{2-}$ ion has **gained 2 electrons**, so it has 18 electrons instead of 16.
</Example>

## Isotopes, Isobars & Isotones

### Isotopes

<Definition term="Isotopes (النظائر)">
Atoms of the **same element** that have the **same number of protons (same Z)** but a **different number of neutrons (different A)**. Isotopes have identical chemical properties but different physical properties (mass, density, radioactivity).
</Definition>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Key rule:** Same element → same protons → same electrons → **same chemical behaviour**. But different neutrons → different mass → **different physical properties**.
</Callout>

#### Hydrogen Isotopes – The Classic Example

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Mass Number | Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen-1 | $^{1}_{1}\text{H}$ | 1 | 0 | 1 | Protium (بروتيوم) |
| Hydrogen-2 | $^{2}_{1}\text{H}$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | Deuterium (ديوتيريوم) |
| Hydrogen-3 | $^{3}_{1}\text{H}$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | Tritium (تريتيوم) |

All three are hydrogen (1 proton each), but they differ in neutron count and mass.

#### Carbon Isotopes

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Stable? | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $^{12}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 6 | Yes | Reference standard for atomic mass |
| $^{13}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 7 | Yes | NMR spectroscopy |
| $^{14}_{6}\text{C}$ | 6 | 8 | **No** (radioactive) | Carbon dating (archaeology) |

#### Oxygen Isotopes

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Abundance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $^{16}_{8}\text{O}$ | 8 | 8 | 99.76% |
| $^{17}_{8}\text{O}$ | 8 | 9 | 0.04% |
| $^{18}_{8}\text{O}$ | 8 | 10 | 0.20% |

### Isobars

<Definition term="Isobars (متساويات الكتلة)">
Atoms of **different elements** that have the **same mass number (same A)** but a **different atomic number (different Z)**. They are different elements with the same total number of nucleons.
</Definition>

<Example>
$^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ and $^{40}_{19}\text{K}$ and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$

All three have mass number $A = 40$:
- Argon-40: 18 protons, 22 neutrons
- Potassium-40: 19 protons, 21 neutrons
- Calcium-40: 20 protons, 20 neutrons

Same total nucleons (40), but different elements with **completely different chemical properties**.
</Example>

### Isotones

<Definition term="Isotones (متساويات النيوترونات)">
Atoms of **different elements** that have the **same number of neutrons** but different atomic numbers and mass numbers.
</Definition>

<Example>
$^{13}_{6}\text{C}$ and $^{14}_{7}\text{N}$

- Carbon-13: 6 protons, **7 neutrons**
- Nitrogen-14: 7 protons, **7 neutrons**

Different elements, different mass numbers, but the **same number of neutrons**.
</Example>

<Example>
$^{30}_{14}\text{Si}$, $^{31}_{15}\text{P}$, and $^{32}_{16}\text{S}$

- Silicon-30: 14 protons, **16 neutrons**
- Phosphorus-31: 15 protons, **16 neutrons**
- Sulfur-32: 16 protons, **16 neutrons**

All have 16 neutrons → they are isotones.
</Example>

### Isoelectronic Species

<Definition term="Isoelectronic Species (الأنواع متساوية الإلكترونات)">
Atoms or ions that have the **same number of electrons**. They have different numbers of protons but identical electron configurations.
</Definition>

<Example>
$\text{Ne}$, $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{F}^-$, $\text{O}^{2-}$ all have **10 electrons**:

| Species | Protons | Electrons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\text{O}^{2-}$ | 8 | 10 |
| $\text{F}^-$ | 9 | 10 |
| $\text{Ne}$ | 10 | 10 |
| $\text{Na}^+$ | 11 | 10 |
| $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ | 12 | 10 |
</Example>

### Summary Comparison

| Concept | Same | Different |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Isotopes** | Protons (Z) | Neutrons (N), Mass number (A) |
| **Isobars** | Mass number (A) | Protons (Z), Neutrons (N) |
| **Isotones** | Neutrons (N) | Protons (Z), Mass number (A) |
| **Isoelectronic** | Electrons | Protons (Z) |

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam trap:** Don't confuse these four terms! Remember:
- Iso**topes** → same **protons** (same element)
- Iso**bars** → same mass number (**A**)
- Iso**tones** → same **neutrons**
- Iso**electronic** → same **electrons**
</Callout>

### Average Atomic Mass

The atomic mass listed on the periodic table is NOT the mass of any single atom – it's the **weighted average** of all naturally occurring isotopes, based on their relative abundance.

<Formula>

$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \sum (\text{mass of isotope} \times \text{fractional abundance})$$

</Formula>

<Example>
Chlorine has two naturally occurring isotopes:
- $^{35}\text{Cl}$: mass = 34.969 amu, abundance = 75.77%
- $^{37}\text{Cl}$: mass = 36.966 amu, abundance = 24.23%

$$\text{Average} = (34.969 \times 0.7577) + (36.966 \times 0.2423)$$
$$= 26.496 + 8.957 = 35.453 \text{ amu}$$

This matches the value on the periodic table: **35.45 amu**.
</Example>

<Example>
Boron has two naturally occurring isotopes:
- $^{10}\text{B}$: mass = 10.013 amu, abundance = 19.9%
- $^{11}\text{B}$: mass = 11.009 amu, abundance = 80.1%

$$\text{Average} = (10.013 \times 0.199) + (11.009 \times 0.801)$$
$$= 1.993 + 8.818 = 10.811 \text{ amu}$$

The periodic table lists boron as **10.81 amu** – a weighted average closer to $^{11}\text{B}$ because it's the more abundant isotope.
</Example>

## Radioactive Radiation – Alpha, Beta & Gamma

Some nuclei are **unstable** – they have too many or too few neutrons relative to protons, or they are simply too large. These unstable nuclei undergo **radioactive decay**, spontaneously emitting particles or energy to become more stable. The three main types of radiation are **alpha (α)**, **beta (β)**, and **gamma (γ)**.

### Alpha Radiation (إشعاع ألفا)

<Definition term="Alpha Particle (جسيم ألفا) – α">
A particle consisting of **2 protons and 2 neutrons** – identical to a **helium-4 nucleus** ($^{4}_{2}\text{He}$). It has a **charge of +2** and a **mass of 4 amu**.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/79/Alpha_Decay.svg/330px-Alpha_Decay.svg.png" alt="Diagram showing alpha decay – an alpha particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons) being ejected from a nucleus" caption="Alpha decay – the parent nucleus ejects an alpha particle (⁴₂He), reducing its atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4" width="300px" />

**Properties of alpha radiation:**
- **Charge:** +2
- **Mass:** 4 amu (heaviest of the three)
- **Speed:** ~5–7% the speed of light
- **Penetrating power:** **Very low** – stopped by a **sheet of paper** or a few centimetres of air
- **Ionising power:** **Very high** – strong ability to knock electrons off atoms (because of large charge and mass)
- **Deflection:** Deflected by electric and magnetic fields (toward the negative plate)

**Alpha decay equation – General form:**

$$^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

The parent atom **loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons**, so the **atomic number drops by 2** (new element!) and the **mass number drops by 4**.

<Example>
**Uranium-238 alpha decay:**

$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$

Uranium (Z=92) becomes thorium (Z=90). The atomic number decreased by 2, mass number by 4.
</Example>

### Beta Radiation (إشعاع بيتا)

<Definition term="Beta Particle (جسيم بيتا) – β⁻">
A **high-speed electron** emitted from the **nucleus** when a **neutron converts into a proton**. It has a **charge of −1** and **negligible mass**.
</Definition>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Beta-minus_Decay.svg/330px-Beta-minus_Decay.svg.png" alt="Diagram showing beta-minus decay – a neutron converting to a proton and emitting an electron and antineutrino" caption="Beta-minus decay – a neutron in the nucleus converts to a proton, emitting a high-speed electron (β⁻) and an antineutrino" width="300px" />

**Properties of beta radiation:**
- **Charge:** −1
- **Mass:** ~$\frac{1}{1836}$ amu (negligible compared to nucleons)
- **Speed:** Up to ~99% the speed of light
- **Penetrating power:** **Medium** – stopped by a few millimetres of **aluminium** or a few metres of air
- **Ionising power:** **Medium** – less than alpha (smaller charge and mass) but more than gamma
- **Deflection:** Deflected by electric and magnetic fields (toward the positive plate – opposite to alpha)

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Key concept:** The beta particle (electron) comes from **inside the nucleus**, NOT from the electron cloud. A neutron converts into a proton and an electron:

$$^{1}_{0}n \rightarrow ^{1}_{1}p + ^{0}_{-1}e + \bar{\nu}_e$$

The $\bar{\nu}_e$ is an antineutrino – a nearly massless, chargeless particle emitted alongside the electron.
</Callout>

**Beta decay equation – General form:**

$$^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow ^{A}_{Z+1}Y + ^{0}_{-1}e$$

The mass number stays the **same** (a neutron became a proton – total nucleons unchanged), but the **atomic number increases by 1** (one more proton → new element).

<Example>
**Carbon-14 beta decay:**

$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{0}_{-1}e$$

Carbon-14 (Z=6) becomes nitrogen-14 (Z=7). Mass number unchanged, atomic number up by 1.
</Example>

### Gamma Radiation (إشعاع جاما)

<Definition term="Gamma Ray (أشعة جاما) – γ">
A high-energy **electromagnetic wave** (photon) emitted from an excited nucleus. Gamma radiation has **no charge** and **no mass** – it is pure energy.
</Definition>

**Properties of gamma radiation:**
- **Charge:** 0
- **Mass:** 0
- **Speed:** Speed of light ($3 \times 10^8$ m/s)
- **Penetrating power:** **Very high** – requires several centimetres of **lead** or metres of **concrete** to significantly reduce intensity
- **Ionising power:** **Very low** (no charge, no mass – interacts weakly with matter)
- **Deflection:** **NOT deflected** by electric or magnetic fields (no charge)

**Gamma emission:**

$$^{A}_{Z}X^* \rightarrow ^{A}_{Z}X + \gamma$$

The asterisk (*) indicates an **excited nuclear state**. Gamma emission does NOT change the atomic number or mass number – it just releases excess energy. Gamma rays often accompany alpha or beta decay.

### Comparison of the Three Types

| Property | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Identity** | $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ nucleus | $^{0}_{-1}e$ (electron) | Electromagnetic wave |
| **Charge** | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| **Mass** | 4 amu | ~0 amu | 0 |
| **Speed** | 5–7% of $c$ | Up to 99% of $c$ | $c$ (speed of light) |
| **Penetration** | Low (paper stops it) | Medium (aluminium stops it) | High (lead/concrete needed) |
| **Ionising power** | **Highest** | Medium | **Lowest** |
| **Deflection in fields** | Yes (toward −) | Yes (toward +) | No |
| **Effect on Z** | Z − 2 | Z + 1 | No change |
| **Effect on A** | A − 4 | No change | No change |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam memory trick:** Think of it as a trade-off:
- **High mass + charge** → high ionising power but low penetration (alpha can't get far because it's large and interacts strongly)
- **No mass + no charge** → low ionising power but high penetration (gamma zips through matter because it barely interacts)
- **Beta** is in the **middle** for everything
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam-critical:** In **ALL nuclear equations**, both the **mass numbers** (top) and the **atomic numbers** (bottom) must **balance** on both sides. This is the law of conservation of mass and charge.

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms – LO 2">
<Term word="Atom (الذرة)">The smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element, consisting of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Atomic Number (العدد الذري) – Z">The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Defines the element.</Term>
<Term word="Mass Number (العدد الكتلي) – A">The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus: A = Z + N.</Term>
<Term word="Proton (البروتون)">A positively charged subatomic particle in the nucleus. Charge = +1, mass ≈ 1 amu.</Term>
<Term word="Neutron (النيوترون)">An electrically neutral subatomic particle in the nucleus. Charge = 0, mass ≈ 1 amu.</Term>
<Term word="Electron (الإلكترون)">A negatively charged subatomic particle in the electron cloud outside the nucleus. Charge = −1, mass ≈ 0 amu.</Term>
<Term word="Nucleon (النيوكليون)">A proton or neutron – any particle found in the nucleus.</Term>
<Term word="Nucleus (النواة)">The tiny, dense, positively charged centre of an atom, containing protons and neutrons.</Term>
<Term word="Ion (أيون)">An atom or group of atoms with a net electrical charge due to loss or gain of electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Cation (كاتيون)">A positively charged ion formed by the loss of one or more electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Anion (أنيون)">A negatively charged ion formed by the gain of one or more electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Isotopes (النظائر)">Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons (same Z, different A).</Term>
<Term word="Isobars (متساويات الكتلة)">Atoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers (same A, different Z).</Term>
<Term word="Isotones (متساويات النيوترونات)">Atoms of different elements with the same number of neutrons but different atomic numbers.</Term>
<Term word="Isoelectronic (متساوي الإلكترونات)">Species (atoms or ions) that have the same number of electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Average Atomic Mass (الكتلة الذرية المتوسطة)">The weighted average mass of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, based on their relative abundances.</Term>
<Term word="Alpha Particle (جسيم ألفا – α)">A particle identical to a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2, mass 4 amu.</Term>
<Term word="Beta Particle (جسيم بيتا – β)">A high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus when a neutron converts to a proton, charge −1.</Term>
<Term word="Gamma Ray (أشعة جاما – γ)">A high-energy electromagnetic wave emitted from an excited nucleus, no charge, no mass.</Term>
<Term word="Radioactive Decay (التحلل الإشعاعي)">The spontaneous emission of particles or energy from an unstable nucleus to become more stable.</Term>
<Term word="Cathode Ray (أشعة المهبط)">A beam of electrons emitted from the cathode in a discharge tube under low pressure and high voltage.</Term>
<Term word="Plum Pudding Model (نموذج حلوى البرقوق)">Thomson's model – electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge, later disproved by Rutherford.</Term>
<Term word="Nuclear Model (النموذج النووي)">Rutherford's model – a tiny dense positive nucleus surrounded by orbiting electrons in mostly empty space.</Term>
<Term word="Energy Level (مستوى الطاقة)">A fixed, quantised orbit around the nucleus where electrons can exist (Bohr model).</Term>
<Term word="Ground State (الحالة المستقرة)">The lowest energy state of an electron in an atom.</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Democritus** proposed atoms (philosophical, no evidence). **Aristotle** rejected them. Science stalled for ~2,000 years.</Point>
<Point>**Dalton** (1803): atoms are solid, indivisible spheres. **Thomson** (1897): discovered electrons via cathode rays → plum pudding model.</Point>
<Point>**Millikan** measured the electron charge ($e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C). Combined with Thomson's $e/m$ → electron mass calculated.</Point>
<Point>**Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1911):** Most α particles pass through (empty space), some deflect (positive nucleus), very few bounce back (dense nucleus). Disproved Thomson's model.</Point>
<Point>**Bohr** (1913): electrons orbit in quantised energy levels. Explained hydrogen's emission spectrum. Fails for multi-electron atoms.</Point>
<Point>**Chadwick** (1932): discovered the neutron by bombarding beryllium with alpha particles.</Point>
<Point>**Three subatomic particles:** Proton (+1, ~1 amu, nucleus), Neutron (0, ~1 amu, nucleus), Electron (−1, ~0 amu, electron cloud).</Point>
<Point>**Atomic number (Z)** = protons. **Mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons. **Neutrons = A − Z**. For neutral atoms, electrons = protons.</Point>
<Point>**Cations** = lost electrons (positive). **Anions** = gained electrons (negative). Electrons = Z − charge.</Point>
<Point>**Isotopes** = same Z, different A. **Isobars** = same A, different Z. **Isotones** = same N. **Isoelectronic** = same electrons.</Point>
<Point>**Average atomic mass** = weighted average of isotope masses × their fractional abundances.</Point>
<Point>**Alpha (α):** $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, charge +2, lowest penetration (paper), highest ionisation. Z drops by 2, A drops by 4.</Point>
<Point>**Beta (β):** $^{0}_{-1}e$, charge −1, medium penetration (aluminium). Z increases by 1, A unchanged.</Point>
<Point>**Gamma (γ):** EM wave, no charge/mass, highest penetration (lead/concrete), lowest ionisation. No change to Z or A.</Point>
<Point>**Nuclear equations must balance:** Total mass numbers and total atomic numbers must be equal on both sides.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
