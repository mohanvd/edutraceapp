# Electron Configuration & Periodic Trends

<Callout type="info" emoji="🧪">
This LO covers the **periodic table's structure**, **electron configuration**, and the **trends** that make the periodic table one of the most powerful tools in all of science. Learn how atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity, and electron affinity change across periods and down groups – and **why**. Master this, and you'll be able to **predict** the behaviour of elements you've never even studied.
</Callout>

<Callout type="default" emoji="❓">
**Essential Question:** *"What are the ways that people can sort a variety of things?"*
</Callout>

## Electron Configuration – The Seating Chart of Electrons

Imagine a concert hall with rows of seats at different heights. The **cheapest seats** (lowest energy) fill first. That's exactly how electrons arrange themselves around the nucleus. **Electron configuration** describes precisely where every electron in an atom "lives."

<Definition term="Electron Configuration (التوزيع الإلكتروني)">
A notation that shows how electrons are distributed among the various atomic orbitals of an atom. In the **ground state** (lowest energy, most stable state), electrons occupy the lowest available energy levels first.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Orbital (المدار)">
A region in space around the nucleus where an electron is most likely to be found. Each orbital can hold a **maximum of 2 electrons** with opposite spins.
</Definition>

<Compare>
<Item label="Ground State (الحالة المستقرة)">
The **lowest energy state** of an atom – the most stable arrangement. Electrons fill the lowest available orbitals first.

This is the "default" state of every atom. When we write electron configurations, we almost always mean the ground state.
</Item>
<Item label="Excited State (الحالة المثارة)">
Any energy state **above** the ground state. An atom becomes excited by **absorbing energy** (heat, light, electricity), causing an electron to jump to a higher energy orbital.

This state is **temporary** – the electron will quickly fall back down, releasing the absorbed energy as light (this is how neon signs and fireworks work!).
</Item>
</Compare>

### The Three Rules of Electron "Seating"

Three fundamental rules govern how electrons fill orbitals – break any of them, and the configuration is wrong.

<Steps>
<Step title="1. Aufbau Principle (مبدأ البناء التصاعدي)">
**"Fill the cheapest seats first."**

Electrons are added one at a time to the **lowest energy orbitals available** before filling higher energy ones. The word "Aufbau" is German for "building up."

**The Aufbau filling order:**

$$1s \rightarrow 2s \rightarrow 2p \rightarrow 3s \rightarrow 3p \rightarrow 4s \rightarrow 3d \rightarrow 4p \rightarrow 5s \rightarrow 4d \rightarrow 5p \rightarrow 6s \rightarrow 4f \rightarrow 5d \rightarrow 6p \rightarrow 7s \rightarrow 5f \rightarrow 6d$$

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Aufbau_Principle-en.svg/960px-Aufbau_Principle-en.svg.png" alt="Aufbau principle diagonal filling order diagram showing arrows crossing energy levels 1s through 7s, indicating the order in which orbitals are filled" caption="The Aufbau diagonal rule – follow the arrows from top to bottom to get the correct filling order. States crossed by the same arrow have the same (n + l) value" width="350px" />

Notice that **4s fills before 3d** because 4s has lower energy. This is a common exam point!
</Step>
<Step title="2. Pauli Exclusion Principle (مبدأ باولي للاستبعاد)">
**"No two electrons can have the same address."**

No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers. The practical result: an orbital can hold a **maximum of 2 electrons**, and they must have **opposite spins** (↑↓).

Think of it like a seat on a bus – maximum two people per seat, and they must face opposite directions.
</Step>
<Step title="3. Hund's Rule (قاعدة هوند)">
**"Take empty seats before sitting next to someone."**

When filling orbitals of **equal energy** (like the three p orbitals or five d orbitals), electrons occupy each orbital **singly** first, all with the **same spin**, before any orbital gets a second electron.

Just like passengers spreading out on a bus – everyone takes their own seat before doubling up.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam-critical:** Each **subshell** holds a specific maximum number of electrons:
- **s subshell:** 1 orbital → **2 electrons** max
- **p subshell:** 3 orbitals → **6 electrons** max
- **d subshell:** 5 orbitals → **10 electrons** max
- **f subshell:** 7 orbitals → **14 electrons** max
</Callout>

### Writing Electron Configurations

There are two ways to write electron configurations:

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Full Configuration">
Lists **every** occupied orbital with the number of electrons as a superscript.

**Examples:**
- **Hydrogen (H, Z=1):** $1s^1$
- **Helium (He, Z=2):** $1s^2$
- **Carbon (C, Z=6):** $1s^2 \, 2s^2 \, 2p^2$
- **Sodium (Na, Z=11):** $1s^2 \, 2s^2 \, 2p^6 \, 3s^1$
- **Calcium (Ca, Z=20):** $1s^2 \, 2s^2 \, 2p^6 \, 3s^2 \, 3p^6 \, 4s^2$
- **Arsenic (As, Z=33):** $1s^2 \, 2s^2 \, 2p^6 \, 3s^2 \, 3p^6 \, 4s^2 \, 3d^{10} \, 4p^3$
</Tab>
<Tab label="Noble Gas Core (Abbreviated)">
Uses the **previous noble gas** symbol in brackets to represent all filled inner shells, then writes only the **outer (valence) electrons**.

**Examples:**
- **Sodium (Na, Z=11):** $[\text{Ne}] \, 3s^1$ - Neon has 10 electrons, Na has 11
- **Calcium (Ca, Z=20):** $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2$ - Argon has 18 electrons, Ca has 20
- **Arsenic (As, Z=33):** $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2 \, 3d^{10} \, 4p^3$
- **Iron (Fe, Z=26):** $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2 \, 3d^6$

This shorthand is faster and highlights the **valence electrons** that determine chemical behaviour.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

### Orbital Diagrams – Visualising Hund's Rule

Orbital diagrams use **boxes** (or lines) for orbitals and **arrows** for electrons. An up arrow (↑) = spin +½, down arrow (↓) = spin −½.

<Example>
**Building up from Hydrogen to Neon:**

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **H** | 1 | $1s^1$ | (↑)( )( _ _ _ ) |
| **He** | 2 | $1s^2$ | (↑↓)( )( _ _ _ ) |
| **Li** | 3 | $1s^2 2s^1$ | (↑↓)(↑)( _ _ _ ) |
| **Be** | 4 | $1s^2 2s^2$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)( _ _ _ ) |
| **B** | 5 | $1s^2 2s^2 2p^1$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)(↑ _ _ ) |
| **C** | 6 | $1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)(↑ ↑ _ ) |
| **N** | 7 | $1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)(↑ ↑ ↑) |
| **O** | 8 | $1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)(↑↓ ↑ ↑) |
| **F** | 9 | $1s^2 2s^2 2p^5$ | (↑↓)(↑↓)(↑↓ ↑↓ ↑) |

**Notice Hund's Rule in action at Carbon and Nitrogen:** electrons spread out into separate p orbitals (all with the same spin ↑) before any orbital gets a second electron. Oxygen is when pairing begins.
</Example>

### Exceptions to the Aufbau Rule – Special Stability

Some elements "cheat" because having a **half-filled** ($d^5$) or **completely filled** ($d^{10}$) d subshell provides **extra stability**. The energy gained from this symmetry outweighs the cost of moving an electron from the s orbital.

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**The two exceptions you MUST know:**

| Element | Expected | Actual | Why? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Chromium (Cr, Z=24)** | $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2 \, 3d^4$ | $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^1 \, 3d^5$ | **Half-filled d** subshell ($d^5$) is extra stable |
| **Copper (Cu, Z=29)** | $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2 \, 3d^9$ | $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^1 \, 3d^{10}$ | **Fully-filled d** subshell ($d^{10}$) is extra stable |

In both cases, one electron "moves" from the 4s to the 3d to achieve the special stability of a half-filled or fully-filled d subshell.
</Callout>

### Electron Configurations of Ions

<Compare>
<Item label="Cations (كاتيونات) – Positive Ions">
Formed by **losing** electrons. Electrons are always removed from the **outermost shell** (highest *n* value) **first**.

**Critical rule for transition metals:** Remove the *ns* electrons **before** the *(n−1)d* electrons, even though the *ns* filled first!

**Example – Iron:**
- Fe (Z=26): $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^2 \, 3d^6$
- Fe²⁺: Remove 2 electrons from 4s → $[\text{Ar}] \, 3d^6$
- Fe³⁺: Remove 2 from 4s + 1 from 3d → $[\text{Ar}] \, 3d^5$
</Item>
<Item label="Anions (أنيونات) – Negative Ions">
Formed by **gaining** electrons. Electrons are added to the **highest energy orbital** that is not yet full.

**Example – Chlorine:**
- Cl (Z=17): $[\text{Ne}] \, 3s^2 \, 3p^5$
- Cl⁻: Add 1 electron to 3p → $[\text{Ne}] \, 3s^2 \, 3p^6$

The chloride ion now has the same electron configuration as **Argon** – a full outer shell!
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Exam trap:** For transition metal cations, always remove electrons from the **4s before the 3d** (or 5s before 4d, etc.). Even though 4s fills before 3d in neutral atoms, it empties first when forming ions. Think of it as "last in relative energy, first out."
</Callout>

## The Modern Periodic Table

The periodic table is the **single most useful tool** in all of chemistry. Dmitri Mendeleev created the first widely accepted version in 1869 because of its **predictive power** – he left gaps for elements not yet discovered and correctly predicted their properties. The modern periodic table arranges elements by **atomic number** (not atomic mass).

### Structure – Groups & Periods

<Compare>
<Item label="Groups (المجموعات) – Vertical Columns">
Elements in the same **group** (column) share the **same number of valence electrons** and therefore have **similar chemical properties**.

- Numbered **1–18** in the modern IUPAC system
- The group number for main-group elements (Groups 1, 2, 13–18) tells you the number of valence electrons
- Group 1 → 1 valence electron, Group 2 → 2, Group 13 → 3, … Group 18 → 8 (except He with 2)
</Item>
<Item label="Periods (الدورات) – Horizontal Rows">
Elements in the same **period** (row) have the **same number of electron shells** (same principal energy level *n*).

- Numbered **1–7**
- Period 1 → 1 shell, Period 2 → 2 shells, Period 3 → 3 shells, etc.
- As you move across a period left to right, the atomic number increases by 1
</Item>
</Compare>

### The Four Blocks

The periodic table is divided into **four blocks** based on which subshell is being filled:

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Simple_Periodic_Table_Chart-blocks.svg/1280px-Simple_Periodic_Table_Chart-blocks.svg.png" alt="Periodic table colour-coded to show the s-block (red), p-block (yellow), d-block (blue), and f-block (green)" caption="The four blocks of the periodic table – colour-coded by the subshell being filled: s-block (Groups 1–2), d-block (Groups 3–12), p-block (Groups 13–18), and f-block (lanthanides and actinides)" width="600px" />

| Block | Location | Subshell Being Filled | Groups |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **s-block** | Far left (+ He) | s orbitals | Groups 1–2 |
| **p-block** | Right side | p orbitals | Groups 13–18 |
| **d-block** | Centre | d orbitals | Groups 3–12 |
| **f-block** | Bottom (lanthanides & actinides) | f orbitals | Pulled out below |

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
**Quick trick:** The block tells you the **last subshell** being filled. If an element is in the d-block, the outermost electrons being added are in a d orbital. If it's in the p-block, a p orbital is being filled.
</Callout>

### Element Families – The Periodic Table's "Neighbourhoods"

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Alkali Metals">
**Group 1** (except hydrogen)
- **Valence electrons:** 1
- **Properties:** Soft, silvery, extremely **reactive** – react violently with water, never found pure in nature
- **Trend:** Reactivity **increases** going down the group (Cs is more reactive than Na)
- Stored under kerosene/oil in the lab to prevent reaction with air and moisture
- **Examples:** Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr
</Tab>
<Tab label="Alkaline Earth Metals">
**Group 2**
- **Valence electrons:** 2
- **Properties:** Reactive metals, but **less** reactive than alkali metals. Harder and denser than Group 1
- **Trend:** Reactivity **increases** going down the group
- Also not found in pure form in nature
- **Examples:** Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra
</Tab>
<Tab label="Transition Metals">
**Groups 3–12**
- **Characteristic:** Outermost electrons fill **d orbitals**
- **Properties:** Hard, high melting points, good conductors, often colourful compounds
- Can form ions with **multiple charges** (e.g., Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺) – hence "transition"
- Include many familiar metals: iron, copper, gold, silver, zinc
</Tab>
<Tab label="Halogens">
**Group 17**
- **Valence electrons:** 7
- **Properties:** Very reactive **non-metals**, often colourful and corrosive
- The name "halogen" means **"salt former"** (they readily react with metals to form salts like NaCl)
- **Trend:** Reactivity **decreases** going down the group (F is more reactive than I)
- **Examples:** F (gas), Cl (gas), Br (liquid), I (solid) – note the change in state!
</Tab>
<Tab label="Noble Gases">
**Group 18**
- **Valence electrons:** 8 (He has 2) – **full outer shell**
- **Properties:** Colourless, odourless, **extremely unreactive** (inert)
- Electronegativity and electron affinity are effectively **zero**
- Ionization energy is the **highest** in their period
- **Examples:** He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn
</Tab>
<Tab label="Metalloids">
**The Zigzag Line**
- Elements along the staircase line separating metals from non-metals: **B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te**
- **Properties intermediate** between metals and non-metals
- Some are **semiconductors** – essential for electronics (silicon chips!)
- May conduct electricity under certain conditions but not others
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## Periodic Trends – The Predictive Power

The real magic of the periodic table is that properties change **predictably** across periods and down groups. Understanding **why** these trends exist (based on nuclear charge and electron shielding) means you never have to memorise individual values.

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**The two key forces behind ALL periodic trends:**

1. **Nuclear charge (الشحنة النووية):** More protons → stronger pull on electrons → electrons held tighter
2. **Electron shielding (الحجب الإلكتروني):** Inner electrons shield outer electrons from the full nuclear charge. More shells → more shielding → outer electrons held less tightly

**Effective nuclear charge** = Nuclear charge − Shielding. This is the "net pull" felt by the outermost electrons, and it drives every trend.
</Callout>

### Metallic vs Non-metallic Character

| Property | Across a Period (→) | Down a Group (↓) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Metallic character** | **Decreases** | **Increases** |
| **Non-metallic character** | **Increases** | **Decreases** |

The most metallic element is **Francium (Fr)** – bottom left. The most non-metallic element is **Fluorine (F)** – top right (excluding noble gases).

### Atomic Radius – How Big is the Atom?

<Definition term="Atomic Radius (نصف القطر الذري)">
Half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms of the same element. It measures the **size** of an atom.
</Definition>

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Down a Group ↓">
**Atomic radius INCREASES ↓**

**Why?** Each new period adds a **new electron shell**. More shells = larger atom. It's like adding another layer to an onion.

$$\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K} < \text{Rb} < \text{Cs}$$
</Tab>
<Tab label="Across a Period →">
**Atomic radius DECREASES →**

**Why?** As you move right, the **number of protons increases** (higher nuclear charge) but electrons are added to the **same shell**. The increased nuclear pull draws all electrons closer to the nucleus, shrinking the atom.

$$\text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Al} > \text{Si} > \text{P} > \text{S} > \text{Cl} > \text{Ar}$$
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4e/Atomic_number_to_radius_graph.png" alt="Graph of atomic radius versus atomic number showing periodic peaks at alkali metals and valleys at noble gases" caption="Atomic radius vs atomic number – notice how the radius peaks at each alkali metal (start of a new period/shell) and decreases across each period as nuclear charge increases" width="500px" />

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
**Memory trick:** Atomic radius increases toward the **bottom-left** corner of the periodic table. The **largest** atom is Cesium (Cs) or Francium (Fr). The **smallest** is Helium (He).

**One arrow:** ↙ = radius increases
</Callout>

### Ionic Radius – How Big is the Ion?

<Compare>
<Item label="Cations – Smaller Than Parent Atom">
Losing electrons means:
- Fewer electrons → **less electron-electron repulsion**
- Often an entire shell is lost (e.g., Na → Na⁺ loses the 3rd shell entirely)
- The **same nuclear charge** now pulls on **fewer electrons** → stronger pull → smaller radius

**Example:** Na (atomic radius ≈ 186 pm) → Na⁺ (ionic radius ≈ 95 pm) – almost **half** the size!
</Item>
<Item label="Anions – Larger Than Parent Atom">
Gaining electrons means:
- More electrons → **more electron-electron repulsion**
- The **same nuclear charge** pulls on **more electrons** → weaker pull per electron → larger radius

**Example:** Cl (atomic radius ≈ 99 pm) → Cl⁻ (ionic radius ≈ 181 pm) – nearly **double** the size!
</Item>
</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam-critical – Comparing isoelectronic species** (ions with the same number of electrons):

For ions with the **same electron count**, the one with **more protons** is **smaller** (greater nuclear charge pulling on the same number of electrons).

**Example:** O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ all have **10 electrons**:

| Species | Protons | Electrons | Relative Size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O²⁻ | 8 | 10 | **Largest** (fewest protons pulling 10 e⁻) |
| F⁻ | 9 | 10 | ↑ |
| Ne | 10 | 10 | Middle |
| Na⁺ | 11 | 10 | ↓ |
| Mg²⁺ | 12 | 10 | **Smallest** (most protons pulling 10 e⁻) |

**Rule:** More protons + same electrons = stronger attraction = **smaller** ion.
</Callout>

### Ionization Energy – How Hard is it to Rip an Electron Away?

<Definition term="Ionization Energy (طاقة التأين)">
The energy required to remove the **most loosely held electron** from a **gaseous atom** in its ground state. It's always a **positive** value (energy must be absorbed). Higher ionization energy = harder to remove an electron.

$$X_{(g)} \rightarrow X^+_{(g)} + e^- \quad \Delta H > 0$$
</Definition>

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Exam point:** Ionization energy must involve a **gaseous** atom. If the question shows a liquid or solid state, the answer is wrong. Also, ionization energy removes an electron – it does NOT add one (that's electron affinity).
</Callout>

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Down a Group ↓">
**Ionization energy DECREASES ↓**

**Why?** The outermost electron is **farther from the nucleus** (more shells = more distance + more shielding). It's easier to remove because the nuclear attraction is weaker.

Think of it like holding a dog on a longer leash – easier for someone to take it away!
</Tab>
<Tab label="Across a Period →">
**Ionization energy INCREASES →**

**Why?** Increasing nuclear charge (more protons) holds the electrons **more tightly** in the same shell. More protons pulling on electrons in the same shell = harder to remove.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/First_Ionization_Energy_blocks.svg" alt="Graph of first ionization energy versus atomic number, colour-coded by s, p, d, and f blocks, showing the periodic trend" caption="First ionization energy vs atomic number – notice the peaks at noble gases (hardest to ionize) and valleys at alkali metals (easiest to ionize). The P > S and N > O exceptions are visible as small dips" width="550px" />

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
**Memory trick:** Ionization energy increases toward the **top-right** corner. The trend arrow points ↗.

Noble gases have the **highest** ionization energies in their period (full, stable shells are hard to break apart).
</Callout>

#### Successive Ionization Energies

Each time you remove an electron, the **next one is harder** to remove. Why? Same nuclear charge pulling on fewer electrons = stronger pull on each remaining electron.

<Example>
**Successive ionization energies of Sodium (Na, Z=11):**

| IE | Value (kJ/mol) | Electron Removed From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | 496 | 3s (valence) |
| 2nd | 4,562 | 2p (core) |
| 3rd | 6,912 | 2p (core) |

**Notice the HUGE jump** between the 1st and 2nd IE – from 496 to 4,562 kJ/mol! This tells us:
- Na has **1 valence electron** (easy to remove)
- After that, you're breaking into the **core electrons** (stable, full inner shell – extremely hard)
- The big jump always occurs **after all valence electrons are removed**

**Rule:** The position of the dramatic jump in successive IEs tells you the **number of valence electrons** → confirms the element's group.
</Example>

#### The Phosphorus > Sulfur Exception

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Phosphorus (P)** has a **higher** first ionization energy than **Sulfur (S)**, even though S is to its right. Why?

- P has a **half-filled** 3p subshell: $3p^3$ → (↑)(↑)(↑) – extra stable
- S has one paired electron: $3p^4$ → (↑↓)(↑)(↑) – the paired electron experiences repulsion, making it slightly easier to remove

This same exception occurs with **N > O** in Period 2. Half-filled subshells have special stability!
</Callout>

### Electron Affinity – How Much Does an Atom *Want* an Electron?

<Definition term="Electron Affinity (الألفة الإلكترونية)">
The energy change that occurs when an atom in the **gaseous state gains one electron**. A **more negative** value indicates a **greater tendency** to gain an electron (more energy released).

$$X_{(g)} + e^- \rightarrow X^-_{(g)} \quad \Delta H < 0 \text{ (usually)}$$
</Definition>

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Down a Group ↓">
**Electron affinity DECREASES (less negative) ↓**

**Why?** The new electron is added **farther from the nucleus** (more shells, more shielding). The atom "wants" it less because the nuclear attraction is weaker at greater distance.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Across a Period →">
**Electron affinity INCREASES (more negative) →**

**Why?** Higher nuclear charge attracts the incoming electron more strongly. Non-metals near the right side of the table are close to a full shell and "desperately want" that extra electron.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7e/Electron_affinities_of_the_elements.png" alt="Graph showing electron affinity values for elements across the periodic table, with halogens having the most negative values" caption="Electron affinity vs atomic number – halogens (Group 17) show the most negative values (highest tendency to gain electrons), while noble gases and alkaline earth metals have near-zero values" width="550px" />

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚠️">
**Key exceptions:**
- **Noble gases:** Electron affinity ≈ **zero**. Their outer shell is already full – no room or desire for more electrons.
- **Beryllium and Magnesium:** Electron affinity ≈ **zero** because their s subshell is fully filled ($s^2$). Adding an electron would mean starting a new, higher-energy subshell.
- **Chlorine > Fluorine:** Chlorine has a **higher** (more negative) electron affinity than fluorine! Fluorine is so small that adding an electron causes intense **electron-electron repulsion** in its tiny 2p orbitals.

**Group 17 (Halogens)** have the most negative electron affinities overall – they're one electron away from a noble gas configuration!
</Callout>

### Electronegativity – The Tug-of-War for Shared Electrons

<Definition term="Electronegativity (الكهروسالبية)">
A measure of an atom's ability to **attract shared electrons** toward itself in a **chemical bond**. Measured on the **Pauling scale** (relative values, no unit). Higher electronegativity = stronger pull on shared electrons.
</Definition>

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Down a Group ↓">
**Electronegativity DECREASES ↓**

**Why?** More electron shells → bonding electrons are farther from the nucleus → weaker attraction.
</Tab>
<Tab label="Across a Period →">
**Electronegativity INCREASES →**

**Why?** More protons → stronger pull on the shared bonding electrons → higher electronegativity.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**Key facts to remember:**
- **Fluorine** is the **most electronegative** element (EN = 4.0)
- **Francium** (or Cesium among stable elements) is the **least electronegative**
- **Noble gases** are generally assigned **zero** electronegativity (they rarely form bonds, so the concept doesn't apply)
- Electronegativity follows the **same trend direction** as ionization energy and electron affinity: **↗ (increases toward top-right)**
</Callout>

<Example>
**Electronegativity in action – HCl:**

In a molecule of HCl, chlorine (EN = 3.16) pulls the shared electrons **toward itself** much more strongly than hydrogen (EN = 2.20). This creates a **polar bond** – the chlorine side becomes partially negative (δ⁻) and the hydrogen side becomes partially positive (δ⁺).

The **electronegativity difference** (ΔEN) between bonded atoms tells you the bond type:
- ΔEN = 0 → **nonpolar covalent** (equal sharing)
- 0 < ΔEN < 1.7 → **polar covalent** (unequal sharing)
- ΔEN ≥ 1.7 → **ionic** (electron transfer)
</Example>

### Noble Gases – Why They're "Special" in Trends

<Callout type="default" emoji="💡">
Noble gases (Group 18) are often **excluded** from trend discussions for electronegativity and electron affinity. Here's why:

- Their outer shell is **completely filled** (He: 2 electrons, others: 8 electrons)
- They have **no tendency** to gain, lose, or share electrons
- This gives them:
  - **Electronegativity:** Effectively **zero** (no bonds to pull electrons in)
  - **Electron Affinity:** Effectively **zero** (no desire for extra electrons)
  - **Ionization Energy:** The **highest** in their period (hard to break a full, stable shell)
- Their inertness is why they're set apart when analysing most trends
</Callout>

### Summary of All Trends – One Diagram

<ImageWithText src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fe/Periodic_trends.svg" alt="Periodic table with arrows showing the direction of all major trends: atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity" caption="All major periodic trends at a glance – notice that atomic radius goes in the opposite direction (↙) from ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity (↗)" width="500px" />

| Property | Across Period (→) | Down Group (↓) | Trend Arrow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Atomic Radius** | Decreases | Increases | ↙ |
| **Ionic Radius** (for same charge type) | Decreases | Increases | ↙ |
| **Ionization Energy** | Increases | Decreases | ↗ |
| **Electron Affinity** (magnitude) | Increases | Decreases | ↗ |
| **Electronegativity** | Increases | Decreases | ↗ |
| **Metallic Character** | Decreases | Increases | ↙ |
| **Non-metallic Character** | Increases | Decreases | ↗ |

<Callout type="info" emoji="📌">
**The ultimate shortcut:** There are really only **two arrows** to remember:
- **↙ (bottom-left):** Atomic radius, metallic character
- **↗ (top-right):** Ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, non-metallic character

Atomic radius is the "odd one out" – it goes the **opposite** direction from everything else. This makes sense: **smaller atoms hold electrons tighter** (higher IE, EA, EN), while **larger atoms let them go easily** (more metallic).
</Callout>

## Metals, Non-metals & Metalloids

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Metals (الفلزات)">
**Location:** Left and centre of the periodic table (most elements are metals!)

**Physical properties:**
- Shiny (lustrous), silvery appearance
- **Malleable** (can be hammered into sheets) and **ductile** (can be drawn into wires)
- Good **conductors** of heat and electricity
- Generally high melting and boiling points
- Solid at room temperature (except mercury, Hg – liquid)

**Chemical properties:**
- Tend to **lose** electrons → form **cations** (positive ions)
- Low ionization energy, low electronegativity
- Metal oxides are **basic** (dissolve in water to form bases)
- React with acids to produce hydrogen gas and a salt
</Tab>
<Tab label="Non-metals (اللافلزات)">
**Location:** Upper right of the periodic table

**Physical properties:**
- Dull, varied colours (not shiny)
- **Brittle** in solid form (shatter, don't bend)
- Poor conductors (insulators) – except graphite (carbon)
- Generally low melting and boiling points
- Can be solid, liquid (bromine), or gas at room temperature

**Chemical properties:**
- Tend to **gain** electrons → form **anions** (negative ions)
- High ionization energy, high electronegativity
- Non-metal oxides are **acidic** (dissolve in water to form acids)
</Tab>
<Tab label="Metalloids (أشباه الفلزات)">
**Location:** Along the "staircase" zigzag line

**Properties – intermediate between metals and non-metals:**
- May be shiny or dull
- Somewhat brittle
- **Semiconductors** – conduct electricity under certain conditions but not others
- This makes them essential for **electronics** (silicon chips, germanium transistors)

**The metalloids:** B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te (and sometimes Po and At)
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## How to Approach Periodic Table Questions

<Steps>
<Step title="Step 1: Write the Electron Configuration">
Determine the complete or abbreviated electron configuration. This tells you exactly where the electrons are.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 2: Identify the Number of Electrons">
Confirm the total number of electrons = atomic number (Z) for a neutral atom, or adjust for ions.
</Step>
<Step title="Step 3: Identify the Valence Electrons">
Find the electrons in the **outermost energy level** (highest *n* value). These are the valence electrons – they determine the element's group, reactivity, and trends.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Example>
**Chlorine (Cl, Z = 17):**

1. **Configuration:** $1s^2 \, 2s^2 \, 2p^6 \, 3s^2 \, 3p^5$ or $[\text{Ne}] \, 3s^2 \, 3p^5$
2. **Total electrons:** 17 <svg style={{display:'inline-block'}} width="16" height="16" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="#10b981" strokeWidth="3" strokeLinecap="round" strokeLinejoin="round"><polyline points="20 6 9 17 4 12"></polyline></svg>
3. **Valence electrons:** 7 (in the 3s and 3p orbitals, *n* = 3)
   → Group 17 → high electronegativity → high electron affinity → halogen!
</Example>

## Vocabulary

<Vocabulary title="Key Terms – LO 4">
<Term word="Electron Configuration (التوزيع الإلكتروني)">The arrangement of electrons in an atom's orbitals, showing which energy levels and subshells are occupied.</Term>
<Term word="Aufbau Principle (مبدأ البناء التصاعدي)">Electrons fill orbitals starting from the lowest energy level and working upward.</Term>
<Term word="Pauli Exclusion Principle (مبدأ باولي)">No two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers; max 2 electrons per orbital with opposite spins.</Term>
<Term word="Hund's Rule (قاعدة هوند)">Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly (same spin) before pairing up.</Term>
<Term word="Ground State (الحالة المستقرة)">The lowest energy configuration of an atom's electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Excited State (الحالة المثارة)">A higher-energy state reached when an electron absorbs energy and jumps to a higher orbital.</Term>
<Term word="Valence Electrons (إلكترونات التكافؤ)">Electrons in the outermost energy level; they determine chemical behaviour and bonding.</Term>
<Term word="Core Electrons (الإلكترونات الداخلية)">Inner electrons that shield valence electrons from the full nuclear charge.</Term>
<Term word="Group (المجموعة)">A vertical column in the periodic table; elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Period (الدورة)">A horizontal row in the periodic table; elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.</Term>
<Term word="Atomic Radius (نصف القطر الذري)">Half the distance between nuclei of two bonded identical atoms; a measure of atom size.</Term>
<Term word="Ionic Radius (نصف القطر الأيوني)">The radius of an ion; cations are smaller and anions are larger than their parent atoms.</Term>
<Term word="Ionization Energy (طاقة التأين)">Energy required to remove the most loosely held electron from a gaseous atom.</Term>
<Term word="Electron Affinity (الألفة الإلكترونية)">Energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron; more negative = greater tendency to gain.</Term>
<Term word="Electronegativity (الكهروسالبية)">An atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond (Pauling scale).</Term>
<Term word="Effective Nuclear Charge (الشحنة النووية الفعالة)">The net positive charge experienced by valence electrons after accounting for shielding by inner electrons.</Term>
<Term word="Alkali Metals (الفلزات القلوية)">Group 1 elements (except H); 1 valence electron, extremely reactive metals.</Term>
<Term word="Alkaline Earth Metals (الفلزات القلوية الأرضية)">Group 2 elements; 2 valence electrons, reactive metals (less than Group 1).</Term>
<Term word="Halogens (الهالوجينات)">Group 17 elements; 7 valence electrons, very reactive non-metals, "salt formers."</Term>
<Term word="Noble Gases (الغازات النبيلة)">Group 18 elements; full valence shell, extremely unreactive (inert).</Term>
<Term word="Transition Metals (الفلزات الانتقالية)">Groups 3–12; d-block elements, can form multiple ion charges, often colourful compounds.</Term>
<Term word="Metalloids (أشباه الفلزات)">Elements along the zigzag line (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te); properties intermediate between metals and non-metals; semiconductors.</Term>
<Term word="Semiconductor (شبه موصل)">A material that conducts electricity under certain conditions; metalloids like silicon are key semiconductors.</Term>
<Term word="Isoelectronic (متساوي الإلكترونات)">Species (atoms or ions) that have the same number of electrons.</Term>
</Vocabulary>

## Key Takeaways

<KeyPoints title="What You Need to Remember">
<Point>**Electron configuration** follows three rules: Aufbau (lowest energy first), Pauli (max 2 per orbital, opposite spins), and Hund (spread out before pairing).</Point>
<Point>**Aufbau order:** 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → ... Note that **4s fills before 3d**.</Point>
<Point>**Exceptions:** Cr is $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^1 \, 3d^5$ (half-filled d) and Cu is $[\text{Ar}] \, 4s^1 \, 3d^{10}$ (fully-filled d) for extra stability.</Point>
<Point>**Ion configurations:** For cations, remove electrons from the **outermost shell first** (ns before (n-1)d). For anions, add electrons to the highest energy unfilled orbital.</Point>
<Point>**Groups** (vertical) share the same number of **valence electrons** and similar chemistry. **Periods** (horizontal) share the same number of **electron shells**.</Point>
<Point>**Atomic radius** increases ↙ (down and left). All other trends (IE, EA, EN) increase ↗ (up and right). Only **two arrows** to remember!</Point>
<Point>**Ionization energy** is inversely related to atomic radius: smaller atom → electrons held tighter → higher IE.</Point>
<Point>**Successive ionization energies** always increase. A **dramatic jump** indicates you've broken into the **core electrons**, revealing the number of valence electrons.</Point>
<Point>**Electron affinity** exception: **Cl > F** because fluorine's tiny size causes excessive electron-electron repulsion.</Point>
<Point>**Electronegativity** measures pull on **shared** electrons. Fluorine (4.0) is highest. Noble gases = 0.</Point>
<Point>**Cations** are smaller than parent atoms; **anions** are larger. For isoelectronic species, more protons = smaller radius.</Point>
<Point>**Metals** (left): lose electrons, form cations, basic oxides, good conductors. **Non-metals** (right): gain electrons, form anions, acidic oxides, insulators. **Metalloids** (zigzag): semiconductors, intermediate properties.</Point>
</KeyPoints>
